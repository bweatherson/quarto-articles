The four points that do double duty (new and tee up the comments)
These earn their place because they're both departures from earlier IRE and the load-bearing background for the comments. If the precis is tight, build it around these.
1. Theoretical interests, not just practical. Stanley's book is literally Knowledge and Practical Interests; you drop the restriction, so idle curiosity — even just wondering how probable p is — can cost you knowledge. Your draft already states this, but I'd give it more room, because it's the single point that sets up the most comment. It frames Friedman's central worry (her "complication 1": theoretical interests don't work in parallel to practical ones — in the bootstrapping cases mere inquiry seems to undermine knowledge, with no second practical variable), and it sets up McGrath's point 1 (if knowing p lets you answer "Pr(p) = 1," then theoretical inquiry into a probability destroys knowledge). Saying enough here — that practical and theoretical interests are meant to operate by one mechanism — is exactly the context their objections need, without replying to them.
2. Odds, not stakes; low-stakes knowledge loss. The break you flag at line 14: it's not high stakes but the L/G ratio, and you can drive knowledge away by shrinking the gain to microscopic levels (the $50 Red-Blue game). This directly frames McGrath's point 2, where he worries that to keep L low you must shrink G so far that the stakes become high after all. Worth stating the inequality Pr(p)/(1−Pr(p)) > L/G explicitly in the precis, since both Friedman and McGrath reason from it — Friedman calls it the thing that "pins down" your view, and it's the shared vocabulary of the symposium.
3. The positive mechanism: "taking for granted" / appropriate starting points for inquiry. This is the biggest gap in the current draft, and it's the conceptual core both reviewers engage. The novelty relative to Stanley/Hawthorne is that knowledge isn't just licensing action — it's what you can use as a premise, what you can "take for granted," cashed out as answering an unconditional question Q the same way you'd answer Q conditional on what you know. Friedman reconstructs this carefully and McGrath's points 1 and 3 both turn on it. The precis needs to state it plainly so the reader can follow what the commentators are pulling on.
4. You can inquire into what you know (the double-checking response). This is new even relative to your own earlier view — you used to think you were committed to Friedman's "don't inquire into what you know," and Chapter 5 reverses that via deliberately restricted inquiry, sensitivity-chasing, and the alethic-vs-other-appropriateness distinction. This is the context for the entire second half of both comments: Friedman's surgeon and Florian-coffee-scale worries ("complication 2"), and McGrath's checking-vs-inquiring proposal. Even a few sentences here is what makes their Chapter 5 material legible to a reader who hasn't seen the book.
Worth a mention if space allows (more new than comment-teeing, except one)
5. Belief and evidence are interest-relative too — and not all knowledge is evidence. The generalization beyond knowledge is a clean novelty over the 2000s views, and the denial of E=K (Williamson) is something McGrath explicitly endorses in his opening. Flagging it sets a cooperative tone and contextualizes his framing, even though neither reviewer attacks it.
6. Rational action isn't just EU-maximization; logical strength matters. The weak-dominance / "prefer the logically weaker bet" material is a real departure, and it's precisely the alternative diagnosis McGrath floats in his point 2 (that Red-Blue is driven by comparative logical strength, not error-risk). Mentioning that you don't simply identify rationality with EU-maximization gives his suggestion its context — and signals it's a live issue in the book rather than something he's catching you out on.
7. The counterintuitiveness reply: modal variation ≠ constitution (plus Das). This frames McGrath's point 4 (his causal-vs-constitutive distinction is pitched directly against the Das move). Lower priority for a precis — it's Chapter 7 and somewhat in the weeds — but if you want the precis to set up all four of McGrath's points, one sentence on "every theory makes knowledge modally vary with truth-irrelevant factors; the objection overgeneralizes" does it.
How I'd weight it
For a 1000–1500 word precis I'd spend the bulk on 1–4 (they're new, they unify the book, and they carry roughly 80% of what the commentators say), give a sentence or two each to 5 and 6, and treat 7 as optional. That ordering also happens to track the book's arc, so the precis can double as a map.
One editorial note on your stated goal of "context, not pre-emptive reply": the cleanest way to stay on the right side of that line is to present 3 and 4 as open commitments — state the taking-for-granted principle and the inquire-into-what-you-know move as what the view says, and let it be visible that they raise questions (how exactly theoretical interests work; how Chapter 5 "knows-but-can't-use" cases differ from the original "doesn't-know" cases). You don't have to name the questions; just don't paper over them. That's what gives Friedman and McGrath somewhere to stand.

## 1. Theoretical interests, not just practical

*New vs.* Stanley's *Knowledge and Practical Interests*. *Sets up* Friedman's "complication 1" and McGrath's point 1 (probability questions).

From `overture.qmd`:

> Humans engage in both practical and theoretical inquiries. … A lot of the focus in the literature on interest-relativity has been on how knowledge interacts with practical inquiry. Indeed the title of Stanley's defence of an interest-relative account is *Knowledge and Practical Interests*. I don't impose any such restriction here. If *p* can't be properly taken to be settled in a purely theoretical inquiry that someone is engaged in, they don't know that *p*. This has one striking implication. Let's say the person is trying to figure out as precisely as possible what the probability of *p* is. If they can take *p* as settled in that inquiry, then the answer to the inquiry will be 1. Unless the correct answer to this inquiry is actually 1, it won't be proper to take *p* as settled. So in general one easy way to lose knowledge that *p* is to launch an inquiry into precisely how probable *p* is. … The Cartesian meditator does, on this view, lose knowledge in anything when they seriously reflect on how good their evidence is for it. Happily, this knowledge comes back when they return to their normal life.

This is the single passage that does the most teeing-up: the probability implication is exactly McGrath's point 1, stated by you first.

## 2. Odds, not stakes (and low-stakes knowledge loss)

*New vs.* the 2000s assumption that IRT = orthodoxy + high-stakes exceptions. *Sets up* McGrath's point 2.

From `overture.qmd`:

> To know something is to properly take it to be settled. There are two kinds of practical considerations that might make it improper to take something to be settled even if the evidence in favor of settling is strong enough for everyday purposes. The first is that the cost of being wrong is very high. The second is that the cost of checking whether one is wrong is very low. The previous literature on interest-relativity has primarily focussed on the first kind of reason. … There is somewhat less discussion, however, about how knowledge is also lost in 'easy checking' cases, or about how (as Mark Schroeder notes), knowledge might not be lost in cases where the stakes are high but checking is impossible. As I'll put it … what matters is not the stakes, but the odds one faces in a particular situation.

From `interests.qmd` (§ Odds and Stakes), the inequality both reviewers reason from:

> The version of IRT defended in this book does not care about whether a subject faces a high stakes bet. Instead, it says that *L* matters, but only indirectly. What is (typically) true in these cases is that the subject should maximise expected utility relative to their evidence. And taking the risky choice maximises expected utility only if this equation is true.

> Pr(*p*)/(1−Pr(*p*)) > L/G

> The left hand side expresses the odds that *p* is true. The right hand side expresses how high those odds have to be before the risk is worth taking. If the equation fails to hold, then the risk is not worth taking. If the risk is not worth taking, then the subject doesn't know that *p*.

Note the "(typically)" hedge on EU-maximization — that's the seam McGrath pulls on in point 2 (logical strength) and you'll want it visible.

## 3. The positive mechanism: taking for granted / appropriate starting point

*New:* the constitutive "what you can use as a premise" framing, more refined than "knowledge licenses action." *Sets up* McGrath's point 1 and 3, and Friedman's reconstruction of the whole framework. **This is the biggest gap in your current draft.**

From `knowledge.qmd` (§ Theoretical Knowledge), building on the Hieronymi quote that a reason is "an item in (actual or possible) reasoning … thought directed at some question":

> So to a first approximation the inquirer knows that *p* only if they can properly use *p* as a reason in "thought directed at the question" they are considering. That is, they can use *p* as a step in this reasoning. This way of putting things connects Hieronymi's view of reasons to the idea present in both Hawthorne and Stanley and Fantl and McGrath that things known are reasons.

The immunity principle (which Friedman quotes back at you):

> If the inquirer uses as a step in reasoning something that she knows to be true, then she is immune to a certain kind of criticism. She is immune to the criticism that the premise she used might not be true.

> So the key principle I'll be working with is that One cannot be criticised for using what one knows in an inquiry on the grounds that one is using what might be false. … sometimes I'll simply say that one can rationally take for granted what one knows.

The two cases in the very next paragraphs are *the cases the reviewers use* — worth knowing they're already in your text:

> Now imagine not that she's playing the Red-Blue game, but thinking about how to play it. … It would be a mistake for her to reason as follows: Well, the Battle of Agincourt was in 1415, so playing Blue-True will get me \$50 … The mistake is the first step; she just can't take this for granted in this very context. [= Friedman's hypothetical Red-Blue case]

> Imagine that the day after reading the book … Anisa starts wondering how likely it is that the book was correct. … it would be an error to reason as follows: Well, the Battle of Agincourt was in 1415, and that's what the book says, so the book is certainly correct. … the problem is the first step. [= Friedman's bootstrapping case]

> Rather, individual inquiries have their own logic, their own rules about what can and can't be taken for granted. [Friedman quotes this line directly]

## 4. You can inquire into what you know (the double-checking response)

*New even vs. your own earlier view.* *Sets up* Friedman's "complication 2" (surgeon, Florian) and McGrath's point 3 (checking vs. inquiring).

From `inquiry.qmd`, the framing:

> I used to think that in these cases the defender of IRT would have to either say that they are not really cases of knowledge, or not really cases of appropriate inquiry. … I now think the objection should be addressed head on. It is possible to properly conduct an inquiry into *p*, even when one knows that *p*, and even when knowledge provides appropriate starting points for inquiry. That's because it is often appropriate to deliberately restrict oneself in inquiry, and use fewer resources than are otherwise available.

The alethic-immunity distinction (Friedman leans on this exact wording):

> The core principle in my version of IRT is that someone who uses what they know in inquiry is immune to criticism on the grounds that what they are doing is epistemically risky. Equivalently, they are immune to criticism on the grounds that their premises might be false. That's compatible with saying that someone can know *p*, and be properly criticised for using *p* in inquiry. … as long as we pay attention not just to whether someone is being criticised, but why they are being criticised.

Sensitivity-chasing / Florian (Friedman's coffee-scale case is yours):

> Florian has just weighed out the coffee beans … He starts wondering whether his scales have recently stopped being reliable. … he has to use some other scale to check this one. … It's fine to use the backup scale to check the main scale, and not fine to use the scale to check itself. … Checking is, at least in part, a matter of *sensitivity chasing*.

The surgeon (Brown), your verdict:

> the surgeon here is doing a bit of mostly harmless sensitivity chasing. Before checking the notes, their belief that the left kidney was diseased was not sensitive to the possibility that they'd misremembered the morning meeting; after checking the notes it is. … All that can be true even if the surgeon knows which kidney is diseased.

And the line that pre-empts the "why not collapse the distinction" worry (good to gesture at in the precis without answering it):

> anyone who wants to separate out knowledge from action … owes a story about why we don't also triple-check, quadruple-check, and so on.

## 5. Belief and evidence are interest-relative too; not all knowledge is evidence

*New* generalization beyond knowledge; *sets up* McGrath's opening endorsement (he agrees not all knowledge is evidence) and frames the cooperative parts of both comments.

From `overture.qmd`:

> the arguments that knowledge is interest-relative generalise into arguments that evidence itself can be interest-relative.

From `evidence.qmd`:

> it is incompatible with E=K, since what it takes to be evidence on this story is slightly different from what it takes to be knowledge.

> There is a difference between knowing *A* and being able to use *A* to support further inductive inferences. It is very natural to call that the difference between knowing *A* and having *A* as evidence.

## 6. Rational action isn't just EU-maximization

*New vs.* the standard decision-theoretic background. *Sets up* McGrath's point 2 (logical strength) and the close-calls strand.

From `inquiry.qmd` (objection from close calls):

> some simple versions of IRT say implausible things about cases where a person is choosing between very similar options. What I'm going to argue is that the problem … is not due to IRT, which is correct, but to the background assumption that choosers should maximise expected utility. … in the cases they describe, choosers should not maximise expected utility. … expected utility theory has fairly implausible things to say about *close call* cases.

(The logical-strength version of the point — that one should prefer betting on a logically weaker proposition even at probability 1 — is developed in `interests.qmd`/`rationality.qmd`; flag it here if you want McGrath's alternative diagnosis to have context.)

## 7. The counterintuitiveness reply: counterfactual dependence ≠ building (+ Das)

*Optional for the precis (Ch. 7 material).* *Sets up* McGrath's point 4 (his causal-vs-constitutive distinction is aimed squarely here).

From `changes.qmd`:

> What one knows is always (in principle) sensitive to what one's interests are. But in cases where one knows, one's knowledge is not explained by what one's interests are.

> Strictly speaking, I don't think a change in interests does make one have knowledge. … it doesn't follow that facts about interests stand in a making, or building, relationship to facts about knowledge. … Those factors, and only those factors, make for knowledge.

The Das overgeneralization move, with the environment example McGrath quotes:

> every plausible theory of knowledge is committed to some kinds of interest-relativity, and so the intuitions that my version of IRT violates are violated by every plausible theory of knowledge.

> my environment, in the relevant sense, consists of a network of college towns and universities throughout the globe, and excludes any number of places a short drive away. But should I become more interested in nearby suburbs than far away colleges, my environment would change. That is to say, environment is an interest-relative notion.

McGrath grants the Das point but replies that *this* dependence is causal while the taking-for-granted dependence is constitutive — so if you cite Das in the precis, you're framing exactly the move he targets.
