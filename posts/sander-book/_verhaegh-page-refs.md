## Also useful for your UK paragraph

- **p. 267 n. 92:** the UK and US analytic turns differed. Citing Rowe (2023), Verhaegh says the Oxford 'analytic' generation (Ryle, Austin, Ayer) emerged in the early 1930s, "Naturally, this analysis does not apply to Cambridge, one of the birth-grounds of analytic philosophy". This supports your point about Oxford and Cambridge being different, but it says the Oxford group were already called analytic.
- **p. 262:** in the 1930s 'analytic' mostly meant the Cambridge school (Moore and his students); Nagel's 1936 "Impressions and Appraisals of Analytic Philosophy in Europe" made it broad-tent.
- **p. 323:** "many British analytic philosophers maintained a strict distinction between science and philosophy", unlike the positivists.
- **p. 172:** Nagel, in Cambridge, attended Moore, Broad and John Wisdom, and was disappointed: "just analysis after analysis". **p. 321:** *Analysis* as the British journal that *Philosophical Studies* was meant to counterpart.
