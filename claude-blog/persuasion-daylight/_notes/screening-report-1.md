# Screening Report: "Persuasion in Daylight"

- **Title:** Persuasion in Daylight
- **Word count:** ~9,200 (excluding references); 71 bibliography items
- **Thesis:** The "true results suffice" propaganda models (Weatherall–O'Connor–Bruner et al.) work only because their audiences are cursed. Once the audience reasons about the process, *selection* of existing results is neutralised by unravelling, but *design* of experiments (Kamenica–Gentzkow persuasion) still moves a fully rational, fully informed audience while leaving her beliefs calibrated. The paper argues this is neither deception nor misleading nor manipulation on standard accounts but is exploitation of epistemic dependence (Wertheimer/Valdman/Vrousalis); hence transparency reforms relocate the problem rather than solve it, and the receiver's remedy is advance commitment to a demanding standard of proof.
- **Decision:** DESK REJECT

(Closest-call flag: this is the nearest thing to a pass in its class. If the decision is overturned, it is EVALUATE, not SCREEN: the formal core is decision-theoretic and the subject is philosophy of economics/social science, squarely the editor-in-chief's areas.)

## Deal-Breakers

**Criterion 1 / "could the author have seen it": the paper's own concessions undercut its headline claims, in three places.**

1. The abstract and conclusion say design is "not deception, not misleading, and not manipulation on any standard account." §6.1 then concedes that Skyrms's deception condition and Fallis & Lewis's refinement are both "met," and §6.2 concedes that Gorin's reasons-tracking account may classify design as manipulation ("Suppose we grant that this makes design manipulation in Gorin's sense"). The fallback, that these accounts "classify without diagnosing," is a different and weaker thesis than the one advertised, and for Gorin and Klenk it rests on a misreading (see Checks, c1).
2. §7 grounds the exploitation claim in Valdman's condition that the receiver "cannot reasonably refuse" and in Hardwig-style dependence. §8.2 then shows the receiver can recover almost the entire value of information by committing to a threshold, and that the sponsor still participates for every t < 1. If the receiver has a cheap, unilateral counter-move that captures the surplus, the vulnerability is her decision rule, not her dependence, and Valdman's condition fails. The paper does not reconcile the two sections; it presents each as if the other did not exist.
3. The paper says "the economics is not new" and that the contribution is "the philosophical use." But the two "institutional consequences" are precisely what the cited economics already says. Henry & Ottaviani (2019), cited twice, is a model of a regulator's approval standard against a sponsor who designs experiments, and its rationale for a demanding standard is the same commitment rationale the paper presents as its own. Felgenhauer (2021), cited, already models pre-registration as a commitment device in a persuasion game. Once these are subtracted, the paper's novelty reduces to the classification "design is exploitation," argued in roughly 1,200 words with the baseline problem settled by stipulation ("the fair way to choose the experiment is by its informativeness about her question").

**Criterion 4: a major objection visible from the paper's own example is not addressed.** The design result is said to survive full rationality because "at the moment the sponsor chooses which experiment to run, he knows nothing the regulator does not." In the drug case this is false: sponsors have phase I/II and preclinical data at design time. With an informed sender, the choice of experiment is itself a signal (Perez-Richet 2014, "Interim Bayesian persuasion"; Hedlund 2017; Koessler & Skreta), the receiver can again be cursed about it, and the clean partition into "selection leaks private information, design does not" collapses. None of this literature is cited.

## Key Strengths

- The selection/design distinction, and the observation that sophistication cures one and not the other for a structural reason (private information exists at the point of selection and not at the point of design), is crisp and correctly derived. The table in §5 and the derivations in the appendix are right; I recomputed every figure.
- The point that transparency reforms are the *commitment device* Bayesian persuasion presupposes, so that pre-registration hands the designer the setting in which his channel works, is a genuinely good observation and stated well ("Daylight is the designer's friend").
- The framing question, whether a fully rational agent can be wronged by true evidence and what the wrong is, has generalist appeal across ethics, epistemology and philosophy of science.
- The engagement with the economics of disclosure and persuasion (Grossman, Milgrom, Dye, Hart–Kremer–Perry, Felgenhauer–Loerke, Fréchette et al.) is accurate and well chosen.

## Key Weaknesses

- **The taxonomic thesis is weaker than advertised** (Deal-Breaker 1). The paper needs the disjunction "either the account excludes design or it fails to explain the wrong," and the second disjunct is asserted, not argued, for the accounts that matter (Gorin, Klenk, Skyrms).
- **Exploitation and the counter-move pull apart** (Deal-Breaker 2). Related: the paper's baseline for "fair terms" is the decision-value-maximising experiment (Henry–Ottaviani), which is itself keyed to the receiver's decision rule. So the §7 contrast between choosing "by reference to her decision rule rather than by reference to her question" does not mark the difference the paper needs; the difference is whose payoff the experiment maximises, and that needs saying.
- **Overgeneration is not confronted.** On the paper's account, any interested party who chooses which evidence to generate, absent a counter-measure, exploits the receiver: a job candidate choosing which references to solicit, a scientist choosing the experiment most likely to yield a publishable result, a firm choosing which product demonstration to run. The paper's one exemption (adversarial trial) is by counter-measure; it does not explain why the ubiquitous everyday cases are not exploitation, or accept that they are.
- **The motivating case undercuts the "relocation" prediction.** Regulators are not passive receivers of design: the FDA reviews protocols, fixes endpoints and comparators (Special Protocol Assessments), and requires two adequate trials. The counter-measure the paper proposes is already institutionalised in exactly the domain it draws its examples from, and the Lundh (2017) finding it offers as confirmation predates full registration and has a standard rival explanation (sponsors select which drugs to test, i.e. informed-sender selection at the pipeline level).
- **Minor formal looseness.** "Exactly nothing, in expectation" depends on the receiver breaking indifference in the sender's favour at posterior 0.5; "selection's power is exactly proportional to the audience's ignorance" is a slogan, not a result; the claim in §4 that with the count known "splitting the budget into small studies simply gives the receiver a less informative body of evidence" is false in the Weatherall–O'Connor–Bruner setup, where total draws are fixed and full disclosure of many small studies carries the same information as one large one.

## Critical Issues for Revision

1. Drop "on any standard account." Either argue that the Skyrms/Fallis–Lewis deception verdict and the Gorin/Klenk manipulation verdict are wrong, engaging what those authors say the wrong *is*, or accept that design is deceptive/manipulative on those accounts and argue that exploitation is the more illuminating category. The current "classified but not diagnosed" move does not work for accounts whose diagnosis concerns the influencer's attitude rather than the target's belief.
2. Reconcile §7 with §8.2. Either the receiver cannot reasonably refuse (then commitment must be unavailable, and say why) or she can (then the wrong is something other than Valdman-style exploitation of dependence).
3. Handle the informed sender. Either restrict the design result to senders with no private information and defend the restriction, or engage the interim-persuasion literature and show what survives.
4. State the contribution net of Henry & Ottaviani (2019), Felgenhauer (2021) and Glazer & Rubinstein (2004). If the institutional half is a philosophical gloss on those results, say so and shorten it; the paper's own claim to novelty is the exploitation diagnosis, and that section should carry the weight.
5. Address overgeneration and the fact that regulators co-design trials.

## Checks a referee would run

### (a) Claims that are false or unsupported

- **"At the moment the sponsor chooses which experiment to run, he knows nothing the regulator does not" (§5).** False in the motivating case and unsupported as an idealisation; the whole "no private information to leak" argument for why sophistication does not help against design depends on it.
- **"Splitting the budget into small studies simply gives the receiver a less informative body of evidence" (§4).** With the count known and all results disclosed, the total evidence in the WOB setting is the same fixed number of Bernoulli draws however partitioned. The sponsor has no reason to prefer small studies, which is the point the paper needs, but the stated reason is wrong.
- **"Selection's power is exactly proportional to the audience's ignorance" (abstract, §4).** No proportionality result is given or cited; Arieli & Stewart (as glossed) says selection can match design under *sufficient* uncertainty, which is a threshold claim, not a proportionality claim.
- **"The sponsor has chosen an experiment that delivers to the regulator exactly nothing, in expectation" (§5).** True only under the KG convention that the receiver breaks indifference in the sender's favour. Under the opposite tie-break the sponsor sets q just below 3/7 and the receiver strictly gains. The paper's own footnote-free presentation hides that the "exactly nothing" is a knife-edge.
- **"The usual story ... does not explain why the standards need to be fixed in advance and applied as rules" (§8.2).** Unsupported. Rule-based standards of proof have standard rationales independent of error costs (predictability, equal treatment, guarding against decision-maker bias, reviewability). The claim that the commitment rationale is *the* explanation is overreach; at most it is one more.
- **The "prediction" in §8.1** (sponsorship advantage persists under transparency, unattributable to conduct, concentrated in design) is offered as novel, but Sismondo (2008), Bero, and Holman's own work already make it, and Lundh (2017) cannot confirm a prediction about a "fully transparent regime" since its trials span 1980s–2010s.
- **"Her not being asked is part of what makes the arrangement exploitative" (§7).** In the actual regulatory setting she *is* asked: protocol review precedes trials. The claim is fine for the toy model but the paper repeatedly invokes the real case.

### (b) Citations whose use looks wrong

- **Gorin (2014b).** Gorin's account locates manipulation in the influencer's insensitivity to whether the reasons he supplies are good ones (he would use bad reasons equally readily). The wrong, on Gorin's view, is in that attitude toward the target, not in the target's ending up with an unjustified belief. The paper's rebuttal ("the regulator's response *is* appropriately connected to what justifies it ... It has classified the case without diagnosing it") attacks a diagnosis Gorin does not give.
- **Klenk (2022).** Klenk's condition is carelessness on the influencer's part about whether the *method* reveals reasons. The paper reads it as a condition on whether the method in fact reveals reasons ("that method reveals to the receiver every reason the experiment generates"). The sponsor chose q = 3/7 for efficacy and would have chosen it whether or not it revealed reasons; that is Klenk's carelessness. The paper's escape trades on the misreading.
- **Skyrms (2010) and Fallis & Lewis (2019).** The paper concedes both conditions are met and then continues to say design is "not deception ... on any standard account." Either the citation shows the abstract is wrong, or the paper must argue these are not standard accounts of deception. It does neither.
- **Henry & Ottaviani (2019).** Cited in §7 (for the costly-experimentation baseline) and §8.2 (for the limits), but not acknowledged as the source of the paper's headline institutional result: a regulator's demanding approval standard as the receiver's optimal commitment against a designing sponsor. The paper presents that result as its own "second consequence."
- **Wood (2014).** The paper says Wood "in the same chapter that rules design out of manipulation on the bypassing account, characterises exploitation." A referee should check that Wood's Coons & Weber chapter ("Coercion, Manipulation, Exploitation") in fact commits to a bypassing account rather than surveying it; Wood's own view of manipulation is broader than bypassing.
- **Kamenica & Gentzkow (2011), Proposition on concavity.** "Whenever the sender's payoff ... is not concave at the prior" is a loose rendering of the concave-closure condition (sender benefits iff the concave closure of v exceeds v at the prior). Acceptable in a survey sentence but imprecise for a paper resting on the result.
- **Kamenica & Gentzkow (2017).** "Competition in design helps only when each sender can generate the signals the others can" is a rough gloss of Blackwell-connectedness; a referee in economics would want it stated correctly or dropped.
- **Unverifiable as cited:** Dai, Fudenberg & Pei (2025); Arieli & Stewart (2025); O'Connor & Freeborn (2025); Kates (2023); Noggle (2025); Pinto & Pinto (2023). None of these was known to me in the form cited; they may be forthcoming or working papers, but the bibliography should give locations.
- **Missing citations a referee would expect:** Glazer & Rubinstein (2004, *Econometrica*, "On optimal rules of persuasion"), the canonical receiver-commits-to-a-rule paper; Perez-Richet (2014), Hedlund (2017) and Koessler & Skreta on persuasion with an informed sender; Sismondo (2008) on design-level sponsorship bias; Cohen (2018, *AJP*, "Manipulation and deception") and Noggle (2020, *APQ*) on manipulation through true information; Berenstain (2016, *Ergo*) on "epistemic exploitation," since the paper's term collides with an established one with a different meaning.

### (c) Objections a well-read reader would raise that the paper does not address

1. **Informed sender** (formal epistemology / economics). Once the sponsor has private information at design time, the experiment choice is a signal and the cursed/uncursed distinction reappears on the design side. This is not an exotic worry; it is the actual situation in every case the paper cites.
2. **Time-inconsistency of the receiver's commitment and its welfare cost** (decision theory). The paper notes the act-by-act Bayesian approves at 0.6, but does not confront that a receiver committed to t = 0.95 who *actually faces* a posterior of 0.6 (because the sponsor deviated, or because evidence arrives from elsewhere) makes a decision she knows to be wrong. The paper's own remedy requires the receiver to act against her evidence off-path, which sits uneasily with a paper whose complaint against design is that it wrongs a perfectly evidence-responsive agent.
3. **Degeneracy of the threshold result.** In the model the receiver-optimal commitment is t → 1 and the sponsor still runs the fully informative experiment. That is an artefact of costless experimentation, unbounded experiment precision, and a sender with no outside option. The "limits" paragraph names the first; a referee will note that with *any* bounded experiment set the sponsor can be driven to run nothing at thresholds well below 1, and that the paper's rhetoric ("the whole game," "the only way to be open") is not licensed by a result this fragile.
4. **Overgeneration of "exploitation"** (ethics). Job references, product demonstrations, grant applications, a student choosing which exam to sit: all are self-interested design of evidence for a dependent receiver. Either these are exploitation (then the concept has been stretched past usefulness) or the paper needs a further condition it has not supplied.
5. **Consent to the design is real in the target case** (philosophy of science / applied ethics). Regulators approve protocols. The exploitation argument's key move, "she consents to rely, not to the design," is false of the FDA, and the paper's institutional recommendation (fix a standard in advance; take design authority from the sponsor) describes what regulators already do. The paper should say what its analysis adds to existing practice.
6. **Why is calibrated belief the right welfare measure to exonerate the sender?** (epistemology). The paper argues no belief is one she "ought not to have." But the Good/Ramsey value-of-information framework the paper invokes says an agent is wronged epistemically when she is denied *expected* accuracy she could have had; on that measure the receiver is wronged in exactly the way the misleading-with-truths literature analyses, and "no individual belief is defective" is beside the point. The paper flirts with this in §6.1 ("locates the wrong at the level of the system") without seeing that it reopens the misleading verdict.
7. **Gorin/Klenk misreadings** (ethics of manipulation), as in (b): the accounts that locate manipulation in the influencer's attitude toward reasons straightforwardly apply and straightforwardly diagnose the wrong.
8. **Community-level design is not the same phenomenon** (philosophy of science). §8.1 assimilates Holman–Bruner's industrial selection and O'Connor–Freeborn's distraction to "design." But industrial selection works through the *community's* aggregation of results, where individual scientists are not cursed and the effect arises from funding-driven population dynamics. Whether a sophisticated community-level receiver is "still moved" is not shown; the paper asserts it ("a receiver who knows this can still be moved") on the strength of the two-agent model.
