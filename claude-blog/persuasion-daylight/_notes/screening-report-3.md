# Screening Report

**Title:** Persuasion in Daylight
**Word count:** c. 10,100 words main text + c. 700-word appendix (excluding references). Bibliography: 80 items.

**Thesis summary.** The philosophy-of-science models of persuasion-by-true-results (Weatherall–O'Connor–Bruner, Holman–Bruner, etc.) assume audiences that are "cursed" in Eyster–Rabin's sense. Once the audience reasons about the process, the strategy splits: *selection* of existing results is neutralised by unravelling, but *design* of which experiments exist is not, and (in the binary case) the designer's optimal experiment leaves the receiver with exactly her no-evidence expected utility whatever her payoffs. The paper argues the wrong done by design is neither deception nor manipulation but Wertheimer-style mutually advantageous exploitation, with a Gricean cooperative norm supplying the fairness baseline, and draws two institutional morals: transparency reforms relocate the problem to design, and the remedies (design authority; commitment to a standard above the receiver's own threshold) are available only to receivers with institutional power.

**Decision: DESK REJECT**

(Closest-call note: this is the nearest thing to an EVALUATE in the batch. The problem is genuine and generalist, and the contribution is nameable in one sentence. It is rejected because the decisive faults below are ones the author could have seen and in two cases half-saw and left standing. If the editor disagrees, the right upgrade is EVALUATE, not SCREEN: the core is a decision-theoretic/mechanism-design argument.)

## Deal-Breakers

**Criterion 4 (unanswered objection the author could have seen), Criterion 1 (thinness of the claimed novel contributions), and Criterion 5 (length relative to achievement).** Details under Key Weaknesses; the short version is that of the four advertised contributions, the second is a known two-line corollary, the third rests on a one-paragraph Gricean move that faces an obvious objection the paper does not raise, and the first and fourth are undercut by the paper's own concessions.

## Key Strengths

- **The diagnosis is real and crisply stated.** The observation that the WOB/Mohseni/Holman–Bruner audiences conditionalise "in the naive space" (Grünwald–Halpern) and are cursed (Eyster–Rabin), and that removing the curse separates disclosure (Grossman–Milgrom unravelling) from information design (Kamenica–Gentzkow), is correct and, as far as I know, not made explicitly in the philosophy-of-science literature. §§3–5 and the two-by-two table make it vivid.
- **The institutional inversion is a genuinely nice point.** "Design requires daylight" (§5, last paragraph): pre-registration, which kills selection, is exactly what makes a design commitment credible, so the reforms feed the surviving channel. This is the paper's most memorable claim and it is well supported by the cited economics (Felgenhauer–Loerke; Fréchette et al.).
- **The ethics section works through the alternatives honestly.** The paper concedes that Skyrms-style deception and Gorin/Klenk-style attitude accounts of manipulation do classify design, then argues (two-regulators case, §6.2) that they miss what varies. That argument is the best philosophical move in the paper.
- **The appendix is correct.** Proposition 1 and the commitment derivation check out (see below); all numerical values in the text and table are right.

## Key Weaknesses

- **The "sharpening" (contribution 2) is folklore, and the paper's novelty claim for it is unsupported.** §5: "it is not, so far as I can find, stated in this form." The result is that the sender-optimal binary signal leaves the receiver indifferent at the persuaded action and at her default otherwise, hence at her no-information value. This is the standard "receiver captures no surplus in the binary-action case" observation; it is implicit in Kamenica–Gentzkow's own prosecutor discussion and explicit in the receiver-welfare literature (Kolotilin et al. 2017; Bergemann–Morris 2019, which the paper cites). The author calls it a "two-line proof." A two-line corollary of a cited theorem is not a contribution a generalist journal should be listing as one of four.
- **The Gricean baseline, which is what the paper claims as its advance over Wertheimer, is asserted in one paragraph and faces an objection the paper never raises.** §7.1: a contribution is cooperative "when it is as informative as the purposes of the exchange require," where the purpose is stipulated "from the point of view of everyone but the sponsor." But Grice's Cooperative Principle is indexed to the *accepted* purpose of the exchange, and regulatory submission, like courtroom advocacy, is a paradigm adversarial setting in which the parties have not accepted a common informational purpose. That is the textbook case where Gricean norms do not apply straightforwardly. The paper needs the baseline to be "not stipulated" (§1, §7.1); without Grice it collapses back into Wertheimer's difficulty. The follow-up move, "she has contributed her reliance," answers a different question (why she has a claim on a share) and does not fix the standard.
- **The paper's own concessions undercut both halves of the headline split.** (i) Selection: §4 concedes, via Dye, Jung–Kwon, Henry, and Arieli–Stewart, that "if the receiver is sufficiently uncertain about the sender's capacity to experiment, selective disclosure can achieve everything that design achieves." The tobacco and beverage cases that motivate the paper are cases of exactly that uncertainty. So "selection is neutralised" holds only under an idealisation the paper's own examples fail, yet the abstract and conclusion state it flat. (ii) Design: the "exactly nothing" result requires an unbounded experiment set with p = 1 available; §8.2's own "limits" paragraph notes that bounding the set changes the commitment analysis but does not note that it also restores receiver surplus in Proposition 1. The rhetoric of "none of it," "all of it," "exactly" runs through the ethics section on the strength of an artefact.
- **The institutional thesis contradicts the paper's own examples.** Conclusion: "The transparency reforms that defeat selection do neither" (neither remove the choice nor arm the audience). §8.2, two pages earlier: "Registered reports do this" (design authority), and FDA protocol review "agrees endpoints and analysis plans with sponsors in advance." The reforms the paper says relocate the problem are, by its own account, bundled with the remedy. The defensible claim is about registration-plus-disclosure alone, which is much weaker than what the abstract promises.
- **Literature gaps in the two areas the paper is most about.** On design bias in industry trials, Stegenga (*Medical Nihilism*, 2018) and Wilholt ("Bias and values in scientific research," 2009) are absent; Wilholt's argument that conventional methodological standards function as coordination devices against preference bias is very close to §8.2's "commitment to a standard" and is not acknowledged. On exploitation, Wertheimer's own *Rethinking the Ethics of Clinical Research* (2011) and the Hawkins–Emanuel volume are absent. On standards of proof, §8.2 says "the present analysis adds one" reason for fixing standards in advance, but Kaplow (2012), which the paper cites, already argues that burdens of proof should be set by their effect on parties' behaviour rather than by error costs alone.
- **Length.** Roughly 2,500 words of §§3–5 are exposition of results the paper says are "not new" and will "present briskly," and §6 walks through six accounts of deception/manipulation seriatim. For what is finally established, this is a 7,000-word paper.

## Checks a referee would run

**(a) Claims that look false or unsupported**

1. §5: Proposition 1 is "not, so far as I can find, stated in this form." I believe it is standard (see above). At minimum the claim needs a literature check against Kolotilin et al. (2017), Alonso–Câmara (2016), and the Bergemann–Morris survey.
2. Abstract and §1: "whatever her payoffs, the designer's best experiment leaves her with exactly the expected utility she would have had with no evidence." True only for (i) binary action, (ii) state-independent sender payoff, (iii) an unrestricted experiment set, (iv) approval at indifference. The appendix states (i)–(iv); the abstract states none.
3. §2, on Lundh et al. (2017): the inference "favourable conclusions without worse risk-of-bias scores, which is what one would expect if the residual mechanism were design" is too quick. Lundh et al. themselves list selective reporting and publication bias, which the risk-of-bias tools also miss, among the candidate explanations. The evidence does not discriminate design from selection.
4. §7: "The sender appropriates the value of information, all of it." There is no common currency. The receiver loses 0.3 in accuracy relative to full information; the sponsor gains 0.3 in approval probability, all of it from approvals of non-working drugs. Nothing is transferred; a harm is inflicted that offsets a benefit. The "division of surplus" language borrowed from the wage-bargain case needs a surplus that both parties measure in the same units, and the paper never supplies one.
5. §8.2: "When the set of available experiments is bounded ... the commitment then binds." Correct, but the paper should also say that boundedness breaks Proposition 1 (the receiver retains surplus), since that bears on the ethics.
6. §5 footnote on WOB's "biased production": the gloss is right, but the claim that partitioning a fixed number of draws makes "no difference to her total evidence" assumes the studies are exchangeable Bernoulli draws; in WOB the studies have a fixed per-study sample and the propagandist's choice of study size does affect the distribution of results-as-reported, so a referee should confirm the footnote's equivalence claim against WOB's actual model.
7. §8.2: "the outcome when the receiver commits to a decision rule in advance coincides with the equilibrium outcome when she does not" (Hart–Kremer–Perry 2017). Correct only under their truth-leaning refinement and their evidence-structure assumptions; the sentence gestures at "conditions they specify" but the argument then treats the result as unconditional.
8. §5: "@DaiFudenbergPei2025" (secret follow-up experiments never help the sender) and "@ArieliStewart2025" (selective disclosure under capacity uncertainty achieves everything design does). I cannot verify either; a referee should check they exist and say what is attributed to them.

**(b) Citations whose use looks wrong**

1. Grice (1989): the Cooperative Principle is indexed to the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange; applying it to an adversarial regulatory submission, and to *experiment choice* rather than assertion, requires argument the paper does not give.
2. Vrousalis (2013): exploitation-as-domination requires the exploiter to have power over the exploited within a systematic relationship. The paper concedes the regulator has legal power over the sponsor and answers by redefining the relevant dimension as "power over the evidence." That is a redefinition, not a fit.
3. Lundh et al. (2017): see (a)3.
4. Kaplow (2012): cited for a "related conclusion" reached "earlier," then §8.2 presents the same idea (standards set by their effect on evidence producers, not by error costs) as the present analysis's addition.
5. Eyster–Rabin (2005): "cursed" in their sense is a parametrised partial neglect (χ ∈ [0,1]). The paper's receivers are fully cursed (χ = 1). Harmless, but the term is being used for the extreme case only.
6. Wood (2014) is cited both as ruling design out of manipulation and as characterising exploitation; the first attribution depends on reading Wood's "non-rational means" condition as the whole of his account, which a Wood scholar may contest.

**(c) Objections a well-read reader would raise that the paper does not address**

1. *The adversarial-context objection to the Gricean baseline* (philosophy of language / ethics). See Key Weaknesses. This is the one that matters most.
2. *Graded actions* (formal epistemology / decision theory). Regulators have more than two options: conditional approval, restricted labelling, post-marketing requirements. With three or more actions, or a continuous action, the receiver retains surplus under the sender-optimal signal, and the "exactly nothing" result fails. The paper's cases (regulators, juries, editors) all have graded options in practice.
3. *Independent production as a third counter-move* (philosophy of science). The paper says the receiver "has no independent capacity to produce [evidence] and no other party will." Publicly funded comparative-effectiveness trials, independent replication, and adversarial collaboration are standard remedies for design bias, and they are neither "authority over the design" nor "commitment to a standard." Their omission makes the two-remedies claim look like an artefact of the model.
4. *Symmetry of the exploitation account* (ethics). If exploitation is "power over the terms," a regulator who commits to t = 0.95 and thereby forces the sponsor to pay for a nearly fully informative experiment now holds the power over the terms. Does she exploit him? The account as stated seems to say yes when the standard is set higher than the sponsor's costs justify; the paper does not confront this.
5. *Who is the exploited party?* (ethics). Exploitation is a wrong to a party to a transaction. The paper's answer, that the regulator is exploited "as agent" and the public "through" her, treats a principal who is not a party to the evidential exchange as the exploited. A referee in ethics will want to know whether the account is really about exploitation or about a fiduciary's failure to secure the principal's interests.
6. *Priority and framing in philosophy of science*: Stegenga's and Wilholt's treatments of design bias and of methodological standards as conventions; Stephen John (2018) on why transparency is the wrong target for science-communication ethics; Biddle and Leuschner (2015) on the manufacture of doubt. A referee will ask how the paper's "design channel" differs from what Stegenga calls "malleability" of medical research.
7. *Resolute choice*: the paper says the receiver must be able to "bind herself to a rule" and then declines to say whether that is rational, on the ground that institutional rules are observable and private resolutions are not. But the paper's own ethical claim is about *individual* receivers (patients, jurors, the public) who cannot commit; and for them the "counter-move" is simply unavailable, which the paper acknowledges. So the central positive claim reduces to: institutions that can commit should set standards above the error-cost threshold. That is Henry–Ottaviani's result plus Kaplow's, with an exploitation gloss.

**Appendix check.** Proposition 1 is correct as stated: with p = 1 and q* = μ(1−t*)/((1−μ)t*), Pr(pass) = μ/t*, and since v coincides with the reject line ℓ on [0, t*], the receiver's expected utility (μ/t*)ℓ(t*) + (1 − μ/t*)ℓ(0) = ℓ(μ) = v(μ) by linearity. The commitment derivation (EU strictly above v(μ) for t > t*, tending to the full-information value as t → 1) is correct. Numerical checks: cursed-receiver approval 0.83 and accuracy 0.47; sophisticated approval 0.30 and accuracy 0.89; q* = 3/7; accuracy 1 − μ(1−t)/t equals 0.7, 0.9, 0.967, 0.984 at t = 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 0.95; q(0.95) = 3/133. All correct. Two things the proof relies on but does not flag as load-bearing: the sender's payoff is state-independent (a sponsor who also loses from approvals of harmful drugs, e.g. through liability, changes the optimal q), and the restriction to two-outcome experiments is without loss only because the receiver has two actions.
