# Annotated bibliography: philosophy side of "selection vs design"

Compiled 2026-09-29. Verification key used throughout:

- **[V]** = citation details confirmed against Crossref, the publisher page, PhilPapers/PhilArchive, or the SEP page itself; content summary drawn from the abstract or text I fetched.
- **[V-cit]** = citation details confirmed, but I could not retrieve the abstract/text, so the content summary is from my own knowledge of the work and should be checked against the text before quoting.
- **[U]** = not verified; treat as a lead.

The thesis being served: selection (choosing which already-generated results to pass on) is neutralised by a receiver who conditionalises on the generating process; design (choosing which experiments run, what is measured, sample size) is not, and can move a fully rational receiver's decisions while leaving her beliefs calibrated. That is not lying, misleading, deception or manipulation on the standard accounts, but is a wrong of exploitation: the sender exploits the receiver's evidential dependence and appropriates the value of information.

## A. Manipulation

### Noggle, Robert (1996). "Manipulative Actions: A Conceptual and Moral Analysis." *American Philosophical Quarterly* 33(1): 43–55. [V]
Noggle's "ideals" (or "trickery") account: manipulation is an attempt to get someone to adopt a belief, desire or emotion that falls short of the ideals governing that state, as the manipulator herself sees those ideals (false belief, attention drawn away from relevant considerations, inappropriate emotion, etc.). The analysis is influencer-centred: what matters is that the manipulator *tries* to lead the target astray relative to the manipulator's own standards.
**Conditions required:** intent to induce a *non-ideal* mental state; no covertness requirement; no requirement that reason be bypassed (one can manipulate by giving reasons that distract).
**Bearing:** *Must be answered.* Noggle is the most natural account on which design could count as manipulation, because "drawing attention selectively" is one of his paradigm forms. The paper's reply has to be that a calibrated posterior induced by a chosen experiment is not a belief that "falls short of the ideal" by the sender's own lights: the receiver's belief is exactly what the evidence warrants. The paper should say explicitly why a *decision* moved without a belief being made non-ideal is outside Noggle's net. Note that Noggle's ideals include ideals for *desire* and *attention*; the design case arguably leaves desire untouched but does channel attention.

### Noggle, Robert (2020). "Pressure, Trickery, and a Unified Account of Manipulation." *American Philosophical Quarterly* 57(3): 241–252. [V-cit]
Extends the 1996 account to cover pressure (nagging, guilt-tripping) as well as trickery, arguing both aim to produce mental states or actions that fall short of the relevant ideals. Useful only as the current statement of the ideals view; same bearing as 1996.

### Noggle, Robert (2018/2026). "The Ethics of Manipulation." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (first published 30 March 2018; substantive revision 31 March 2026). https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-manipulation/ [V]
Surveys the field under five headings: (i) trickery/ideals accounts (Noggle, Barnhill); (ii) covert-influence accounts (Susser, Roessler & Nissenbaum); (iii) bypassing-rational-agency accounts (a family; the entry singles out Renzo's version, on which manipulation exploits rather than bypasses practical agency); (iv) pressure accounts (Faden, Beauchamp & King's "harder to resist than persuasion, easier than coercion"); (v) Gorin's view that manipulation "deliberately fails to track reasons" and can operate through reasons. The entry states plainly that covertness is not necessary: "many manipulative influences, such as peer pressure, nagging, and guilt trips, do not seem covert."
**Bearing:** *Supports* the paper's framing that no single standard account has covertness, bypassing reason, or false belief as jointly necessary, so the paper cannot dispose of "manipulation" by pointing to overtness alone. The paper's negative claim ("not manipulation on the standard accounts") must be run against each of the five families separately; the entry gives the map for doing that. The 2026 revision is the one to cite.

### Coons, Christian & Weber, Michael (eds.) (2014). *Manipulation: Theory and Practice*. New York: Oxford University Press. ISBN 9780199338207. [V]
Contents (verified from OUP): Wood, "Coercion, Manipulation, Exploitation" (17–50); Barnhill, "What Is Manipulation?" (51–72); Gorin, "Towards a Theory of Interpersonal Manipulation" (73–97); Baron, "The Mens Rea and Moral Status of Manipulation"; Blumenthal-Barby, "A Framework for Assessing the Moral Status of 'Manipulation'"; Mills, "Manipulation as an Aesthetic Flaw"; Long, "Information Manipulation and Moral Responsibility"; Cave, "Unsavory Seduction and Manipulation" (176–200); Cholbi, "The Implications of Ego Depletion..."; Manne, "Non-Machiavellian Manipulation and the Opacity of Motive". Page ranges for Wood, Barnhill, Gorin and Cave are from the SEP bibliography.

#### Barnhill, Anne (2014). "What Is Manipulation?" In Coons & Weber (eds.), 51–72. DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199338207.003.0003. [V-cit]
Builds on Noggle's ideals account but adds a self-interest clause: manipulation is directly influencing someone's beliefs, desires or emotions such that she falls short of the ideals for those states *in ways typically not in her self-interest, or likely not in her self-interest in the present context*. (Wording recalled; check against p. 72.)
**Conditions required:** non-ideal mental state; not in target's interest; no covertness or bypassing condition.
**Bearing:** *Must be answered.* Barnhill's self-interest clause is the one most likely to catch design: the receiver's decision is moved against her interest. But Barnhill's definiens is still a *non-ideal mental state*; the paper's point that the receiver's credences are calibrated and well-founded means the first conjunct fails, however the interest clause comes out. Worth citing because it shows that "against the target's interest" is already treated by the literature as insufficient for manipulation without a defect in the mental state.

#### Gorin, Moti (2014). "Towards a Theory of Interpersonal Manipulation." In Coons & Weber (eds.), 73–97. DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199338207.003.0004. [V-cit]
Mostly negative: argues against accounts that require deception, bypassing rationality, or covertness. Sketches the view that manipulation consists in influence that is *not appropriately responsive to reasons*, i.e. the manipulator treats the target's reasons as causal levers and is indifferent to whether the influence tracks reasons that justify the outcome.

#### Gorin, Moti (2014). "Do Manipulators Always Threaten Rationality?" *American Philosophical Quarterly* 51(1): 51–61. [V]
The sharper statement of the same view, and the paper directly relevant here. Gorin attacks the "Bypass or Subvert View" (manipulation necessarily bypasses or subverts rational capacities) with two cases: *Trust Me* (an adviser gives sound arguments for two months solely to build trust for a later lie he never tells) and *Global Warming* (a candidate adopts popular policies solely because they win votes, intending to pursue her real priority afterwards). In both, the influencer fully engages the target's rational capacities with good reasons, yet manipulates. The key condition is *indifference to justificatory quality*: manipulators "judge reasons not by their quality but by their efficacy" as "causal levers."
**Conditions required:** intentional influence + indifference to whether the reasons given justify the response; explicitly *no* bypassing, *no* deception, *no* covertness.
**Bearing:** *Must be answered; this is the main threat to the "not manipulation" claim.* The strategic designer gives true evidence and is indifferent to whether it justifies the receiver's decision; he chose the experiment for its efficacy in moving her. On Gorin's view that is manipulation "through reason." The paper has three options: (a) argue that Gorin's condition over-generates (any advocate who selects arguments for efficacy would count), (b) accept that design is manipulation on Gorin's view and argue this is a reason to prefer the exploitation diagnosis, or (c) argue that the designer is *not* indifferent to justification because the experiment genuinely justifies whatever posterior it produces, so the reasons given are good by construction; what he is indifferent to is the *decision*, not the belief. Option (c) seems strongest and dovetails with the belief/decision distinction. The paper should also note that Gorin's cases involve a *concealed* ulterior motive (the later lie; the post-election priority), whereas the design case is overt about motive.

#### Wood, Allen W. (2014). "Coercion, Manipulation, Exploitation." In Coons & Weber (eds.), 17–50. DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199338207.003.0002. [V-cit]
Wood distinguishes the three by their relation to rational agency: coercion removes options, manipulation influences through means that do not appeal to (or that bypass) the target's reason (playing on emotions, weaknesses, biases), and exploitation is using another's vulnerability (weakness, need, dependence) to one's own advantage. Wood, following his 1995 "Exploitation" (*Social Philosophy and Policy* 12(2): 136–158), stresses that exploitation is compatible with consent and with the exploited party's benefiting: what makes it exploitation is the *use of vulnerability for advantage*, and what makes it wrong is a failure of respect. (Chapter abstract not retrievable; summary from knowledge of the chapter and of Wood 1995.)
**Bearing:** *Supports* on two fronts. First, on Wood's account of manipulation (non-rational means) design is clearly *not* manipulation: it works entirely through the receiver's rational updating. Second, Wood's account of exploitation is the one the paper's positive thesis most resembles: the receiver's *dependence* on the sender for evidence is exactly the kind of vulnerability Wood has in mind, and Wood already argues that consent and mutual benefit do not dissolve exploitation. Wood is thus the bridge between sections A and C; cite him for the claim that the same chapter that rules design out of manipulation rules it into exploitation.

#### Cave, Eric M. (2007). "What's Wrong with Motive Manipulation?" *Ethical Theory and Moral Practice* 10(2): 129–144. DOI 10.1007/s10677-006-9052-4. [V-cit]
Cave distinguishes motive manipulation (inducing a desire or motive by non-rational means) from other influence, and argues its wrong lies in bypassing rational self-governance rather than in harm or consent violations. Also Cave (2014), "Unsavory Seduction and Manipulation," in Coons & Weber, 176–200.
**Conditions required:** non-rational (bypassing) influence on *motives*.
**Bearing:** Neutral/supports the "not manipulation" claim, since design does not touch motives and works through reasons. Low priority.

### Susser, Daniel, Roessler, Beate & Nissenbaum, Helen (2019). "Online Manipulation: Hidden Influences in a Digital World." *Georgetown Law Technology Review* 4(1): 1–45. [V]
### Susser, Daniel, Roessler, Beate & Nissenbaum, Helen (2019). "Technology, Autonomy, and Manipulation." *Internet Policy Review* 8(2). DOI 10.14763/2019.2.1410. [V]
The covert-influence account: manipulation is "intentionally and covertly influencing [someone's] decision-making, by targeting and exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities." Covert = "influencing them in a way they aren't consciously aware of." Persuasion and coercion are "forthright"; deception is one species of manipulation; nudges are manipulative only when covert and vulnerability-exploiting.
**Conditions required:** covertness (hidden influence) *and* exploitation of decision-making vulnerabilities.
**Bearing:** *Supports* the "not manipulation" claim: design is overt (the receiver knows which experiment was run and why) so the covertness condition fails. Note however that their second condition, "exploiting decision-making vulnerabilities," is close kin to the paper's own exploitation diagnosis; the paper can say that Susser et al. locate the wrong in the wrong place (hiddenness) while correctly seeing that vulnerability-exploitation is at issue.

### Klenk, Michael (2022). "(Online) Manipulation: Sometimes Hidden, Always Careless." *Review of Social Economy* 80(1): 85–105. DOI 10.1080/00346764.2021.1894350. [V]
Against Susser et al.: covertness is neither necessary nor sufficient (guilt trips, recognised targeted advertising, propaganda are overt and manipulative; making manipulation depend on whether the victim notices misplaces its ground in the victim rather than the influencer). Klenk's "careless influence" definition: "M aims to manipulate a patient S if and only if a) M aims for S to do, think, or feel b through some method m and b) M disregards whether m reveals eventually existing reasons for S to do, think, or feel for b to S." Explicitly builds on Gorin.
**Conditions required:** intention to influence + disregard for whether the method *reveals reasons* to the target. No covertness, no bypassing, no false belief.
**Bearing:** *Must be answered, alongside Gorin.* Klenk's condition is if anything harder to escape than Gorin's, because it is about whether the method *reveals reasons*, and a well-designed experiment reveals reasons par excellence. That cuts the paper's way: the strategic designer's method (running an informative experiment) *does* reveal to the receiver whatever reasons exist, so condition (b) fails. Klenk is therefore the account on which the paper can most cleanly say "not manipulation" while conceding that the motive is self-serving. The paper should quote the definition and make this point explicitly; it is the strongest reason-based account and it lets design out.

### Buss, Sarah (2005). "Valuing Autonomy and Respecting Persons: Manipulation, Seduction, and the Basis of Moral Constraints." *Ethics* 115(2): 195–235. DOI 10.1086/426304. [V-cit]
Buss argues that many manipulative and seductive influences (non-rational influences on belief and desire) are morally unobjectionable, and that the wrongness of the objectionable ones is not well explained by appeal to a right to autonomy or to "respect for rational agency." She locates the basis of the moral constraints instead in something closer to the value of the person's well-being and our duties of care toward them. (Abstract not retrievable; summary from knowledge of the text.)
**Bearing:** *Supports* indirectly: Buss decouples "wrong influence" from "autonomy-violating influence," which is what the paper needs, since the receiver's autonomy is intact (she reasons correctly from true evidence) and yet she is wronged. Buss can be cited for the general point that the wrong of an influence need not consist in what it does to the target's rational agency.

## B. Lying, misleading, deception with truths; misleading evidence; selection effects

### Saul, Jennifer Mather (2012). *Lying, Misleading, and What Is Said: An Exploration in Philosophy of Language and in Ethics*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. [V]
Lying = saying P, believing P false, in a context one takes to be warranting. Misleading = conveying a falsehood without saying it (implicature, careful truths; the Clinton/Athanasius cases). Chapter 4 ("Is Lying Worse than Merely Misleading?") argues against the common view that lying is morally worse than misleading, rebutting Adler's "hearer contributes the inference, so the misleader is less responsible" defence with mugging analogies.
**Bearing:** *Complicates* the paper's "not misleading in the standard sense." Saul's misleading requires that the speaker intend a false belief to be conveyed; design induces no false belief, so design escapes even Saul's broad category. But Saul's *moral* conclusion (the lying/misleading line does little moral work) is grist for the paper's claim that the interesting moral line is elsewhere (exploitation), not in the content/implicature distinction. Also note Saul's "warranting context" machinery: a sender who presents evidence in a context where the receiver expects representativeness may be warranting something he does not deliver; the paper should say why design does not breach a warrant (the receiver knows the process).

### Stokke, Andreas (2016). "Lying and Misleading in Discourse." *Philosophical Review* 125(1): 83–134. DOI 10.1215/00318108-3321731. [V]
### Stokke, Andreas (2018). *Lying and Insincerity*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. [V-cit; the 2018 OUP volume DOI prefix 10.1093/oso/9780198825968 confirmed via Crossref for its ch. 5, "The Difference between Lying and Misleading"]
Lying = proposing to add to the common ground something one believes false (a Stalnakerian assertion account, no intent-to-deceive requirement). Misleading = conveying a disbelieved proposition without proposing it for the common ground, analysed via the *question under discussion*: an utterance is misleading when it is a true but incomplete answer to the QUD that invites a false complete answer. The 2016 paper explicitly covers "incompleteness" and "partial answers" as ways of misleading with truths.
**Bearing:** *Must be answered.* Stokke's QUD apparatus is the most precise existing account of misleading-with-truths, and it is the natural framework in which to say why *selection* is misleading: a cherry-picked result is a true partial answer to "does the drug work?" that invites a false complete answer. The paper's claim is that *design* is not like this: the sender does not offer a partial answer to a QUD with a false completion in view; he answers a *different* (self-servingly chosen) question fully and truthfully. Stokke gives the paper the tools to state that difference cleanly, and also a possible objection: if the receiver's QUD is "should I approve?", a well-chosen experiment is a partial answer to *that*. Reply: the receiver's calibrated posterior on "should I approve?" is exactly right given the evidence; nothing false is invited.

### Adler, Jonathan E. (1997). "Lying, Deceiving, or Falsely Implicating." *Journal of Philosophy* 94(9): 435–452. DOI 10.2307/2564617. [V-cit; Crossref shows first page 435; last page 452 from memory]
Defends the view that lying is worse than falsely implicating because in the latter the hearer contributes the inference and so shares responsibility; ties this to the Gricean picture on which implicature depends on the hearer's cooperative reasoning.
**Bearing:** *Supports* a small piece of the paper: Adler's mechanism (the hearer does the inferential work) is one reason overt, reason-mediated influence looks less culpable. The paper's design case pushes this to the limit: the receiver does *all* the inferential work, correctly, and the sender's contribution is only to choose the question. Adler's line, taken seriously, would exonerate the designer; the paper needs the exploitation account precisely because the responsibility-sharing story runs out.

### Chisholm, Roderick M. & Feehan, Thomas D. (1977). "The Intent to Deceive." *Journal of Philosophy* 74(3): 143–159. DOI 10.2307/2025605. [V-cit; last page from memory]
The classic taxonomy of deception (positive/negative, by commission/omission, causing belief/preventing belief/allowing loss of belief), and the "breach of faith" account of why lying is distinctively wrong: the liar exploits the hearer's trust that the speaker is expressing his own belief.
**Bearing:** *Supports* the taxonomy the paper needs. Chisholm & Feehan's "negative deception by omission" (allowing someone to persist in a false belief) is the category selection lives in; design does not fit any of their eight cells, because no false belief is caused, sustained or protected. Also useful: their "breach of faith" idea is structurally parallel to the paper's exploitation diagnosis (the wrong is in what the speaker does with the hearer's *reliance*), but they tie it to false belief; the paper generalises it to reliance-for-evidence.

### Carson, Thomas L. (2010). *Lying and Deception: Theory and Practice*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. [V]
Lying = a believed-false statement made in a context where one warrants its truth (no intent-to-deceive requirement). Deception = intentionally causing someone to have a false belief that one believes false or does not believe true. Carson treats *withholding information* as deceptive only when there is "a clear expectation, promise, and/or professional obligation" to disclose; he also discusses half-truths.
**Bearing:** *Complicates.* Carson's disclosure-obligation clause is the most direct way the literature could try to bring *selection* under deception (a drug company has an obligation to disclose, so withholding trials is deceptive). Design is again outside: there is nothing withheld. But Carson's clause invites the question the paper needs to address: is there an obligation to run the *representative* experiment, analogous to the obligation to disclose? The paper's answer (no such obligation is breached; the wrong is exploitation, not deception) should be stated against Carson.

### Mahon, James Edwin (2008/2015). "The Definition of Lying and Deception." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lying-definition/ [V]
Surveys definitions of lying (Chisholm & Feehan, Carson, Fallis, Stokke etc.) and deception (the standard "intentionally cause a false belief" definition, D1). Covers *palters* (true statements intended to convey falsehoods), double bluffs, and the question whether "negative deception" (keeping someone ignorant / preventing true belief) is deception at all; some hold that "to prevent someone from acquiring a true belief is to keep that person in ignorance," not to deceive, since no false belief is acquired.
**Bearing:** *Supports.* Gives the standard definition of deception against which design plainly fails (no false belief), and the ignorance/deception distinction the paper can use for selection (which at most keeps the receiver ignorant of unfavourable results). The paper's novelty is that design does not even keep her ignorant of anything that exists.

### Fallis, Don (2015). "What Is Disinformation?" *Library Trends* 63(3): 401–426. DOI 10.1353/lib.2015.0014. [V]
Disinformation = "misleading information that has the function of misleading" (p. 413), where misleading = "likely to create false beliefs" (p. 406) and the function may be acquired by design or because the misleading systematically benefits the source. Explicitly allows *true* disinformation: Athanasius's "not far away" (p. 409); political "spin" that "selectively emphasiz[es] only certain facts" (p. 409); the Black Flag roach advert using resistant roaches (p. 409).
**Bearing:** *Must be answered.* Fallis is the philosopher who has most clearly said that accurate, selectively emphasised information is disinformation, and he uses a probabilistic notion of misleading. Two of his three true-disinformation examples are *selection* cases (spin) and one, the roach advert, is arguably a *design* case (the experiment was rigged by choosing the population). The paper needs to say whether the roach advert is design in its sense (it is not: the sender concealed the breeding, so the receiver did not conditionalise on the process) and to insist that on Fallis's own definition, design against a process-conditioning receiver is not disinformation because it is not likely to create a false belief: it creates a *calibrated* one.

### Skyrms, Brian (2010). *Signals: Evolution, Learning, and Information*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199580828.001.0001. [V-cit] Chapter on deception.
### Martínez, Manolo (2015). "Deception in Sender–Receiver Games." *Erkenntnis* 80(1): 215–227. DOI 10.1007/s10670-014-9623-z. [V-cit]
### Fallis, Don & Lewis, Peter J. (2019). "Toward a Formal Analysis of Deceptive Signaling." *Synthese* 196(6): 2279–2303. DOI 10.1007/s11229-017-1536-3. [V-cit]
This is the closest thing in philosophy to a formal "Bayesian definition of misleading": Skyrms defines a signal as carrying *misinformation* when it raises the receiver's probability of a state that does not obtain, and as *deceptive* when it is misinformation that benefits the sender at the receiver's expense (and is systematic, not accidental). Martínez and Fallis & Lewis refine this (e.g. requiring that the signal move the receiver away from the truth relative to what she would otherwise believe, and handling half-truths). (Content from knowledge of the works; abstracts not retrieved.)
**Bearing:** *Directly supports the belief/decision distinction.* On the Skyrms-style definition, a signal chosen by the sender but truthfully reported is *not* misinformation, because the receiver's posterior is correct given the signal, and it is not deceptive in the technical sense even though it benefits the sender at her expense. The paper should say explicitly that Bayesian persuasion is *non-deceptive signalling that nonetheless harms the receiver*, which is exactly the gap these definitions leave open, and that Fallis & Lewis's attention to half-truths shows the literature has approached but not reached the design case.

### Sober, Elliott (2009). "Absence of Evidence and Evidence of Absence: Evidential Transitivity in Connection with Fossils, Fishing, Fine-Tuning, and Firing Squads." *Philosophical Studies* 143(1): 63–90. DOI 10.1007/s11098-008-9315-0. [V-cit] (Online 2008; print vol. 143 is 2009.)
### Weisberg, Jonathan (2005). "Firing Squads and Fine-Tuning: Sober on the Design Argument." *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 56(4): 809–821. DOI 10.1093/bjps/axi139. [V-cit]
### White, Roger (2000). "Fine-Tuning and Multiple Universes." *Noûs* 34(2): 260–276. DOI 10.1111/0029-4624.00210. [V-cit]
### Kotzen, Matthew (2012). "Selection Biases in Likelihood Arguments." *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 63(4): 825–839. DOI 10.1093/bjps/axr044. [V-cit]
The observation-selection-effect debate. Sober (Eddington's fishing net; the firing squad) argues that the evidence one must conditionalise on is not "E" but "I observed E," and that when the observation procedure could only have delivered E the likelihood argument is blocked; Weisberg replies that the firing-squad and fine-tuning cases come apart and that Sober's principle over-blocks; White argues that "this universe is fine-tuned" (not "some universe is") is the total evidence and does not confirm many universes; Kotzen (2012 BJPS) gives a general treatment of when a selection bias in the process by which evidence was obtained should be built into the likelihoods. (Summaries from knowledge; abstracts not retrieved.)
**Bearing:** *Supports the framework.* This literature is the philosophical home of "the total evidence includes how you got the evidence," which is the receiver's counter to selection. The paper should cite it as establishing, independently of any strategic setting, that a rational agent conditionalises on the observation process. The paper's twist is that when the *sender* controls the process and the receiver knows it, conditionalising on the process neutralises selection but not design.

### Kotzen, Matthew (2012). "Dragging and Confirming." *Philosophical Review* 121(1): 55–93. DOI 10.1215/00318108-1426364. [V]
Abstract: addresses when evidence for a stronger claim H1 is also evidence for a weaker H2; "Always" is false on the Bayesian conception; proposes the "Dragging Condition" and applies it to transmission failure and bootstrapping.
**Bearing:** *Less relevant than the request assumed.* This paper is about evidential transmission, not observation selection; the selection-effect paper by Kotzen is the BJPS one above. "Dragging and Confirming" is worth a footnote only if the paper discusses whether evidence for "the drug passed *this* trial" transmits to "the drug works."

### Grünwald, Peter D. & Halpern, Joseph Y. (2003). "Updating Probabilities." *Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research* 19: 243–278. DOI 10.1613/jair.1164. [V]
Formal treatment of *naive conditioning* (conditioning on the content of the observation in a "naive space" that ignores the protocol) versus conditioning in a "sophisticated space" that includes the observation protocol (Monty Hall, three prisoners). Characterises the CAR ("coarsening at random") condition under which naive conditioning gives the right answer, and shows it often fails. Cites Shafer (1985), "Conditional Probability," *International Statistical Review* 53: 261–277 [V-cit] as the origin of the protocol point.
**Bearing:** *Supports.* This is the cleanest formal statement of the paper's "conditionalise on the process, not merely the content" contrast: selection violates CAR, so the naive-space receiver is misled and the sophisticated-space receiver is not. The paper can borrow CAR to say precisely what "process-conditioning" means, and can note that Bayesian persuasion is what remains *after* the receiver moves to the sophisticated space.

### Royall, Richard (1997). *Statistical Evidence: A Likelihood Paradigm*. London: Chapman & Hall. [U: not verified in this session]
Defines "misleading evidence" as evidence (a likelihood ratio) favouring a false hypothesis over a true one, and bounds the probability of strong misleading evidence. Lead only; if the paper wants a canonical statement of "E is misleading about H iff it raises credence in a false H," this and the Skyrms/Fallis–Lewis line are the candidates. I did not find a single canonical *philosophical* source for the Bayesian definition of misleading evidence; the notion is used, not defended, in the misleading-defeaters and fine-tuning literatures.

### Herresthal, Claudia (2022). "Hidden Testing and Selective Disclosure of Evidence." *Journal of Economic Theory* 200: 105402. DOI 10.1016/j.jet.2021.105402. [V] (Economics, not philosophy; flagged because it is on the exact selection/design seam.)
A decision maker (higher safety standard) relies on an agent who runs sequential tests; compares "hidden testing" (agent controls both what is run and what is disclosed) with "observable testing." Hidden testing can be Pareto-improving when conflict is large but leaves the decision maker worse off when conflict is small.
**Bearing:** *Complicates.* Shows that in a formal model the value of transparency about *testing* (design) versus *disclosure* (selection) depends on the degree of preference conflict; the paper's claim that transparency neutralises selection but not design should be checked against this result, and the paper should say whether Herresthal's decision maker is process-conditioning (she is Bayesian and knows the game).

## C. Exploitation

### Wertheimer, Alan (1996). *Exploitation*. Princeton: Princeton University Press. [V]
Exploitation = taking unfair advantage; fairness baseline is a hypothetical competitive market free of informational asymmetry and undue pressure. Distinguishes *harmful* exploitation from *mutually advantageous* exploitation (both gain, but the exploited gains less than she should because the exploiter captures an unfair share of the cooperative surplus), and *consensual* from *non-consensual*. Central puzzle: how can mutually advantageous, consensual exploitation be seriously wrong, given that a transaction that leaves both better off cannot be worse than no transaction ("nonworseness")? Wertheimer's answer is that consent and mutual benefit defeat *third-party interference* and may defeat a *right* to a better deal, but do not defeat the *judgment of unfairness*; the moral force of exploitation-claims is real but limited.
**Bearing:** *Supports the positive thesis and sets its terms.* The receiver in the design case is (weakly) better off than with no evidence, and she consents (she chose to rely); so the paper's exploitation claim must be of the mutually-advantageous-consensual kind, and Wertheimer is the source for why that is still a wrong. The paper's specific fairness baseline (what the receiver would have got from a *disinterested* experimenter, or from a competitive market in experiments) should be stated in Wertheimer's terms, and Wertheimer's "informational asymmetry" exclusion from the baseline is exactly the point: the receiver depends on the sender for evidence. Note Wertheimer's own scepticism about how much *action-guiding* force mutually advantageous exploitation has; the paper's "commit in advance to a demanding standard" is a receiver-side remedy that sits comfortably with that scepticism.

### Zwolinski, Matt, Ferguson, Benjamin & Wertheimer, Alan (2001/2022). "Exploitation." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (first published 20 Dec 2001; substantive revision 3 Oct 2022). https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/exploitation/ [V]
Survey. Key items: the nonworseness claim (NWC) and its critics; Valdman (excessive benefit from those who cannot reasonably refuse); Vrousalis (domination: instrumentalising another's vulnerability to extract benefit); Sample (degradation: failing to respect inherent value); Wood (vulnerability). The entry summarises why each account holds consensual mutually beneficial exploitation can still be wrong: fairness is an independent requirement; the exploiter's knowing use of vulnerability adds culpability; respect constrains advantage-taking regardless of net benefit.
**Bearing:** *Supports.* Use for the map and for the standard rebuttals of NWC, which the paper will meet ("the receiver is better off with the designed experiment than with none, so what's the complaint?").

### Valdman, Mikhail (2009). "A Theory of Wrongful Exploitation." *Philosophers' Imprint* 9(6): 1–14. [V]
Exploitation "is not always wrong, but ... it can be, and ... its wrongness cannot be fully explained with familiar moral constraints" (harm, coercion, deception). Wrongful exploitation = extracting *excessive* benefit from someone who *cannot, or cannot reasonably, refuse* one's offer; the wrong is the violation of a duty not to do this, and it is compatible with consent and with the exploitee's benefiting.
**Bearing:** *Supports, and gives the two conditions the paper must establish.* (i) Cannot reasonably refuse: the receiver has no alternative source of evidence, so she cannot reasonably refuse the sender's experiment. (ii) Excessive benefit: the sender captures the value of information that a fair process would have delivered to the receiver. The paper's "appropriates the value of information" is a Valdman-style excess claim, and the paper should state the baseline that makes the benefit *excessive* (e.g. the receiver's expected payoff under the receiver-optimal experiment). Valdman's insistence that the wrong is *not* reducible to harm/coercion/deception exactly parallels the paper's "not lying, not deceiving, not manipulating."

### Vrousalis, Nicholas (2013). "Exploitation, Vulnerability, and Social Domination." *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 41(2): 131–157. DOI 10.1111/papa.12013. [V-cit]
Exploitation as *domination for self-enrichment*: A exploits B when A and B are in a systematic relationship in which A instrumentalises B's vulnerability to extract a net benefit from B. The wrong is the instrumentalisation of vulnerability (a form of domination), not unfairness of outcome; consent and mutual benefit are beside the point. (Abstract not retrieved; formulation from memory, corroborated by the SEP summary.)
**Bearing:** *Supports, and is probably the best fit for the paper's diagnosis.* The receiver's *epistemic dependence* is a structural vulnerability; the sender who chooses the experiment to move her decision instrumentalises that dependence. Vrousalis's account explains why the wrong survives the receiver's calibration and consent, and why it is a wrong *to her* rather than a mere unfairness. Vrousalis's requirement of a *systematic relationship* fits regulator/industry and funder/scientist cases well.

### Sample, Ruth J. (2003). *Exploitation: What It Is and Why It's Wrong*. Lanham, MD: Rowman & Littlefield. [V]
Exploitation = interacting with another for one's own advantage in a way that *degrades* her, i.e. fails to respect her inherent value; three forms: failing to respect basic needs, commodifying what should not be commodified, and taking advantage of injustice. Rejects the market-fairness baseline.
**Bearing:** *Supports* an alternative, respect-based framing. If the paper prefers "appropriating the value of information" as an unfairness claim, Wertheimer/Valdman is the frame; if it prefers "failing to respect the receiver as an inquirer," Sample and Wood are. The paper should choose and say why.

### Zwolinski, Matt (2007). "Sweatshops, Choice, and Exploitation." *Business Ethics Quarterly* 17(4): 689–727. DOI 10.5840/beq20071745. [V]
Abstract (verified): workers' choices to accept sweatshop conditions are morally significant as exercises of autonomy and expressions of preference; this grounds a claim against third-party interference and against demands that MNEs improve conditions as a matter of *right*; defended against the objections that consent is not voluntary, that the package harms workers, and "that even if sweatshop labor benefits workers, it is nevertheless wrongfully exploitative." Zwolinski uses the nonworseness claim to press the last point.
**Bearing:** *Must be answered.* Zwolinski is the standard-bearer for "consensual, mutually beneficial, therefore not seriously wrong and not a ground for interference." Applied here: the receiver chose to rely on the sender, is better off than with no evidence, so the sender does nothing wrong and transparency mandates are unjustified. The paper's reply should (a) invoke the standard anti-NWC replies (a beneficial interaction can still be wrong *as an interaction*; cf. Kates 2022, "Sweatshops, Exploitation, and the Nonworseness Claim," *BEQ* 33(4): 682–703, DOI 10.1017/beq.2022.11 [V-cit]) and (b) note that the receiver's consent is to *reliance*, not to the sender's choice of experiment, so the consent does not cover the exploitative act.

## D. Industry and science; social epistemology of propaganda; reform

### Weatherall, James Owen, O'Connor, Cailin & Bruner, Justin P. (2020). "How to Beat Science and Influence People: Policymakers and Propaganda in Epistemic Networks." *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 71(4): 1157–1186. DOI 10.1093/bjps/axy062. [V; full text read]
Models Oreskes & Conway's "tobacco strategy" in a Zollman-style bandit network. Two treatments: *selective sharing* (propagandist passes on only those real, independently produced results that favour the industry) and *biased production* (propagandist runs its own studies and publishes selectively). Policy makers "rationally update on all evidence available to them," but by strict conditionalisation on the *content* of reported results, treating propagandist-sourced results at face value (Appendix A: credence updated by multiplying likelihood ratios from scientists and propagandist alike). Findings: selective sharing is cheaper and often *more* effective than biased production, especially in large communities working on hard problems, because the community itself generates enough spurious results to share; under biased production the propagandist does best by splitting a fixed budget into "the smallest possible chunks" (many tiny studies, publish the failures of the rival arm); policy makers do better when scientific resources go to a few large studies; and "fair" journalism mimics selective sharing.
**Bearing:** *Central, and complicates in an instructive way.* (1) Their policy makers are exactly the naive-space receivers of Grünwald & Halpern: they do not conditionalise on the sharing process. The paper can present its selection result as what happens when the WOB policy maker is upgraded to process-conditioning (Milgrom-style unravelling), and should say so explicitly. (2) Their "biased production" result about sample splitting is a *design* choice, but its power in their model derives entirely from the subsequent *selection*; a process-conditioning receiver would unravel it. The paper's claim that design retains power against such a receiver is therefore *not* WOB's result, and the paper should mark that the KG-style design effect is a different mechanism from WOB's small-studies effect. (3) Their remedy (fund few large studies) is a receiver-side/institutional commitment to a demanding evidential standard, which is congenial to the paper's "commit in advance" counter-move.

### Holman, Bennett & Bruner, Justin P. (2015). "The Problem of Intransigently Biased Agents." *Philosophy of Science* 82(5): 956–968. DOI 10.1086/683344. [V]
Abstract (verified): extends Zollman's network model with an "epistemically impure" agent who never updates and always reports industry-favourable results; the community never converges to the truth; two solutions explored, including endogenous network formation in which agents choose whom to trust.
**Bearing:** *Supports* the "conditionalise on the source" move: their fix is for agents to learn to discount the biased source, i.e. to condition on process. Useful as the modelling precedent for the claim that selection/bias can be neutralised by source-tracking; note their biased agent fabricates, so this is a stronger case than selection.

### Holman, Bennett & Bruner, Justin P. (2017). "Experimentation by Industrial Selection." *Philosophy of Science* 84(5): 1008–1019. DOI 10.1086/694037. [V]
Abstract (verified): "given methodological diversity and a merit-based system, industry funding can bias a community without corrupting any particular individual"; industry funds scientists whose (honestly held) methods happen to yield favourable results; a seemingly clean solution (independent funding) "actually exacerbates the problem without additional restrictions."
**Bearing:** *Strongly supports the design category and its immunity to disclosure.* Industrial selection is *design* at the level of the community: choosing which experiments (methods, questions) get run, with nothing selected, suppressed or falsified afterward. Every scientist is honest and every result is published; disclosure reforms have nothing to bite on. The paper should cite this as the existing philosophical recognition that "which experiments get run" is a lever independent of "which results get shared," and note that Holman & Bruner's receiver (the community) does not model the funding process as evidence, whereas the paper's receiver does and is *still* moved.

### Holman, Bennett (2015). *The Fundamental Antagonism: Science and Commerce in Medical Epistemology*. PhD dissertation, UC Irvine. https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4kx8g2r1 [V]
Frames industry/science as an "asymmetric arms race": methodological rigour and epistemic reliability come apart because industry adapts to each methodological safeguard; medical epistemology must be understood socially, attending to how manufacturers shape the available information; "there is no final resolution ... There is only a next move."
**Bearing:** *Supports* the paper's structural claim that reforms neutralise one move (selection) and shift the game to the next (design). Holman is the source for the arms-race framing, and for the diagnosis that the *availability* of evidence, not its content, is what industry controls.

### Holman, Bennett & Geislar, Sally (2018). "Sex Drugs and Corporate Ventriloquism: How to Evaluate Science Policies Intended to Manage Industry-Funded Bias." *Philosophy of Science* 85(5): 869–881. DOI 10.1086/699713. [V]
Abstract (verified): case study of flibanserin ("female Viagra"); shows that a proposed policy (incorporating citizen stakeholders in research/regulation) can be co-opted; uses Holman's asymmetric-arms-race framework to evaluate policies.
**Bearing:** *Supports* the claim that procedural reforms are gamed; the paper can use it as evidence that reforms aimed at one move create the next. Also see Holman & Elliott (2018), "The Promise and Perils of Industry-Funded Science," *Philosophy Compass* 13(11), DOI 10.1111/phc3.12544 [V-cit] for a survey.

### O'Connor, Cailin & Weatherall, James Owen (2019). *The Misinformation Age: How False Beliefs Spread*. New Haven: Yale University Press. [V-cit]
Popular/synthetic treatment of the network models (Zollman; WOB; Holman & Bruner) with historical cases (tobacco, Vegetable Lamb, climate). Chapters on industrial propaganda present selective sharing and biased production as the core strategies.
**Bearing:** *Supports* as the accessible statement of the selection strategies; not needed beyond WOB 2020 for the argument.

### Mohseni, Aydin, O'Connor, Cailin & Weatherall, James Owen (2022). "The Best Paper You'll Read Today: Media Biases and the Public Understanding of Science." *Philosophical Topics* 50(2): 127–153. DOI 10.5840/philtopics202250220. [V; preprint full text read]
Models a journalist who selects events from a true distribution to report, under three curation practices (extreme-event reporting, "fair"/balanced reporting, hype), and members of the public who learn from the reports. **The question asked: does the modelled public update naively on content?** Yes. The public "updates their beliefs using Bayes' rule, under the assumptions that the actual distribution of events is normally distributed and that reported values are exchangeable" (sec. 3); agents fit a normal to the reported data as if it were an unbiased sample. There is no modelling of the selection procedure by the receiver. A variant adds confirmation bias (agents reject data far from their mean), which is a departure from Bayesian updating, not a sophistication of it. Conclusion: even norm-compliant curation practices distort public belief; ideal curation tracks the true distribution; single-study reporting is discouraged.
**Bearing:** *Confirms the paper's diagnosis of this literature.* The models' receivers are naive-space conditionalisers; all three "biases" are selection, not design; and a process-conditioning receiver would (in principle) unravel them. The paper can cite this alongside WOB as showing that the existing philosophical models of misleading-with-true-content locate the harm in receiver naivety, which is exactly what the paper's design result does *not* rely on.

### O'Connor, Cailin & Freeborn, David (2025). "Industrial Distraction." *Philosophy of Science* 92(3): 666–687. DOI 10.1017/psa.2025.1. [V; preprint full text read; note the preprint lists Freeborn first, the journal version O'Connor first]
Industry actors "fund and share research that is accurate and (often) high quality, but nonetheless misleads": e.g. Coca-Cola funding exercise science to shift causal attribution of obesity from sugar to sedentariness. Modelled with causal Bayesian networks; the mechanism is explaining-away: true evidence for an alternative cause rationally lowers credence in the industrial cause. Three variants (distracting causes; distracting downstream harms of policy; and one using inaccurate mitigants). Explicitly: "even fully rational learners and decision makers can err," no fraud or questionable practice is involved, and this bears on "philosophical ... debates about how to define and understand misleading content" (cites Fallis 2015).
**Bearing:** *Strongly supports the design category and is the closest existing philosophical treatment.* Industrial distraction is design: choosing which questions get investigated, with all results true and published. Two points to make: (1) their "rational learner" is rational about *content* (conditionalises in the causal network) but does not treat *the choice of what to fund* as evidence; the paper's receiver does, and the paper should show that the distraction effect partly survives that upgrade (this is the KG point) and partly does not. (2) They frame the harm as misleading content; the paper's alternative framing (no misleading; exploitation) is a direct rival and should be argued against them by name.

### Lewandowsky, Stephan, Pilditch, Toby D., Madsen, Jens K., Oreskes, Naomi & Risbey, James S. (2019). "Influence and Seepage: An Evidence-Resistant Minority Can Affect Public Opinion and Scientific Belief Formation." *Cognition* 188: 124–139. DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2019.01.011. [V-cit; abstract partially retrieved]
Agent-based model with Bayesian scientist-agents who communicate evidence to one another and to a public; a vocal, evidence-resistant minority delays scientific consensus and keeps the public "ambivalent." (Mechanism details, i.e. whether agents discount the minority as a source, not confirmed from the text.)
**Bearing:** Marginal; another naive-receiver model. Cite, if at all, as part of the same family as WOB and Mohseni et al.

### Bright, Liam Kofi (2017). "On Fraud." *Philosophical Studies* 174(2): 291–310. DOI 10.1007/s11098-016-0682-7. [V]
Abstract (verified): argues against promoting the veritistic ideal among scientists, since a sincere desire to see the truth propagated "may well promote more fraud rather than less" (a scientist convinced of the truth of H may fake results for H).
**Bearing:** *Complicates* the motive story. Bright shows that truth-directed motives can produce fraud; the paper's sender has self-interested motives and produces *no* fraud. Useful to show that the moral line does not track motive purity in either direction.

### Romero, Felipe (2016). "Can the Behavioral Sciences Self-Correct? A Social Epistemic Study." *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science Part A* 60: 55–69. DOI 10.1016/j.shpsa.2016.10.002. [V-cit]
Models a scientific community under publication bias (novel positive results rewarded, replications not) and shows that self-correction fails; recommends structural fixes (replication incentives, registration). Also Romero (2019), "Philosophy of Science and the Replicability Crisis," *Philosophy Compass* 14(11), DOI 10.1111/phc3.12633 [V-cit], for a survey; and Heesen, Remco (2018), "Why the Reward Structure of Science Makes Reproducibility Problems Inevitable," *Journal of Philosophy* 115(12): 661–674, DOI 10.5840/jphil20181151239 [V-cit].
**Bearing:** *Supports* the claim that the file drawer is a selection problem and that registration-type reforms target it. Romero/Heesen are the philosophy-of-science home for the file drawer.

### Zollman, Kevin J. S. (2007). "The Communication Structure of Epistemic Communities." *Philosophy of Science* 74(5): 574–587. DOI 10.1086/525605. [V]
### Zollman, Kevin J. S. (2010). "The Epistemic Benefit of Transient Diversity." *Erkenntnis* 72(1): 17–35. DOI 10.1007/s10670-009-9194-6. [V]
The bandit-network framework all the above models extend. Only needed as the source of the framework.

### Oreskes, Naomi & Conway, Erik M. (2010). *Merchants of Doubt*. New York: Bloomsbury Press. [V-cit]
Historical source for the tobacco strategy: the industry's funding of research and "selective sharing" of favourable results; Big Tobacco's own internal recognition that "doubt is our product."
**Bearing:** *Supports* the historical anchoring; the paper's "selection" category is their tobacco strategy, and the paper should note that the historical record also contains *design* moves (funding research on other causes of cancer, e.g. asbestos, radon; funding "confounders" research), which is what O'Connor & Freeborn call distraction.

### Douglas, Heather (2009). *Science, Policy, and the Value-Free Ideal*. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press. DOI 10.2307/j.ctt6wrc78. [V]
Inductive risk: value judgements are unavoidable in setting evidential thresholds; scientists bear responsibility for the consequences of error.
**Bearing:** *Supports* the receiver's counter-move. The paper's "commit in advance to a demanding standard of proof" is a standard-of-evidence choice of exactly the kind Douglas says is value-laden; Douglas can be cited to argue that the receiver's threshold is *hers* to set and that setting it strategically (anticipating persuasion) is legitimate.

### Elliott, Kevin C. (2011). *Is a Little Pollution Good for You? Incorporating Societal Values in Environmental Research*. New York: Oxford University Press. [V-cit]
Case studies (hormesis, chemical risk) of how industry shapes research agendas, framing and interpretation; argues for deliberate incorporation of societal values and for institutional safeguards.
**Bearing:** *Supports* the agenda-setting (design) category: Elliott's hormesis case is a design case (funding research on a framing that favours the funder), and he already argues that disclosure alone is insufficient.

### Biddle, Justin B. & Leuschner, Anna (2015). "Climate Skepticism and the Manufacture of Doubt: Can Dissent in Science Be Epistemically Detrimental?" *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* 5(3): 261–278. DOI 10.1007/s13194-014-0101-x. [V]
### de Melo-Martín, Inmaculada & Intemann, Kristen (2018). *The Fight Against Doubt: How to Bridge the Gap Between Scientists and the Public*. New York: Oxford University Press. DOI 10.1093/oso/9780190869229.001.0001. [V]
Biddle & Leuschner propose conditions for "epistemically detrimental dissent" (inductive-risk asymmetry, violation of conventional standards, etc.); de Melo-Martín & Intemann argue that no reliable criterion for "normatively inappropriate dissent" is available and that the focus on bad dissent is a distraction from institutional trust.
**Bearing:** *Complicates.* Both frame industry influence as *dissent* (content); the paper's design case is not dissent at all (the sender produces consensus-quality evidence). Cite to show that the doubt/dissent framing misses design, which manufactures no doubt.

### Pinto, Manuela Fernández & Pinto, Daniel Fernández (2023). "Epistemic Diversity and Industrial Selection Bias." *Synthese* 201: art. 182. DOI 10.1007/s11229-023-04158-7. [V]
Abstract (verified): reinforcement-learning model of industry funding allocation extending Holman & Bruner 2017; industry learns which scientists' methods produce favourable results and funds them; "the more epistemically diverse the scientific community, the easier it is for the industry to move scientific consensus"; a *random funding agent* counteracts industrial selection, so the authors propose lottery funding (two-stage: quality filter then lottery).
**Bearing:** *Supports, and supplies a remedy on the design side.* Lottery funding is an institutional way of taking the choice of which experiments get run out of interested hands; the paper can present it as the design-side analogue of pre-registration, and note that it works by *removing the sender's choice* rather than by making the choice transparent, which is exactly the asymmetry the paper predicts.

## E. Testimony from interested sources; persuasion and nudging

### Hardwig, John (1985). "Epistemic Dependence." *Journal of Philosophy* 82(7): 335–349. DOI 10.2307/2026523. [V-cit; last page from memory]
Rational deference to experts is unavoidable; the layperson's belief is justified by the expert's having good reasons, which the layperson cannot assess; knowledge is thus irreducibly social and rests on trust.
**Bearing:** *Supports the vulnerability premise.* Hardwig is the canonical source for the receiver's dependence on others for evidence, which the paper treats as the vulnerability exploited by design.

### Fricker, Elizabeth (1994). "Against Gullibility." In B. K. Matilal & A. Chakrabarti (eds.), *Knowing from Words*, Dordrecht: Kluwer, 125–161. DOI 10.1007/978-94-017-2018-2_8. [V]
Hearers must monitor speakers for trustworthiness (sincerity and competence); testimonial justification requires this local reductionist check.
**Bearing:** *Supports* the process-conditioning receiver: Fricker's monitoring is the testimonial analogue of conditionalising on source. Design defeats monitoring because the sender is sincere *and* competent.

### Lackey, Jennifer (2008). *Learning from Words: Testimony as a Source of Knowledge*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. [V]
### Goldberg, Sanford C. (2010). *Relying on Others: An Essay in Epistemology*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. [V]
Lackey: testimonial knowledge depends on the reliability of the *statement*, not the speaker's belief; Goldberg: the hearer's justification depends on the reliability of the speaker's cognitive processes (extended reliabilism).
**Bearing:** Background. Goldberg's process-reliabilist framing is the closest to the paper's "conditionalise on the process": the hearer's epistemic standing is fixed by the sender's process, which in the design case is reliable. This supports the paper's insistence that the receiver's beliefs are well-founded.

### Guerrero, Alexander (2021). "The Interested Expert Problem and the Epistemology of Juries." *Episteme* 18(3): 428–452. DOI 10.1017/epi.2021.36. [V]
Abstract (verified): introduces "interested experts" (experts with values or preferences about their domain); asks whether beliefs formed on their testimony can be justified or knowledge; focuses on jury service.
**Bearing:** *Supports.* The nearest philosophical treatment of "testimony from an interested party" I found. Guerrero's problem is that all the experts may be interested, so there is no disinterested source; that is the receiver's situation vis-à-vis an industry that is the only party able to run the trials. I did not find a developed "epistemology of advocacy" literature in philosophy journals beyond this; flag as thin.

### Tsai, George (2014). "Rational Persuasion as Paternalism." *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 42(1): 78–112. DOI 10.1111/papa.12026. [V-cit]
Argues that offering someone reasons and evidence can be paternalistic and disrespectful when motivated by distrust of her capacity to reach a good decision on her own, since it intrudes on her deliberative domain; rational persuasion is not automatically respectful just because it works through reason. (Abstract not retrievable; summary from knowledge of the paper.)
**Bearing:** *Supports the structural point that reason-mediated influence can wrong its target.* Tsai establishes a wrong (paternalism) that survives the influence's being overt and reason-giving; the paper establishes a different one (exploitation) with the same structure. Cite Tsai to pre-empt "but it works through reasons, so it can't be a wrong."

### Levy, Neil (2019). "Nudge, Nudge, Wink, Wink: Nudging Is Giving Reasons." *Ergo* 6(10). DOI 10.3998/ergo.12405314.0006.010. [V]
Abstract (verified): well-designed nudges "present reasons to mechanisms designed to respond to reasons" (e.g. defaults carry implicit recommendations, a form of testimony), so nudging does not bypass rational agency; withholding good nudges undermines autonomy.
**Bearing:** *Complicates, in the paper's favour.* Levy shows that "works through reasons" has been used to *exonerate* an influence (nudging) from the manipulation charge. The paper accepts the exoneration for design and then argues that this leaves the exploitation charge untouched; Levy is the foil showing that the reason-giving defence is not the end of the moral story.

### Blumenthal-Barby, J.S. & Burroughs, Hadley (2012). "Seeking Better Health Care Outcomes: The Ethics of Using the 'Nudge'." *American Journal of Bioethics* 12(2): 1–10. DOI 10.1080/15265161.2011.634481. [V]
Taxonomy of nudges (defaults, framing, salience, incentives, social norms) and the ethical questions each raises (autonomy, manipulation, transparency, welfare).
**Bearing:** Background; framing and salience nudges are informational influences that are overt and true, the closest everyday analogue to design.

## Summary of what must be answered, in priority order

1. **Gorin (2014 APQ) and Klenk (2022)**: reason-mediated, overt, true-reason influence can be manipulation if the influencer is indifferent to justification (Gorin) or to whether the method reveals reasons (Klenk). Klenk's formulation lets design out cleanly (the method reveals all existing reasons); Gorin's needs the reply that the designer is indifferent to the *decision*, not to justification of the *belief*.
2. **Fallis (2015) and O'Connor & Freeborn (2025)**: true content that misleads is disinformation / misleading content. The paper must argue that against a process-conditioning receiver, design produces no misleading (no false belief, no raised credence in a falsehood), so the "misleading content" framing is wrong and exploitation is the right one.
3. **Stokke (2016)**: the QUD account of misleading with partial truths. The paper needs to say why a self-servingly chosen experiment is not a "partial answer inviting a false completion."
4. **Zwolinski (2007) / the nonworseness claim**: the receiver consents and benefits. Reply via Wertheimer, Valdman, Vrousalis, Kates.
5. **Barnhill (2014)**: the "not in her self-interest" clause. Reply: her mental states are ideal; only her decision is worse.

## Where the paper's claim appears to be novel
- No philosophy-of-science paper I found distinguishes selection from design *as such* and argues that transparency reforms neutralise only the former; Holman & Bruner 2017, O'Connor & Freeborn 2025, Pinto & Pinto 2023 and Elliott 2011 each treat a design-type case, but frame the harm as bias or misleading content, not as exploitation, and none has a process-conditioning receiver.
- The Mohseni et al. (2022) and Weatherall et al. (2020) receivers are verified naive-space conditionalisers (Bayes on content as an exchangeable sample); this should be stated in the paper with the page/section references above.
- No philosophical account of manipulation surveyed treats "leaving beliefs calibrated while moving decisions" as its target; the exploitation literature (Wood, Valdman, Vrousalis) is where the paper's diagnosis has precedent.
