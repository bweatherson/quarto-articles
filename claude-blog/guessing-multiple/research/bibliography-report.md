## 1. Annotated bibliography

Verification key: **[CR]** means I confirmed journal, volume, issue, pages, year and DOI against the Crossref API. **[PUB]** means I confirmed it only on a publisher or repository page. **[UNVERIFIED]** means I could not confirm the publication. Where the online-first and print years differ, both are given.

### A. The epistemology of guessing (core)

1. **Dorst, Kevin & Matthew Mandelkern (2022). "Good Guesses." *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 105(3): 581–618 (online 2021). DOI 10.1111/phpr.12831.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/DORGG
   - **Thesis:** A guess is an answer to a question (a partition) that trades accuracy against informativity. A guess p maximises P(p)·J^(number of cells p excludes) for some J ≥ 1. The model predicts several patterns: Improbable Guessing, Question-Sensitivity, Optionality, Filtering and Fit.
   - **Applications:** belief is tied to one's best guess relative to a question; assertion follows a "Say Your Guess" norm (when the question under discussion (QUD) is Q, communicate your best guess about Q); the conjunction fallacy arises because people rank answers by expected answer-value rather than by probability.
   - **[MULTI]** Optionality makes several guesses of different specificity permissible (unions of k cells). Guesses are explicitly question-relative: "Yale" to *where*, "not Yale" to *whether Yale*. The model ranks all answers. It does not treat sequences of guesses, "guess again", or whether guesses must be consistent across questions.

2. **Holguín, Ben (2022). "Thinking, Guessing, and Believing." *Philosophers' Imprint* 22. DOI 10.3998/phimp.2123.** [CR; Crossref gives issue 1, the author's CV gives 22(6):1–25] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/HOLTGA-2
   - **Thesis:** To think p is to guess that p answers the question under consideration. Rational thinking is a non-arbitrary guess. "Thinks" is question- and context-sensitive. One can rationally think p with very low confidence. Rational thinking is closed under entailment and is not fixed by credence. Often what one thinks is literally a choice. All of this carries over to belief.
   - I could not read the full text; this summary comes from the abstract plus Teague's and Jovićević's discussions.
   - **[MULTI]** Relativising thinking to questions lets an agent think different things relative to different questions. The "choice among ties" claim is an optionality thesis: Jovićević reads it as letting one believe either heads or tails.

3. **Goodman, Jeremy & Ben Holguín (2023). "Thinking and Being Sure." *PPR* 106(3): 634–654 (online 2022). DOI 10.1111/phpr.12876.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/GOOTAB-5
   - Distinguishes weak, guess-like *thinking* from *being sure*. The two have different normative links to assertion, inquiry and decision. There is no theoretically significant "full belief" between them.

4. **Skipper, Mattias (2023). "Good guesses as accuracy-specificity tradeoffs." *Philosophical Studies* 180(7): 2025–2050. DOI 10.1007/s11098-022-01915-1.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/SKIGGA
   - Accepts the accuracy/specificity framing. Argues that Dorst & Mandelkern's measure violates Neutrality (on a flat distribution all guesses should be equally good) and Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives for guessing (whether p beats q should not depend on the size of the question, e.g. on splitting an "Other" cell).
   - Proposes a log-ratio specificity measure, at the cost of full Optionality.
   - **[MULTI] (partial)** The independence principle governs how comparisons between guesses survive when the question is refined. That bears on re-ranking options as a conversation reshapes the question. The paper has no separate or sequential guesses.

5. **Linnemann, Niels & Feraz Azhar (2025). "Better guesses." *PPR* 110(2): 661–686 (online 2024). DOI 10.1111/phpr.13118.** [CR] OA preprint ("Better permissible guesses"): https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/23928
   - Offers a minimal account without epistemic utility theory: the permissible guesses are the Pareto-optimal accuracy/specificity trade-offs. It reproduces Dorst & Mandelkern's patterns and conjunction-fallacy predictions.
   - Focuses on how guesses improve as credences sharpen, illustrated with Fermi problems.
   - **[MULTI]** The Pareto front gives a *set* of permissible guesses (one per specificity level). Fermi problems split a question into sub-questions whose guesses are combined into an overall guess, with discussion of how errors propagate or cancel.

6. **Horowitz, Sophie (2019). "Accuracy and Educated Guesses." *Oxford Studies in Epistemology* 6: 85–113. DOI 10.1093/oso/9780198833314.003.0004.** [CR] Record: https://philpapers.org/rec/HORAAE
   - Credences are accurate insofar as they license true educated guesses on forced-choice questions. The account is compatible with immodesty and can justify probabilism.
   - **[MULTI]** Accuracy belongs to the whole profile of guesses a credence licenses across the family of binary questions, not to any single guess.

7. **Horowitz, Sophie (2025). "Do credences model guesses?" *Noûs* 59(2): 409–424 (online 2024). DOI 10.1111/nous.12519.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/HORDCM
   - Proposes that credences are models of our dispositions to guess, as an alternative to primitivist, betting, comparative and full-belief views. Asks whether we should accept this.
   - **[MULTI]** On this view a credence encodes guessing dispositions across many questions. This is my inference from the abstract and the book excerpt.

8. **Horowitz, Sophie. *Guesswork: Accuracy for Uncertain Believers*. Princeton UP, forthcoming (PUP lists 13 April 2027; ISBN 9780691277660).** [PUB; no DOI] Excerpt (chs. 3–4, prepared for "MEW 2026"): https://philosophy.osu.edu/sites/default/files/2026-08/Guesswork.pdf
   - A book-length theory of accuracy for credences based on guessing. Tests it against Truth-Directedness and Immodesty, and argues it is simpler than epistemic utility theory.
   - **[MULTI]** The excerpts show differences between credences (0.8 vs 0.6) only across many questions. They use a repeated "coin game" scored per correct guess, and a quiz analogy (answer every question because you don't know which are graded). Conditional credences are assessed by suppositional questions that count only if the supposition is true, which is effectively a theory of conditional guesses.

9. **Builes, David, Sophie Horowitz & Miriam Schoenfield (2022). "Dilating and contracting arbitrarily." *Noûs* 56(1): 3–20 (online 2020). DOI 10.1111/nous.12338.** [CR]
   - Uses Horowitz's guessing framework for imprecise credences. Moving arbitrarily from precise to imprecise credence is irrational; the reverse can be rational.

10. **Teague, Richard (2024). "A puzzle about guessing and inquiry." *Analysis* 84(2): 327–336. DOI 10.1093/analys/anad057.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/TEAAPA
    - **The puzzle:** a best guess should track likelihoods, yet rational inquiry needs guesses that cohere. Example (Latif): Yale 38%, Harvard 30%, Stanford 20%, NYU 12%. "Yale" is the best guess to *where*, but "not Yale" (62%) is the likely answer to *whether Yale*.
    - **Responses rejected:** contextualism (Dorst & Mandelkern, Holguín, Schaffer) requires implausible semantic blindness. The paper discusses whole-to-part and part-to-whole closure, a link between wondering and guessing, and "divide and conquer" by sub-questions. It leaves open "take your pick" weak guessing.
    - **[MULTI]** Central: the paper is about whether several guesses made in a single inquiry, across a wh-question and its polar sub-questions, can be jointly coherent.

11. **Fang, Helena (2026). "Guessing and Its Limits." *PPR* 112(3): 563–577 (online Feb 2026). DOI 10.1111/phpr.70101.** [CR] Record: https://philarchive.org/rec/FANGAI-2 (full text was blocked to me)
    - Targets "Guessing": you may believe p iff p is among the most probable answers to a salient question. Introduces an inter-question principle for belief reports in multi-question scenarios and argues Guessing is incompatible with it. The puzzle is intractable and generalises to other question-sensitive accounts.
    - **[MULTI]** Central: the topic is belief/guess reports across several questions in one scenario. I could not see the exact principle.

12. **Jovićević, Andrej (2025). "A semantics for weak, question-sensitive belief." *Synthese* 206(3): art. 165. DOI 10.1007/s11229-025-05233-x.** [CR] OA: https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:5e672ba6-18e9-4d21-9fdb-ef12906704c5. Short version in *Proceedings of the 24th Amsterdam Colloquium* (2024): 188–195, DOI 10.53437/vwtz6412 [CR]
    - An epistemic logic over "situations": a world plus evidence, where evidence includes live possibilities, a probability and a QUD. You believe p iff p is implied by the most informative probabilistically dominant answer. Compared with Holguín and Dorst & Mandelkern; rejects Holguín-style optionality. Also tests the knowledge–belief principles of KD45.
    - **[MULTI]** Studies what happens to belief when the QUD shifts:
      - Preservation under refining or coarsening the question fails in general. In the urn case, "blue" is believed relative to one question and "non-blue" relative to another; restricted versions hold.
      - Closure under conjunction holds within a situation but deliberately fails across questions.
      - Updating is treated only for information *consistent* with current beliefs. The belief-contravening case, e.g. learning your guess was wrong, is explicitly set aside.

13. **Pearson, Joshua Edward (2025). "A puzzle about weak belief." *Analysis* 85(2): 405–415 (online 2024). DOI 10.1093/analys/anae018.** [CR]
    - Weak-belief theorists either cannot apply their own methodology consistently to beliefs in conditionals, or must deny that conditional beliefs can be used in reasoning.
    - Not MULTI as such. My own observation: a "second guess" ("if not A, then B") is a conditional guess, so this puzzle may transfer.

14. **Beddor, Bob (2025). "Unsettled belief." *Philosophical Quarterly* 75(3): 834–857 (online 2024). DOI 10.1093/pq/pqae037.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/BEDUBG
    - Ordinary ascriptions of unsettled belief are common, which tells against the view that believing p disposes you to take p for granted. Draws consequences for the relation of belief to credence.

### B. Weak belief, "think", and question-sensitive belief

15. **Hawthorne, John, Daniel Rothschild & Levi Spectre (2016). "Belief is weak." *Philosophical Studies* 173(5): 1393–1404 (online 2015). DOI 10.1007/s11098-015-0553-7.** [CR]
    - Ordinary "believe"/"think" pick out an attitude compatible with modest credence. The evidence is neg-raising and weak upper bounds. This is the origin point of the guessing literature.

16. **Rothschild, Daniel (2020). "What it takes to believe." *Philosophical Studies* 177(5): 1345–1362 (online 2019). DOI 10.1007/s11098-019-01256-6.** [CR] OA via UCL Discovery
    - Linguistic evidence suggests believing p requires only thinking p likely. Rejects a homogeneity-based "strong" rival view.

17. **Clarke, Roger (2024). "Strong Belief is Ordinary." *Episteme* 21(3): 773–793 (online 2022). DOI 10.1017/epi.2022.42.** [CR]
    - Other ordinary belief idioms fail the weakness tests, so the ordinary concept of belief is not weak. Closes with problems for belief-as-guessing accounts (Dorst, Mandelkern, Holguín).

18. **Schiller, Henry Ian (2023). "What's your Opinion? Negation and 'Weak' Attitude Verbs." *Philosophical Quarterly* 73(4): 1141–1161. DOI 10.1093/pq/pqad049.** [CR]
    - "Weak" attitude verbs ascribe an *opinion* about a subject matter: a selection from among its elements, responsive to the conversational topic. This is close to a guess.

19. **Koev, Todor, Cory Bill & Maryam Mohammadi (2021). "'Believe' is Strong but Subjective: Experimental Evidence from Hedging." *Proceedings of Sinn und Bedeutung* 25: 497–514. DOI 10.18148/sub/2021.v25i0.950.** [PUB; not in Crossref] https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/sub/index.php/sub/article/view/950
    - Experiments on hedging uses of "believe" favour a strong-but-subjective semantics over a weak ("likely") one.

20. **Staffel, Julia (2025). "Are there transitional beliefs? – I think so?" *PPR* 110(3): 1114–1136. DOI 10.1111/phpr.70006.** [CR]
    - It can be rational to believe an answer to Q before concluding deliberation about Q. Such "transitional beliefs" are relevant to provisional guesses made mid-inquiry.

21. **Yalcin, Seth (2018). "Belief as Question-Sensitive." *PPR* 97(1): 23–47 (online 2016). DOI 10.1111/phpr.12330.** [CR]
    - The foundational question-sensitive model of belief.

22. **Hoek, Daniel. "Minimal Rationality and the Web of Questions." In *Unstructured Content* (OUP), 3–41. DOI 10.1093/9780191862175.003.0001.** [CR; Crossref dates it 2025, the literature cites it as 2022]
    - Models belief states as a web of questions and answers.

23. **Kipper, Jens, Alexander W. Kocurek & Zeynep Soysal (2025). "Idle Questions." *Journal of Philosophy* 122(4): 160–176. DOI 10.5840/jphil20244221.** [CR] Record: https://philarchive.org/rec/KIPIQN
    - Argues that Hoek's question-sensitive account reintroduces logical-omniscience problems and ties belief to action too rigidly. Sketches a generalisation.

24. **Hong, Frank (2023). "Prefaces, Knowledge, and Questions." *Ergo* 10. DOI 10.3998/ergo.4631.** [CR]
    - "Socraticism": in any context we know very little. Defended with a question-sensitive contextualist model.
    - **[MULTI] (partial)** The preface setting concerns many claims across questions and whether they can be held jointly.

25. **Teague, Richard (2022). "The problem of closure and questioning attitudes." *Synthese* 200(5): art. 345. DOI 10.1007/s11229-022-03826-4.** [CR]
    - The closure problem recurs for wondering and similar attitudes, where question-sensitivity is a weaker remedy. Background for Teague (2024).

### C. Guesses and related speech acts: weak assertion, hedges, conjecture, prediction, exam questions

26. **Mandelkern, Matthew & Kevin Dorst (2022). "Assertion is weak." *Philosophers' Imprint* 22 (no. 19 per the author's CV). DOI 10.3998/phimp.1076.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/MANAIW
    - Assertion is as weak as belief. The norm is to say the strongest thing you believe about the QUD; asserting is guessing.
    - **[MULTI] (partial)** Discusses several speakers asserting competing answers to one question, and "I don't know" followed by a best guess as a separate speech act. The ban on admitting uncertainty is local to a single speech act. Retraction is not discussed.

27. **van Elswyk, Peter & Matthew A. Benton (2023). "Assertion remains strong." *Philosophical Studies* 180(1): 27–50 (online 2022). DOI 10.1007/s11098-022-01871-w.** [CR]
    - Reply defending strong assertion norms against Mandelkern & Dorst. No abstract was available to me.

28. **Ninan, Dilip (2026). "The Strength of Assertion." *Analytic Philosophy* 67(2): 182–191 (online 2025). DOI 10.1111/phib.12375.** [CR]
    - Distinguishes two empirically separable classes of declarative utterance. They differ in what they add to the common ground, how they are elicited, and which attitude reports they license. Whether "the norm of assertion" is weak is largely terminological.
    - Directly relevant to guesses that are elicited versus volunteered.

29. **Benton, Matthew A. & Peter van Elswyk (2020). "Hedged Assertion." In S. Goldberg (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of Assertion*, 243–263. DOI 10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190675233.013.11.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/BENHA
    - Sets out the structural, functional and taxonomic questions about hedged assertion.

30. **van Elswyk, Peter (2023). "Hedged testimony." *Noûs* 57(2): 341–369 (online 2022). DOI 10.1111/nous.12411.** [CR]
    - Hedging changes both the hearer's warrant and the speaker's responsibility.

31. **van Elswyk, Peter (2024). "Hedging in Discourse." *Synthese* 204(3): art. 98. DOI 10.1007/s11229-024-04733-6.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/VANHID
    - A "positional" theory: whether an epistemic term hedges depends on how its declarative sits within the surrounding discourse.

32. **van Elswyk, Peter & Christopher Willard-Kyle (2025). "Hedging and the Norm of Belief." *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 103(3): 718–739. DOI 10.1080/00048402.2024.2379258.** [CR] OA: https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/296836
    - Because "I believe" is used to hedge, knowledge is not the norm of belief.

33. **Sapir, Yasha & Peter van Elswyk (2021). "Hedging and the ignorance norm on inquiry." *Synthese* 199(3–4): 5837–5859. DOI 10.1007/s11229-021-03048-0.** [CR; Crossref lists van Elswyk first]
    - Any position weaker than knowledge, including a hedged guess, is compatible with continuing to inquire into the question.

34. **Incurvati, Luca & Julian J. Schlöder (2019). "Weak Assertion." *Philosophical Quarterly* 69(277): 741–770. DOI 10.1093/pq/pqz016.** [CR] OA: https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/43249266/incurvati_schloeder2019.pdf
    - Argues for a speech act of weak assertion and uses it to give an inferentialist account of "might".

35. **Simion, Mona (2022). "The epistemic normativity of conjecture." *Philosophical Studies* 179(11): 3447–3471. DOI 10.1007/s11098-022-01829-y.** [CR] OA: https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/273781
    - Conjecturing p is permissible only if you know you have some warrant for p, but not enough to believe it. Conjecture is therefore more demanding than assertion. Earlier version in *Shifty Speech and Independent Thought* (OUP 2021).

36. **Gaszczyk, Grzegorz (2023). "Norms of Constatives." *Acta Analytica* 38(3): 517–536 (online 2022). DOI 10.1007/s12136-022-00531-2.** [CR]
    - Against Simion: not all constatives (predictives, suggestives, etc.) are species of assertion. Proposes five tests for assertion.

37. **Gaszczyk, Grzegorz (2022). "Norms of Speech Acts." *Studia Semiotyczne* 36(2): 11–45. DOI 10.26333/sts.xxxvi2.02.** [PUB; not in Crossref]
    - Taxonomy of speech-act norms. Cites Dorst & Mandelkern and Holguín.

38. **Gaszczyk, Grzegorz (2025). "Interrogatives, inquiries, and exam questions." *Erkenntnis* 90(4): 1437–1456 (online 2023). DOI 10.1007/s10670-023-00758-4.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/GASIIA-2
    - "Exam questions" are a distinct speech act: the asker has access to the answer and does not officially know whether the hearer does. Guesses appear only as an example of a non-assertoric declarative.
    - This is the asker's side of a guessing game.

39. **Cariani, Fabrizio (2020). "On Predicting." *Ergo* 7. DOI 10.3998/ergo.12405314.0007.011.** [CR]
    - Prediction as a speech act defined by a future "time of discovery" and constitutive norms. Cited by Dorst & Mandelkern.

40. **Santorio, Paolo. "Guesses and predictions."** Work-in-progress talk, University of Maryland, 17 April 2024 (https://philosophy.umd.edu/node/3256). **[UNVERIFIED as a publication; no paper found]**
    - Guessing p is choosing p among salient alternatives; predicting is permissible only when no salient non-p options are in view.

### D. Belief dynamics relevant to "guess again" (revising after feedback)

41. **Goodman, Jeremy & Bernhard Salow (2025). "Belief Revision Normalized." *Journal of Philosophical Logic* 54(1): 1–49 (online 2024). DOI 10.1007/s10992-024-09769-0.** [CR] Also "Belief Revision from Probability," TARK 2023, *EPTCS* 379: 308–317, DOI 10.4204/EPTCS.379.25 [CR]
    - Rational belief rules out possibilities that are much less normal, with normality fixed by probabilities plus a contextual question. On this view the logic of belief revision is much weaker than AGM.
    - Directly relevant to how one's next guess should change after feedback.

42. **Pearson, Joshua Edward (2025). "Belief revision revised." *PPR* 111(2): 696–727. DOI 10.1111/phpr.70037.** [CR] See also his MIT PhD thesis *Belief is Messy* (2024), https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/155481 [PUB]
    - Counterexample to the "Anticipation" principle: some justified beliefs must be dropped after a single observation. New theory based on ruling out sufficiently improbable possibilities.

### E. Virtue-theoretic and experimental work on the norms of guessing

43. **Sosa, Ernest (2015). *Judgment and Agency*, ch. 3, 65–88. DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198719694.003.0003** [CR]; and **Sosa (2021). *Epistemic Explanations*. DOI 10.1093/oso/9780198856467.001.0001** [CR]
    - Judgment is distinguished from mere guessing as an alethic affirmation aimed at aptness, not just correctness.

44. **Carter, J. Adam (2020). "Sosa on knowledge, judgment and guessing." *Synthese* 197(12): 5117–5136 (online 2016). DOI 10.1007/s11229-016-1181-2.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/CARSOK
    - Diagnoses a flawed picture of guessing in Sosa's "eye exam" case. Offers an alternative account of guessing as a cognitive performance.

45. **Peacocke, Antonia. "A simpler model of judgment: on Sosa's *Epistemic Explanations*." *Philosophical Studies* (online 28 Nov 2024; volume not yet assigned). DOI 10.1007/s11098-024-02232-5.** [CR] OA: https://philarchive.org/rec/PEAASM
    - Rejects Sosa's argument that his richer model is needed to separate judgment from guessing.

46. **Roberts, Pendaran, James Andow & Kelly Ann Schmidtke (2018). "Lay intuitions about epistemic normativity." *Synthese* 195(7): 3267–3287 (online 2017). DOI 10.1007/s11229-017-1371-6.** [CR]
    - Experiments on the permissibility of guesses, hypotheses and beliefs. Supports a two-factor (consequentialist plus non-consequentialist) model.

### F. Cognitive science and linguistics of guesses

47. **Quillien, Tadeg & Christopher G. Lucas (2022). "The logic of guesses: how people communicate probabilistic information." *Proceedings of CogSci* 44: 2876–2882.** [PUB] https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4sm5s4r4
    - A guess is a compressed message about a distribution, scored information-theoretically.

48. **Quillien, Tadeg, Neil R. Bramley & Christopher G. Lucas (2025). "Lossy encoding of distributions in judgment under uncertainty." *Cognitive Psychology* 159: 101745. DOI 10.1016/j.cogpsych.2025.101745.** [CR] OA: https://www.bramleylab.ppls.ed.ac.uk/pdfs/quillien2025lossy.pdf. Blog summary contrasting this with Dorst & Mandelkern: https://xphi.net/?p=995
    - Guesses are judged by how well a listener can reconstruct the speaker's distribution ("distribution-encoding"), not only by probability of being correct. Four experiments on making, interpreting and rating set-valued and interval guesses. Bears on the conjunction fallacy.
    - **[MULTI]** Studies multi-option (disjunctive) guesses and listeners decoding guesses in an imagined conversation. It does not address ranked lists or repeated guessing.

49. **Chung, WooJin, Kevin Dorst, Matthew Mandelkern & Salvador Mascarenhas (2025). "The conjunction fallacy: confirmation or relevance?" *Thinking & Reasoning* 31(1): 82–108 (online 2024). DOI 10.1080/13546783.2024.2374545.** [CR] Preprint: DOI 10.31234/osf.io/r7ab3
    - Tests whether relevance to the QUD, rather than degree of confirmation, drives the conjunction fallacy.
    - **[MULTI] (partial)** Concerns how people rank several candidate answers.

50. **Égré, Paul, Benjamin Spector, Adèle Mortier & Steven Verheyen (2023). "On the optimality of vagueness: 'around', 'between' and the Gricean maxims." *Linguistics and Philosophy* 46(5): 1075–1130. DOI 10.1007/s10988-022-09379-6.** [CR]
    - The semantics and pragmatics of interval and approximate answers, i.e. interval guesses. Cited by Quillien et al.

51. **Dorst, Kevin (2024). "Bayesians Commit the Gambler's Fallacy." SSRN preprint, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4683064.** [CR as posted content; journal status UNVERIFIED] https://philarchive.org/rec/DORBCT
    - Rational Bayesians with causal uncertainty and limited memory expect switching after short streaks, reproducing the gambler's fallacy.
    - **[MULTI]** About sequences of binary predictions.

52. **Peng, Peiru & Jianmin Zeng (2024). "Guess What? The Asker–Guesser Discrepancy in Desire for the Right Guess." *Journal of Language and Social Psychology* 43(5–6): 670–690. DOI 10.1177/0261927X241291259.** [CR]
    - Five studies of "guess what" conversations. Askers want correct guesses less than guessers do, and enjoy the conversation more when guesses are wrong. Guessers lower their desire to be right only when reminded of the asker's feelings.
    - **[MULTI]** The only empirical study I found of the conversational dynamics of being asked to guess.

53. **Kalai, Adam Tauman, Ofir Nachum, Santosh Vempala & Edwin Zhang (2025). "Why Language Models Hallucinate." arXiv:2509.04664 (preprint).** [PUB]
    - Right/wrong grading with no credit for abstaining rewards guessing over admitting uncertainty, as in exams. Proposes rescoring benchmarks.

### G. Second guesses, the "inner crowd", and sequential guessing with feedback (empirical and formal)

54. **Gaertig, Celia & Joseph P. Simmons (2021). "The Psychology of Second Guesses: Implications for the Wisdom of the Inner Crowd." *Management Science* 67(9): 5921–5942. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2020.3781.** [CR] OA: https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/37-Gaertig-Simmons-2021-1.pdf
    - Whether averaging two guesses helps depends on how the second guess is generated. First asking whether your guess was too high or too low makes second guesses more extreme: this hurts when the truth is moderate and helps when it is extreme.
    - **[MULTI]** Directly about second guesses.

55. **van Dolder, Dennie & Martijn J. van den Assem (2018). "The wisdom of the inner crowd in three large natural experiments." *Nature Human Behaviour* 2(1): 21–26 (online 2017). DOI 10.1038/s41562-017-0247-6.** [CR] With commentary: **Vul, Edward (2018). "Pulling guesses up by their bootstraps." *Nature Human Behaviour* 2(1): 15–16. DOI 10.1038/s41562-017-0280-5** [CR]
    - **[MULTI]** Field evidence that repeated guesses by one person carry independent information.

56. **McLean, Charlotte S., Bowen Ouyang & Jochen Ditterich (2020). "Second Guessing in Perceptual Decision-Making." *Journal of Neuroscience* 40(26): 5078–5089. DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2787-19.2020.** [CR]
    - After a first choice, subjects say what they would pick if the first were wrong. Second guesses track the evidence-ranking of the unchosen options.
    - **[MULTI]** An empirical model of conditional "guess again" answers.

57. **Diaconis, Persi, Ron Graham & Sam Spiro (2022). "Guessing about Guessing: Practical Strategies for Card Guessing with Feedback." *American Mathematical Monthly* 129(7): 607–622. DOI 10.1080/00029890.2022.2069986.** [CR] arXiv 2012.04019
    - **[MULTI]** In sequential guessing with feedback, greedily guessing the most likely card is not optimal, but simple heuristics come close.

58. **Genin, Konstantin & Kevin T. Kelly (2019). "Theory Choice, Theory Change, and Inductive Truth-Conduciveness." *Studia Logica* 107(5): 949–989 (online 2018). DOI 10.1007/s11225-018-9809-5.** [CR]
    - Formal learning theory: inquiry as a sequence of conjectures, with retractions as a cost.
    - **[MULTI]** A formal model of a series of revisable guesses; I did not read the full text.

## 2. The 2024–2026 items a paper on multiple guesses must engage, in order of importance

1. **Fang 2026.** The inter-question puzzle for Guessing; the closest published target.
2. **Teague 2024.** Coherence of guesses across a wh-question and its polar sub-questions within one inquiry.
3. **Jovićević 2025.** The formal semantics: what survives a QUD shift, conjunction closure within versus across questions, and the explicit gap on belief-contravening updates.
4. **Horowitz 2025 and *Guesswork* (forthcoming; excerpt 2026).** Guessing judged across many forced-choice questions, plus conditional (suppositional) guesses.
5. **Quillien, Bramley & Lucas 2025.** Guesses as messages a listener decodes; set-valued guesses.
6. **Linnemann & Azhar 2025.** A *set* of permissible guesses; Fermi decomposition into sub-guesses.
7. **Ninan 2026** (elicited versus volunteered declaratives), read against Mandelkern & Dorst 2022 and van Elswyk & Benton 2023.
8. **Goodman & Salow 2025 and Pearson 2025 (PPR).** How guesses should be revised after feedback.
9. **Pearson 2025 (Analysis).** Weak belief and conditionals; relevant to "if not A, then B" second guesses.
10. **Chung, Dorst, Mandelkern & Mascarenhas 2025.** Ranking candidate answers by relevance to the QUD.
11. **Beddor 2025 and Staffel 2025.** Unsettled and transitional beliefs, i.e. provisional guesses.
12. **Peng & Zeng 2024.** Empirical data on "guess what" conversations.
13. **van Elswyk 2024 and van Elswyk & Willard-Kyle 2025.** Hedging as a discourse function.
14. **Kipper, Kocurek & Soysal 2025.** Limits of question-sensitive belief.
15. **Kalai et al. 2025.** Grading schemes that reward guessing, the exam case.

## 3. Close antecedents to a paper on making several guesses in one conversation

I found **no philosophy paper whose main topic is the norms or pragmatics of several guesses in one conversation**: sequential guesses on one question, "guess again", ranked guess lists, or guessing games with feedback. The closest antecedents:

- **Teague 2024** comes nearest. It is about several guesses within one inquiry (the "divide and conquer" sub-questions) and their joint coherence. **Fang 2026** is the multi-question counterpart for belief reports.
- **Jovićević 2025** explicitly sets aside updating on information inconsistent with current beliefs. That is exactly the "your guess was wrong, guess again" step, so it is an open gap.
- **Dorst & Mandelkern 2022** supply the components but not the sequence: Optionality, a full ranking of answers by answer-value, and the "Say Your Guess" norm. **Mandelkern & Dorst 2022** note that "I don't know" plus a guess are separate speech acts, and that the ban on admitting uncertainty is local to one speech act.
- **Horowitz** (the book excerpt) is the only formal treatment I found of conditional guesses, via suppositional questions. It also uses a quiz in which every question is answered.
- **Skipper's independence principle** concerns how comparisons between guesses survive refining the question, which matters when feedback reshapes the question mid-conversation.
- **Linnemann & Azhar's Fermi decomposition** treats multiple sub-guesses combined into one answer.
- On the asker's side: **Gaszczyk** (exam questions, where the asker knows the answer, as in Twenty Questions or "guess what") and **Peng & Zeng 2024** (the empirical asker/guesser asymmetry).
- Empirical second-guess work:
  - McLean et al. 2020: conditional second guesses.
  - Gaertig & Simmons 2021; van Dolder & van den Assem 2018: the inner crowd.
  - Older (pre-2015, verified): Herzog & Hertwig, "The Wisdom of Many in One Mind," *Psych Sci* 20(2): 231–237 (2009), DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02271.x; Vul & Pashler, "Measuring the Crowd Within," *Psych Sci* 19(7): 645–647 (2008), DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02136.x; Kruger, Wirtz & Miller, "Counterfactual Thinking and the First Instinct Fallacy," *JPSP* 88(5): 725–735 (2005), DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.88.5.725 (on changing answers).
- Formal treatments of sequential guessing:
  - Diaconis, Graham & Spiro 2022: guessing with feedback; greedy is suboptimal.
  - Massey, "Guessing and entropy," *ISIT 1994*, p. 204, DOI 10.1109/ISIT.1994.394764 [CR]. The expected number of guesses until correct, a natural measure for "guess again" games.
  - Genin & Kelly 2019: sequences of conjectures and the cost of retracting them.
- The accuracy–informativeness trade-off for interval guesses predates the philosophy literature: Yaniv & Foster, *JEP: General* 124(4): 424–432 (1995), DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.124.4.424 [CR]. Exam guessing: Budescu & Bar-Hillel, "To Guess or Not to Guess," *J. Educational Measurement* 30(4): 277–291 (1993), DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1993.tb00427.x [CR].
- Older philosophical items worth checking (verified but **not read**):
  - Mackenzie, Jim, "I guess," *AJP* 65(3): 290–300 (1987), DOI 10.1080/00048408712342951. Mackenzie works on formal dialogue games, so this may treat guessing as a move in dialogue; content unconfirmed.
  - Sorensen, "Knowing, believing, and guessing," *Analysis* 42(4): 212–213 (1982), DOI 10.1093/analys/42.4.212.
- Talks with no paper found: Santorio, "Guesses and predictions" (UMD 2024); M. van Veldhuizen, "How Sherlock Holmes Gets it Right: An Investigation into the Epistemology of Guessing" (UNC Greensboro, 25 March 2025; the event page spells the name "van Veldhuzien"). Neither is verified as a publication.
