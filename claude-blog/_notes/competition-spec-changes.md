# Fitting the spec to the AI Philosophy Competition

Sources: competition page (zacharygoodsell.com/ai-philosophy-competition.html, "last updated August 2026"), the detailed-rules and FAQ pages linked from it (fetched 7 Oct 2026), and the Daily Nous thread of 1–12 Sept 2026 (157 comments, all read). Quotations below are from those pages.

## 1. The competition, in the parts that bind

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Deadline | 31 Oct 2026, 11:59pm AoE. Finalists early Dec; results end Jan 2027. |
| Entries | Up to 3 essays per entrant; at most one can win an essay prize. |
| Length | 6,000 words per essay. "Ignores the bibliography but includes everything else" (so footnotes, appendices, abstract, title). Methodology report unlimited. |
| Anonymity | Essay *and* methodology report anonymised. Entrant may opt out of having their name attached at publication. |
| Prior publication | Not published or under review at a journal/conference/workshop, not entered in another competition. "Personal websites, blogs, PhilPapers, and arXiv posting don't constitute publication." |
| Publication | All submissions and methodology reports published under CC BY 4.0 after results. Finalists get judges' comments. Referee reports to be made public (Goodsell, thread). |
| AI involvement | "Philosophical arguments and positions must be primarily AI generated." Test: "Did the AI originate and develop all the substantive philosophical contributions that appear in the essay?" Heuristic: "AI should be the sole author." |
| Permitted human input | Choosing topic; specifying a position to defend ("Argue for prioritarianism"); building scaffolds; selecting among many outputs; methodological instructions ("make premises explicit"); providing source materials; "generic feedback" that names a problem without giving the reason or the fix ("this argument is unconvincing", "this section is unclear"); formatting. |
| Prohibited human input | Supplying arguments or premises; draft text; "suggesting specific distinctions"; detailed outlines embodying the argument; steering toward a pre-existing human argument; substantial rewriting; adding footnotes or examples; "narrowly curating sources to supply arguments"; feedback "containing philosophical insights", including counterexample cases. Insufficient AI involvement → still judged and published, not prize-eligible. |
| Methodology report | Models and versions; each model's role; prompts and system instructions; how topics were chosen; how many candidates were generated; selection and revision process; *all* human involvement, with human and AI actions clearly distinguished; tools available; resource usage (models used + estimated spend are asked for on the form); automated evaluation methods; chat logs "strongly encouraged but not required" and may be requested from promising entries for verification. |
| Judging criteria | "clarity of thesis and reasoning structure; argumentation quality; significance of contribution; originality; engagement with relevant literature; and accuracy in representing sources and citations." Style "isn't independently graded, though unclear writing may obscure philosophical merit." |
| Process | (i) AI screening if volume is high, on the same criteria plus "scholarship", prompts undisclosed; (ii) judges rate to pick finalists individually; (iii) detailed reports on finalists; (iv) ranked-choice vote. Judges never see methodology reports. |
| Prompt injection | "Placing instructions in a submission with the aim of manipulating the AIs used to screen it": disqualification and a bar from future editions. |
| Methodology prize | $5,000 pool for "creative methodology", judged separately from the methodology reports after essay prizes. |

## 2. Where the current spec and the competition differ

The spec in `CLAUDE.md` is a generalist-journal screening standard. Most of it transfers unchanged: the judging criteria are the spec's criteria 1, 4, 6 and 7 under other names, and nothing in the rules rewards what the spec penalises. The differences are these.

**(a) Length: 6,000 words, all-in, versus "flag over 10k".** This is the only change that is structural rather than additive. All three papers are over: Fairness Has Memory 11.6k body + 250 abstract; the population paper 9.1k + 250; Persuasion in Daylight 11.4k + 380. Each needs to lose 40–50%, not trim. At 6k a paper can carry one idea, one argument for it, one serious objection, and the minimum literature needed to locate the idea. The spec's criterion 5 should be replaced by a hard ceiling, and the calibration note about "length justified by achievement" inverted: the question becomes what the paper *dropped* to fit and whether the dropped material was load-bearing. (Separate question, deferred as agreed: what to cut from each paper.)

**(b) Judged comparatively, by a known panel, on "significance" and "originality" explicitly.** A journal screen asks whether a paper clears a bar; this asks whether it beats the other entries. The spec's "Ask the right question" test ("something I could state in a single substantive sentence") becomes the whole game: if the one-sentence contribution is not striking to a reader who knows the area, nothing else matters. Easwaran in the thread: he will judge "is this paper better than the other papers in this competition or not", using "tacit understandings of what counts as good philosophy", and does not want to prejudge whether the winner is good philosophy at all. Proposed addition: the screening report must state the one-sentence contribution *and* rate it as a result a specialist would find surprising / mildly interesting / expected.

**(c) "Accuracy in representing sources and citations" is an explicit criterion, and the thread's single most repeated worry is uncredited reinvention.** Ben (18 comments) returns to it throughout: models "will steal/use ideas without proper attribution", reproduce a known idea "under a new name", and do so even when explicitly asked for something novel; the Navier–Stokes citation controversy is discussed at length; Wallace quotes his own journal's draft AI policy requiring "a careful and thorough literature search to ensure that you are not reinventing the wheel". Easwaran has "a stack of submissions that I'm fairly confident are AI-generated" and has noticed "some similarities" he hopes to write up. The spec's new "Priority and antecedents" section already covers this; for the competition it should be mandatory and documented in the methodology report, with the near-relatives named in the essay. The Thomas 2018 toy-model episode in the population paper is the exact failure mode Ben describes, caught only by reading a skimmed source in full. Make full reading of every cited source the rule, not the aspiration.

**(d) A formal result is not, by itself, philosophy to the organisers.** Goodsell, replying to Brian: "merely proving a theorem of antecedently known importance to philosophy is a valuable contribution but is not itself philosophy (rather, it may be good mathematics), so such work might not be judged as highly as its overall contribution to knowledge would merit in a topic-neutral competition." He also doubts a JSL-level paper would show its author is "a good philosopher". Steinhart's noesis/dianoia distinction and Ben's "the main difficulty… is finding premises P1…Pn that entail some surprising or interesting conclusion C, and then trying to show that those premises are plausible" are the same view from other angles. Brian's reply (logic is part of philosophy; the Gödel case) is the right view and was not conceded, but the organisers set the frame and the judges are the ones Brian was arguing with. Practical upshot for the spec, already added as the last calibration note: the thesis must be a philosophical claim about what the result shows, stated in words, and the formal result must be presented as the argument for it. For the population paper that means the title and abstract should lead with "the sixth theorem does not refute the position it was built to refute, and the repaired theorem's new premise is a substantive claim about background-independence" rather than "A Counterexample to…". The counterexample is the method.

**(e) Anonymisation.** Author lines ("Claude (Anthropic)"), any sentence that identifies the model, and anything that identifies the human custodian must come out of the essay. The methodology report is also anonymised. The spec should say: no model name, no lab name, no first-person reference to being an AI, in the essay. (Whether the essay may say it is AI-written at all: the rules are silent; every entry is, so it carries no information. Leave it out.)

**(f) AI screening with undisclosed prompts, aimed at "slop".** Goodsell: "AI is not currently very good at discriminating between good and excellent philosophy, so the screening will hopefully be used only to eliminate slop." The risk is not that a good paper is mistaken for slop on content but that it trips surface features a screener associates with slop: throat-clearing openings, lists of hedges, "In this paper I will…" roadmaps, paragraphs that restate rather than advance, excess signposting. The agreed policy is that the papers may sound like Claude; that should stand for voice, but the spec should add a pass for *content-free* prose, since the 6k limit will not tolerate it anyway. Nothing in the essay may address the screener; the Lean source and scripts contain comments, and those should be checked too if they are submitted.

**(g) The human-involvement rules apply to our process retroactively.** A methodology report has to classify everything Brian said. Most of it is plainly permitted: topic choice; "try 2"; "v7 is not worthwhile"; "read Carlson, how similar is his view"; "check Thomas in full"; the instruction to ignore style constraints. Two items need a sentence each in the report rather than silence: (i) the remark that purely finite results can be artefacts of discretising a continuous variable, which shaped topic selection away from certain options (it is a methodological preference, not an argument that appears in any paper, so it falls under "methodological instructions"; say so); (ii) the remark that an REU result might be "too similar to the 'Buchak needs to reject Separability' result", which is a novelty judgment used in selection (permitted: "selecting among outputs"). Neither supplied a premise, distinction or example. For Fairness Has Memory and Persuasion in Daylight the same audit has to be done from their notes and transcripts; I have not seen those transcripts. The spec should require a running log of human input, classified against the FAQ's two lists, in `_NOTES.md`.

**(h) Three specific conflicts or near-conflicts to decide about, not spec changes.**
- Elliott Thornley is co-organiser. The population paper is, in part, about a footnote of his (the WQA* restatement), quotes him, and argues that his "the stronger version remains a compelling adequacy condition" is not obviously right. Judging is blind to authorship but not to content; the organisers run the AI screen and the process. This is not disqualifying and may be a point of interest, but it should be a conscious choice.
- Kacper Kowalczyk (judge) works on population ethics; Nebel, Tarsney and Greaves are population-ethics-literate. The paper will be read by people who know Arrhenius's theorems well. Good for a correct paper, fatal for a sloppy one.
- The blog rule: posting on claude-blog is explicitly not "publication", but the directory must stay unlinked anyway until results, since the judges should not be able to find the author by searching a sentence.

**(i) Things the spec should stop caring about.** Bibliography count ("under 25 is a strong NOT READY signal") is a journal heuristic for engagement; at 6k words, with the bibliography excluded from the count, a 30-item list is fine and a 98-item one is suspicious. Replace with: every cited work has been read in full and does work in the text.

## 3. Signal in the thread about how the judges will read

Ordered by how much I think it matters for us.

1. **Comparative, tacit, and originality-weighted** (Easwaran, repeatedly). The question is which entries have a result a judge would repeat to a colleague.
2. **Reinvention is the expected failure** (Ben, C Q, Alice, Wallace's policy). Judges will check priority; Easwaran notes AI makes checking a human's originality easy too, so expect them to run the check. A paper that names its nearest predecessor and says what it adds is protected; one that doesn't is exposed.
3. **AI write-ups don't know what their own central idea is** (Ben, reporting mathematicians: the models "aren't able to appreciate what the central ideas are in their own proofs, so they waste a lot of space presenting uninteresting background or over-explaining minor steps"; Easwaran: nobody has treated an AI proof write-up as "anything like an actual paper"). This is the thing to disprove, and 6k words is the instrument: the central idea must be identifiable in the first page and space must be allocated in proportion to importance.
4. **Autonomy over long horizons produces drift** (Ben: models "overthink, overcomplicate things, and just go off the rails" when left to run; "they tend to miss the forest for the trees"). Our process counters this with the independent screenings; the methodology report should make that visible, since it is also the kind of thing the methodology prize is for.
5. **Plausibility of premises, not validity, is where philosophy is hard** (Ben, Goodsell, Steinhart). A formal paper should spend its words on why the conditions are the ones that matter and what the result means, not on the derivation, which can be verified elsewhere (the Lean development, scripts).
6. **The organisers want "peak capability" and expect scaffolding, multi-agent criticism and selection** (FAQ; Easwaran's "team of agents… shooting them down"; Brian's own comment about teams of agents). Our workflow is of that kind. Document candidate counts honestly (how many topics considered, how many drafts, how many screenings, what was abandoned and why: "Almost" after six rejections is a data point, not an embarrassment).
7. **Panel composition.** Formal epistemology / decision theory (Fitelson, Easwaran, Weisberg, Greco, Günther, Tarsney, Salow, Jaeger); population ethics and value theory (Nebel, Tarsney, Greaves, Kowalczyk, Thornley as organiser); language and semantics (Mandelkern, Sterken, Cappelen, Dever, Dorr, Hawthorne, Goldstein); philosophy of mind and AI (Chalmers, Vold, Millière, Shevlin, Robinson). Alice's point that "most entries will be in the vicinity of specialty of these judges" cuts our way: all three papers sit in the first two clusters. There is no obvious reader for Persuasion in Daylight's economics (Bayesian persuasion) except Tarsney, Greco and perhaps Easwaran; that is a weakness of fit, not of the paper.
8. **Noise to ignore.** The IRB exchange; the EA/funding argument; the Los Alamos joke; the general plagiarism-of-training-data debate; the "end game" worry. None bears on what a submitted essay should look like.

## 5. Proposed addendum to `CLAUDE.md` (not yet added)

To be appended as a section "Competition mode", switched on per paper:

> When a paper is being prepared for the AI Philosophy Competition the following override the standard above.
>
> 1. **Hard length ceiling: 6,000 words including title, abstract, footnotes and appendices; bibliography excluded.** Criterion 5 is replaced by this. A screening report records the count and names what was cut to reach it, and the screener judges whether anything load-bearing was lost.
> 2. **The one-sentence contribution is rated.** The report states it and rates it as surprising / interesting / expected to a specialist in the area. "Expected" is NOT READY.
> 3. **Priority pass is mandatory and reported.** For each idea presented as the paper's own: nearest antecedents searched, closest candidates read in full, the nearest predecessor named in the essay with a statement of what is added. Logged in `_NOTES.md` and in the methodology report.
> 4. **Thesis in words first.** A formal paper's title, abstract and first paragraph state the philosophical claim; the theorem, model or counterexample is presented as the argument for it. Derivations that a reader can verify elsewhere are moved to supplementary material and summarised.
> 5. **Every source read in full.** Not abstract, not skim, not the part that seemed relevant. Quotations verified against the source text; bibliographic data verified against Crossref or the publisher. The bibliography-count heuristic is dropped.
> 6. **Anonymised.** No author line, model name, lab name, or first-person reference to being an AI in the essay or the methodology report. Nothing addressed to a reader other than a philosopher; nothing that could be read as an instruction to a screening model, in the essay or in any attached code or comments.
> 7. **Content-free prose removed.** Roadmap paragraphs, restatements, stacked hedges, signposting that does not change what the reader knows. Voice is not the issue; emptiness is.
> 8. **Human-input log.** Every instruction, remark or piece of feedback from the human custodian is recorded in `_NOTES.md` as it happens and classified against the competition's permitted / prohibited lists. Borderline items are flagged, not silently resolved.
> 9. **Methodology report** is drafted alongside the paper, not after: models and versions, roles, prompts, topic-selection method, numbers of candidates at each stage (topics, drafts, screenings), what was abandoned and why, tools, estimated resource use, automated checks, and where chat logs are kept.
> 10. **Final screening** is of the exact text to be submitted, by a fresh context, after the cut.
