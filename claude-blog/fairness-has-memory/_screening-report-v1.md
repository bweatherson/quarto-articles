# Screening report: paper-v1

**Paper title:** Fairness has memory (no title line in the file; taken from the thesis statement. First section heading is "Two lotteries".)

**Word count:** 10,300 (excluding references; includes abstract, footnotes, one table, short appendix)

**Thesis summary:** Broome's Division Principle (a divisible good among equal claimants must be divided, not raffled) applies to a *sequence* of indivisible units allocated among overlapping claimants, because the sequence is divisible even when its members are not. It follows that fairness is non-separable across occasions, that fair repeated lotteries must give claimants chances that depend on their history of unsatisfied claims, and that the right memory tracks a claimant's shortfall from her proportional share, pressed at a bounded rate: memoryless lotteries (H-1B) are unfair to the persistently unlucky, and lexical queues and convex point systems (Colorado, Nevada) are unfair to newcomers.

**Decision:** EVALUATE

Reasons for this rather than the neighbours. Not DESK REJECT: the paper starts from a problem that would exist with no literature at all (two real lotteries that treat losers differently), states a thesis of the "X is Y" form, and names a mechanism (Division Principle applied diachronically) that yields a determinate rule; the obvious objections (extinction, ex ante, luck egalitarianism, gaming) are all engaged. Not SCREEN: the third claim rests on a parametrised family of weighted lotteries and a simulation model, and the design section is fair-division/mechanism-design, which is the editor's own territory; the conceptual core is also formal enough (non-separability, apportionment divisor methods) that the assessment should be done in-house. Not REVIEW: there is one objection (temporal fungibility of units, below) that a Broome-literate referee could press to rejection, and the paper's own lead example sits awkwardly with its own Section 5.4, both of which a careful author could have seen. I would not be surprised by a referee recommending rejection on those grounds, so the paper has not earned the straight-to-review call.

## Key Strengths

- **A genuine, one-sentence contribution that is not in the literature.** "Broome's own Division Principle, applied to the sequence rather than the period, entails memory" is new, simple, and derived from premises the paper correctly notes that Broome's critics share. The cake-sliced-and-raffled reductio (Section 3.2) is the kind of argument a generalist reader will remember.
- **The extinction reply is well made.** The three-structure distinction (re-run, interrupted allocation, recurrence) cleanly separates the paper from Vong and Feldblyum Le Blevennec, and the point that "surrogate satisfaction is not entered in the ledger" turns Wasserman's objection to lotteries into an argument for memory. This is the best section.
- **The newcomer objection is taken seriously and answered with a model rather than a hand-wave.** The table's headline finding, that queues and convex point systems transfer expected share from short-tenure to long-tenure claimants (queue: expected share 0.00 at tenure 3, 1.27 at tenure 30), is a substantive and checkable indictment of rules actually in use, and the "complete tenure" framing (McKerlie) is the right unit of comparison.
- **Generalist appeal is real.** The question (does last year's loss bear on this year's chance?) is one any philosopher can see the point of, and the institutional range (visas, hunting tags, kidneys, housing, IVF, the NBA draft) shows the thesis has consequences.

## Key Weaknesses

- **The argument needs, and never states, a premise about temporal fungibility.** The move from "the sequence can be divided" to "fairness requires it be divided" treats a 2029 visa as a portion of the same good as a 2026 visa. Section 3.3 addresses "units not identical" only via Broome's conscription case, where division genuinely shortens each person's burden; rotation of visas does not divide any visa, it sequences whole ones. The paper's own exclusion of the "particular organ available now" case (3.3, with a promise to return to it that Section 4 does not keep) shows the author sees that some goods are not fungible across time, but gives no criterion for which are.
- **The lead example is in the wrong structure.** In H-1B (and housing, and kidneys) winners leave the pool, so w is always 0 for everyone present, shortfall reduces to r/N, and the shortfall rule is a time-weighted lottery. Section 5.4 correctly says that in winners-exit cases the divisible quantity is waiting and the Division Principle favours something queue-like. That undercuts the Section 5.2 charge that queues "track the wrong quantity" precisely in the cases where queues are used, and leaves the reader unsure what the paper recommends for the visa lottery it opened with.
- **Several claims are stronger than their support.** "Proportion is established by the table" rests on one parametrisation (N=10, exit rate 0.1, 40 runs) with no error bars, and the κ=2 row (newcomer chance 0.97, tenure-1 share 0.91) does not show "proportional at every tenure". Footnote 8's "nothing turns on this floor" is wrong: the zero floor is the mechanism by which higher κ penalises newcomers. The "losing on purpose" reply says the shortfall rule is less exposed because share tracks "genuine participation", but the rule as defined in the appendix counts periods present.
- **Length (Criterion 5 flag).** At 10,300 words the paper is over the threshold. The thesis is of the right form and the paper does accomplish a lot, so this is not a default reject, but there is 1,000 words of slack (see notes below).

## Critical Issues for Revision

1. State and defend the fungibility premise explicitly: under what conditions are units allocated in different periods portions of one divisible good? Give the criterion that puts visas and hunting tags on one side and "this organ now" on the other, and say what happens to the argument for goods with steep time-discounting (a job offer that lapses).
2. Sort the examples by structure before running the argument. Repeat-winner cases (tags, permits, chores, drafts) get the units-shortfall rule; winners-exit cases (visas, housing, kidneys) get the waiting argument of 5.4. Then say plainly what the paper recommends for H-1B, and revise the "queues track the wrong quantity" charge so it applies only where it is true.
3. Make the table bear the weight put on it: report standard errors, add at least one other parametrisation (larger N, different exit rate), and derive analytically rather than simulate the newcomer's first-period chance as a function of κ and the floor.
4. Fix the losing-on-purpose reply so it is true of the rule actually defined, or change the rule.
5. Cut to about 9,000 words.

# Notes for the author

## 1. The objection a good referee will press hardest

Here it is in full. Broome's Division Principle says that when equal claimants face a divisible good, the good should be divided among them. You apply it to a sequence of units and conclude that the sequence should be divided, i.e. rotated. But Broome's examples of division (food, conscription) are cases where each claimant ends up with a *fraction* of the good at the *same time*, or, in the conscription case, with a genuinely smaller burden. Rotation does neither. Nobody gets a fraction of a visa; each person gets a whole visa or none, and what rotation distributes is the *order* in which whole units are handed out. Whether ordering whole units counts as dividing a good depends entirely on whether a unit received in period t+k is a portion of the same good as a unit received in period t, from the claimant's point of view. For a cake that keeps, yes. For a job offer with a start date, a hunting season in which one is still fit to climb, a transplant while one is still eligible, or a visa while a sponsor still wants you, the later unit is a different and lesser good, and a claim grounded in present need is not a claim to it. So the referee says: your argument equivocates on "divisible". The sequence is divisible in the allocator's bookkeeping; it is not divisible in the currency of the claims, which is need satisfied now. Broome's transplant case, which you set aside in 3.3, is not an exception to your argument but the general case for time-sensitive goods, and most of your examples are time-sensitive.

Does the paper have the materials to answer this? Partly. Three things in the paper bear on it and none is currently pointed at it. (a) Section 3.3 says claims are "to a visa, not to the 2027 visa", but this is asserted, not argued, and the promise to explain in Section 4 why particular-unit cases differ is not kept; Section 4 discusses the re-run, not the temporal identity of the good. (b) Section 5.4 already has the right answer for the time-sensitive case: when what matters is being served soon, the divisible quantity is *delay*, and fairness in delay is what memory should track. If you generalise this, the thesis survives the objection in a modified form: for fungible-across-time goods, track shortfall in units; for time-sensitive goods, track shortfall in waiting; either way, memory. (c) The "surrogate is a surrogate" argument in Section 4 shows that a chance is not a portion of the good, but it does not show that a later unit *is* a portion of the good, which is what you need. What is missing is an explicit premise about when units across time compose one good, and a sentence about what the argument says when they do not. As it stands, a referee who thinks visas are time-sensitive can grant every step and deny the conclusion for your lead example.

## 2. Section 6: does it get the balance right?

Mostly, and it is the section where the paper's voice is best. The four-bounds structure is exactly what the reach question needs, and "shortfall is a state, not a stack of debts" disposes of the regress in one line, which is the right length. But:

Unnecessary. The Dworkin option-luck paragraph in 6.1 is doing no work the preceding luck-egalitarianism paragraph does not already do; the whole of it is "the claimants did not choose to gamble", which is one sentence. The Anderson sentence is decoration. The lineage paragraph in 6.3 could be halved: say that the practice fixes the claimant, that households and lineages are possible claimants, and that you accept the implication; the rectification paragraph that follows is good and can stay.

Missing, in rough order of importance. (i) *Time-varying claim strength within a claimant.* The reach section is about which histories count, but says nothing about a claimant whose claim strength changes across periods, and your own conclusion says the H-1B wage-weighting "fixes the wrong half", implying the two halves can be combined. They cannot be combined until you define shortfall under unequal, time-varying claims. The apportionment footnote (fn 7) sketches this for fixed unequal claims; a paragraph in Section 6 saying how shortfall is computed when a claimant's claim was weak in 2026 and strong in 2028 is needed, because otherwise the reach of memory "within the claim" is undefined. (ii) *The transition problem.* When a memoryless practice adopts memory, do losses under the old rule count? For H-1B this is the first question any policymaker would ask, and your view gives an answer (yes: the shortfall existed whether or not the rule tracked it) that is worth stating because it is not obvious and because it sharpens the contrast with rectification. (iii) *Same good, different agency.* 6.1 says the reason a housing loss does not count in the visa lottery is that the goods differ, "not that the two agencies are different, though they are". But the corrective claim is described throughout as "addressed to that agency". So which is doing the work? If a hunter is passed over ten years in Arizona's sheep draw and moves to Nevada, the good is the same. Your position seems to be that memory is bound to the agency's own allocation, and you should say so and give the reason (the agency owes a fair division of what *it* is dividing). One paragraph.

None of these should be long. Adding (i) to (iii) and cutting the Dworkin material leaves the section roughly the same length.

## 3. Where the paper's own examples, concessions or numbers undercut it

- **The visa lottery is not in the recurrence structure as you model it.** In your simulation, winners stay in the pool and can win again; that is Nevada sheep, not H-1B. In H-1B, housing and kidneys, winners exit, so no one accumulates a surplus, w=0 for everyone present, and your shortfall rule *is* a time-weighted lottery with weight 1+κr/N. Section 5.4 concedes as much and says the Division Principle applied to waiting recommends equalising it, which is a queue with an attrition adjustment. So for the example the paper opens with and closes on, your view and the queue's differ only in κ, and the charge that queues "track the wrong quantity" (5.2) does not apply. Your intro also lists kidney allocation as a system that "remembers", while 3.3 says the argument "does not apply" to a particular organ available now. Decide which.
- **The table shows the memoryless rule is proportional at every tenure.** Row 1: expected share 1.04, 1.01, 0.98. You say this "as it must be". But it means the memoryless rule commits no proportionality violation in expectation; its only defect is dispersion of realised shortfall. Section 5.2 then says explicitly that under all κ "there is no ex ante cost because symmetry guarantees equal chances whatever the rule". You have thereby conceded the ex ante theorist's premise in Section 5 and left yourself only Section 7.4 to fight on. That is a defensible place to fight, but a reader who reaches 7.4 having been told twice that ex ante nothing distinguishes the rules will feel the ground has shifted.
- **Footnote 8 versus the table.** "Nothing turns on this floor." Without the floor, the incumbents' weights sum to (N−1)+κ×(pool shortfall) ≈ N−1 and the newcomer's chance is 1/N for every κ; with the floor, surplus claimants are clipped at zero, the incumbents' weights sum to more than N−1, and the newcomer's chance falls. That is exactly the 1.00 → 0.97 → 0.55 column. The floor is the mechanism of the paper's third claim. Either own it or replace it with a floor at some small positive value and show the result is robust.
- **The κ=2 row does not show what the text says.** Newcomer chance 0.97 and tenure-1 share 0.91 are not "proportional at every tenure, within simulation error"; the 0.97 is a computed probability, not a noisy estimate. Say κ≈1 answers the newcomer objection completely and κ=2 nearly does.
- **Footnote 9 versus the conclusion.** Fn 9 concedes κ is not uniquely fixed, only that the region between 1 and 2 has the right property. The conclusion says "the memory has a measure". A region is not a measure. Say "a bounded rate" and drop "measure", or argue for a value.
- **"Losing on purpose", third reply.** You say the shortfall rule is less exposed than point systems because "share is a function of genuine participation". In the appendix, r_i is periods present, so the rule counts a speculative registration exactly as a real one, and unlike a memoryless rule it gives a speculative early entrant something to gain (accumulated shortfall). Memory does create a new incentive here; the first reply's "raises the stakes of the failure without creating it" is not quite right either.

## 4. Claims that are false, overstated or under-supported

- Intro, para 3: "Philosophy has almost nothing to say about this disagreement." The queue-versus-lottery literature you cite (John and Millum 2020; Hersch and Rowe 2024) is about whether waiting, which is history, should count, and Elster 1989 discusses rotation. Say the literature has not connected the question to the theory of fair claims, which is true.
- 3.2, "the failure of separability", with fn 6. "Member by member equally fair ... in each period, each claimant has probability 1/N under both rules." At period t, under rotation, the claimant who is up has chance 1 given the history, and the standard theory evaluates the lottery when it is held. The unconditional 1/N is available only from the standpoint of the start of the practice, which is the ex ante standpoint you reject in 7.4. The non-separability result survives (either fair members make an unfair sequence, or unfair members make a fair one), but you should state it in a way that does not depend on the unconditional reading, or a referee will say the argument begs the question against ex ante evaluation.
- 3.2: "Broome's own theory of goodness is built around separability assumptions of just this kind." Separability in *Weighing Goods* is separability of the betterness relation across people, times and states; what you have is non-additivity of a comparative pattern across occasions. The analogy is suggestive but "of just this kind" is too strong.
- 5.3: "So the third claim, Proportion, is established by the table." One N, one exit rate, 40 runs, no standard errors; the memoryless row's 1.04 shows about 4 per cent noise. The queue result is analytic and general; the κ results are not, and the sentence should distinguish them.
- 5.3: "These rules answer the newcomer objection completely." True at κ=1 on your numbers; not at κ=2.
- 7.2: Spiekermann "is in fact committed to non-separability" on the strength of a footnote stipulating that a fixed-outcome procedure must not be reused. His point is about a procedure whose outcome is *determined*, so reuse is not a lottery at all; that is not obviously a concession that the fairness of a genuine lottery at t depends on t−1. "The thin end" is fair; "committed" is not.
- 3.3, last para: "the Webster method (Sainte-Laguë) is the one that is unbiased between large and small claims" and fn 7's "with equal claims every divisor method reduces to rotation". The second is right. The first is Balinski and Young's result under their specific bias criterion; worth a hedge, since it is doing no work here.
- Appendix and 5.2: the κ→∞ limit is described as rotation. It is exclusion-until-served with random tie-breaking, which fn 4 says has the same distribution of totals; say that rather than "is rotation".

## 6. Is the formal material pitched right?

The level is right for a generalist journal: one table, one parametrised family stated in a sentence, an appendix a non-specialist can skip and a specialist can check. Do not add formalism to the body. Three changes, all in the direction of making what is there trustworthy rather than making it bigger:

- Report standard errors in the table (a second line in small type, or a ± after each figure) and say how many leavers each tenure cell is based on. Without this, "within simulation error" is unverifiable, and one referee will notice that 0.91 and 1.06 are both being called proportional.
- Derive the newcomer's first-period chance analytically as a function of κ and the floor rather than only simulating it. It is a two-line calculation, it explains the whole first column, and it would let you replace fn 8 with a true statement.
- Add one sensitivity line to the appendix (N=100, exit rate 0.05 and 0.2). The claim that the rules in use "lie well outside" the acceptable region needs to be robust to N, since Nevada's pool is not ten.

The apportionment material (fn 7, Wintein and Heilmann) is at the right level as a footnote and would be too much in the body. The κ family is clear; the one addition worth making in the body is the sentence from fn 9 about the half-life, which is the most intuitive gloss on "proportionate" in the paper and is currently buried.
