# Screening Report: "Fairness Has Memory"

**Paper title:** Fairness Has Memory

**Word count:** c. 9,850 (main text, footnotes, one table) plus a 500-word appendix; c. 10,350 in all.

**Thesis:** Broome's own Division Principle (equal claims to a divisible good require division; a lottery is a second-best surrogate for division) entails that a good allocated repeatedly among overlapping claimants is divisible over the sequence, so fairness requires each claimant's later chances to depend on her past shortfall. What memory should track is shortfall in units where later units are as good as earlier ones and time waited where they are not; and it must be pressed proportionately, so that lexical rules (queues, preference points) are unfair to newcomers where the currency is units and exactly right where it is waiting.

**Decision:** EVALUATE

The paper is problem-driven, generalist, and carries a concrete positive contribution that can be stated in one sentence: a principle the Broomean and his critics already share entails that repeated lotteries must remember, and a parametric analysis says how much. It is EVALUATE rather than SCREEN because its centre of gravity is allocation rules, weighted lotteries, apportionment and simulated mechanisms, which is social-choice territory in the editor-in-chief's own areas. If it is routed to an area editor instead, Matthew Smith is the fit. It is not REVIEW: there are internal tensions a careful author could have removed, and a referee will press them hard. They are fixable without abandoning the thesis, which is why they do not add up to a desk reject.

## Key Strengths

- **A real problem with generalist reach.** The paper starts from an institutional disagreement anyone can see the point of (Nevada remembers, New Mexico forgets; H-1B forgets; kidneys queue), and asks a question that exists independently of any recent exchange: does a fair lottery owe anything to its past losers? The Broome literature is the occasion, not the subject.
- **The core argument is economical and new.** It uses only premises Broome and his critics share (the Division Principle; lotteries as second best), and the cake reductio plus Broome's own conscription passage make the conclusion hard to resist. The claim that the standard theory has been evaluating the wrong unit is a genuine finding, not a gloss.
- **The extinction reply does real work.** §4's separation of re-run, interrupted allocation and recurrence is exactly right, and the point that a surrogate is not a portion (fn 10) turns the standard theory's own commitment into a reason for memory.
- **§5 delivers a quantified verdict.** The paper does not stop at "memory matters"; it shows that the rules in use (Arizona, Nevada, Colorado) transfer expected share from short-tenured to long-tenured claimants, and that a moderate shortfall rule does not. Few papers in this literature get to a number.
- **§6's bounds are principled.** Binding memory to the practice, the claim, the claimant and a weight keeps the view distinct from luck egalitarianism and from rectification, and the transition and regress answers are crisp.

## Key Weaknesses

- **The weight is chosen, not derived.** §4 says Maggie's claim to the 2027 tag is "stronger"; Broome then says chances should be proportional to claims; so the rule should follow from a function from shortfall to claim strength. The paper never supplies one. Instead κ is selected by a composite criterion (expected share proportional to tenure, then minimise dispersion) whose Broomean credentials are not argued and whose first half the forgetting rule also satisfies. This is exactly the step at which the third claim is said to be "established" (§5.3).
- **Non-separability conflicts with the paper's own account of claims.** §3.2 defines local fairness using history-blind claims and concludes fairness is non-separable. §4 then makes claims history-laden. On the paper's own view, each period's allocation is fair iff it treats history-laden claims proportionally, and sequence fairness is then a function of member fairness after all. Claim 1 is either a claim about the standard theory or it is false.
- **The two verdicts on queues conflict under attrition.** §5.3 condemns the recurrence-structure queue because a claimant whose tenure turns out short gets nothing with certainty, and refuses to be consoled by ex ante equality. §5.4 excuses the identical feature of the bottleneck queue on the ground that "every entrant's prospects at entry are identical," which is the unconditional ex ante standard §5.3 and §7.4 reject. The "currency" distinction does not dissolve this: the claimant who dies unserved lost a unit, not waiting.
- **Fungibility is binary; timeliness is a matter of degree.** Later units are almost always somewhat worse than earlier ones, so strictly Fungibility almost never holds, and several of the paper's own recurrence examples (chores, research grants, IVF cycles) are timeliness-sensitive. The two-structure division leaves the mixed case, which is probably the common case, without a currency.
- **The motivating cases are never shown to involve claims.** Broome's claims are duties owed to the candidate herself, distinguished from usefulness. The paper assumes hunters, ballot entrants and visa registrants have equal claims without saying why, and then reads the H-1B wage weighting as a "proxy for the strength of claims," which is the claim/usefulness conflation §2 warns against.

## Critical Issues for Revision

1. Derive the weight. Either say how shortfall maps to claim strength, or derive the newcomer constraint from the second-best status of lotteries: a claimant for whom division is unavailable (short tenure) is owed the surrogate, so every period's newcomer must get her proportional chance, and κ is the largest weight consistent with that. The table then confirms a derived constraint instead of standing in for one.
2. Restate Claim 1 as a claim about the standard, history-blind theory, or drop it and make Memory the headline. As written it is contradicted by §4.
3. Reconcile the queue verdicts. Either concede that a bottleneck with unforeseeable attrition calls for a queue-weighted lottery rather than a pure queue, or explain why the conditional-on-tenure standard of §5.3 does not apply there.
4. Replace binary Fungibility with a degree notion (units discounted by delay) and fill in the 2x2 of pool dynamics by fungibility, including the mixed case and the bottleneck-with-fungible-units case, in which the view implies the forgetting rule is fair.
5. Say in a paragraph why public allocation among eligible applicants generates equal claims in Broome's sense, and correct the reading of the 2026 H-1B rule.

# Notes for the author

## 1. The objection a good referee will press hardest

The objection is this. Your argument runs from the Division Principle to memory, and from there to a specific weight, via Broome's rule that chances be proportional to claims. That route needs a premise you never state: how much a unit of shortfall strengthens a claim. Without it, the shortfall rule's κ is a free parameter, and what you actually do in §5.3 is fix it by a criterion of your own manufacture: expected share proportional to tenure, then minimise dispersion. Three things are wrong with that as it stands.

First, the criterion is not shown to be Broome's. Broome gives you proportional satisfaction of claims; you have to argue that "expected share over realised tenure" is what proportional satisfaction of a persisting person's claim comes to, and the McKerlie sentence gestures at that without doing it.

Second, the two halves of the criterion are of different kinds, and you use each against a different opponent. The forgetting rule passes the expectational half perfectly (your own Table, row 1) and is condemned on the dispersional half alone. The queue and κ=10 beat κ=1 on the dispersional half (0.46 and 0.41 against 0.58) and are condemned on the expectational half alone. A referee will ask why the ex post measure is decisive against forgetting and the ex ante measure decisive against the queue, and will note that fn 10 says chances are not entered in the ledger, while the Table's headline column is a chance.

Third, the tie to §4. There you say Maggie's claim to the 2027 tag is stronger than a past winner's "for the same reason that ... the claim of someone who has had no slice yet is stronger." That is a claim about claim strength, and on Broome's theory claim strength fixes chances directly. So a referee will ask: if you know the claims, you know the rule; why the detour through simulation? And if you do not know the claims, in what sense has the third claim been "established"?

Does the paper contain the materials to answer? Partly. The answer I think you want is already in §2 and §3.4: a lottery is not merely permitted but required as surrogate where division is unavailable. For a claimant whose tenure is shorter than the cycle of the practice, division is unavailable; nothing can give her 0.3 of a tag. So she is owed the surrogate: an equal chance, this period, at this unit. That is a Broomean reason, not an expectational one, for the constraint that every period's newcomer get her proportional chance, and it is a reason the queue violates outright. Given that constraint, the Division Principle says press shortfall as hard as the constraint allows, and your Table says that is κ in the neighbourhood of 1. Stated that way, κ≈1 is derived rather than picked, the two halves of the criterion have different jobs (the surrogate constraint is lexically prior; dispersion is what you minimise subject to it), and fn 10 is no longer in tension with the Table. What the paper does not contain is any way of turning shortfall into claim strength directly, and I would stop claiming that it does: say that the weight is fixed by the surrogate constraint, not by a claim-strength function.

## 2. Fungibility and the two structures

The division does not hold up as stated, for three reasons.

(a) The two structures are individuated by pool dynamics (does a winner leave?), but the currency is fixed by Fungibility (is a later unit as good?). Those are logically independent, and you have four cells, not two. Recurrence with non-fungible units is the mixed case: a night off the rota this week is not the same as next week's; a grant this year, for a lab that will close, is not the same as one next year; an IVF cycle at 38 is not the same as one at 41. These are your own examples, and the paper says nothing about what memory tracks in them. Bottleneck with fungible units (a claimant wants exactly one and truly does not care when) is the other empty cell, and it is a useful test: your view implies the forgetting lottery is fair there and the persistent loser has no complaint. I think that is right, and saying so would show the view has teeth, but it should be said.

(b) Fungibility as defined ("a later unit would satisfy it as an earlier one would") is a binary that almost never holds, because delay is almost always a cost. You need a degree notion: units discounted by delay. Once you have it, the shortfall rule and the queue are endpoints of a single family (shortfall measured in discounted units; as the discount goes to infinity, delay dominates and the rule becomes a queue), and the two-structure architecture becomes one rule with one parameter fixed by the claim. That is a stronger paper than the one you have, and it removes the need to sort institutions into bins before evaluating them.

(c) The treatments of queues do conflict, and the place is attrition. In §5.3 the decisive count against Colorado is that a claimant whose tenure turns out short gets zero with certainty, and you explicitly refuse to be consoled by the fact that her prospects at entry were fine. In §5.4, the claimant who dies or gives up before reaching the front of the queue also gets zero with certainty, and you console yourself with exactly that fact: "every entrant's prospects at entry are identical under either rule." That is the unconditional ex ante standard §7.4 says does not carry over to repeated structures. The currency move does not rescue you here, because the unserved leaver's loss is not in waiting (she waited less than anyone) but in units (she never got the flat). Without attrition there is no conflict, since everyone is served and only delay varies; with attrition there is, and your headline practical verdict (the visa lotteries should be queues) is delivered in the case where your own §3.3 example, the sponsor who will not wait, guarantees attrition. Two fixes are available. The cheaper is to concede that where exit is unforeseeable, the bottleneck rule is a queue-weighted lottery, not a pure queue, and to note that this is what several real bottleneck systems actually are. The more principled is the surrogate constraint from note 1, applied uniformly: where division of the divisible currency is unavailable to a claimant (because she may leave before it is completed), she is owed the surrogate, in both structures.

One further gap. §4's answer to the extinction objection is built for recurrence: Maggie's claim to the 2027 tag is stronger because a past winner has had a slice. In a bottleneck nobody in the pool has had a slice, so the cake gives no reason to prefer Maggie to a newcomer. The reason has to come from waiting, and §4 does not say so. A sentence would do.

## 3. Section 6

The balance is mostly right. The section answers the reach question rather than surveying it, the derivations from "a claim, to a specific good, on the agency dividing it" are genuinely derivations, and the three boundary questions in 6.2 are short and determinate. The weeds risk is low. The problems are argumentative.

Unnecessary: 6.4 ("bound in weight") repeats §5.3 and §5.4 and can be two sentences. The re-listing of the intro's questions at the top of §6 can go.

Necessary and missing, in order of importance:

- 6.3 hands the individuation of claimants to the practice ("it is the practice, not the theory of fairness, that makes that decision"). That lets New Mexico say its claimants are "2027 applicants," a new set each year, so that nobody persists and there is nothing to remember. §5.3 says claims belong to persons, who persist. You need the theory to constrain the practice here: persons are the default bearers, and a practice's decision to recognise households or lineages is a decision about which persons' claims are pooled, not a licence to define persisting claimants out of existence.
- 6.1 needs a criterion for individuating practices. The federal government runs both the H-1B and the Diversity Visa lotteries; Nevada runs sheep and elk draws. Are those one practice or two? "The good matters" handles sheep versus elk; nothing handles two agencies of one government allocating the same good, or one agency reorganising itself. As written, an allocator can shed its obligations by splitting.
- A sentence on periods of non-application. Your shortfall formula counts periods "present." Nevada resets points after two years of not applying. Is a claimant who did not apply present? On the "bound to the claim" view she presumably has no claim in that period and it does not count; say so, since it matters for the transition question.
- The agency bound produces the Arizona-to-Nevada result, which you call harsh only in appearance. It is harsh in fact, and the sentence "Nevada is not completing Arizona's division" is a restatement, not a reason. If the good is the same (a sheep tag) and the claimant is the same, the Division Principle as you state it does not obviously care which agency held the earlier lottery. The bound is defensible, but you have to say why the agency, rather than the good and the claimant, is the unit.

## 4. Where the paper's own examples, concessions or numbers undercut it

- Table, κ=1, tenure 10: 1.04 ± .01. That is four standard errors from proportional, and the text says κ=1 keeps expected share "proportional to tenure at every tenure" and "answers the newcomer objection completely." Either the standard errors are wrong or the claim is. Also "surpluses are rarely clipped" at κ=1 is asserted, not shown, and I doubt it: with N=10, a claimant who wins twice in her first ten periods is clipped, and that is not rare.
- Fixed population, κ=1, ten periods: 2.1 of 10 claimants still get nothing. Your recommended rule, in the case most favourable to it, removes about two-fifths of the unfairness you identified. Rotation removes all of it. The Table's dispersion column says the same thing for churn. You are candid that κ=1 is not uniquely correct, but the paper reads as though the shortfall rule fixes the problem, and your numbers say it roughly halves it. The honest statement of the third claim is that fairness requires a substantial residual lottery even after memory, and it would be worth saying why that is not a defeat.
- Huesch and Brady: 78 per cent chose to exclude the first winner. Exclusion is the lexical rule you say is unfair to newcomers. In their design there are no newcomers, so no conflict, but you should say so rather than citing the study as if it supports your rule.
- §3.2: "Rotation is not locally fair at any period after the first." On your own §4, it is: the claimant next in the rotation has the strongest claim, and gets the unit. This is the non-separability problem from the report.
- §5.4: "one in a hundred waits more than forty periods" requires the pool to be replenished, which the paragraph does not say (with ten fixed claimants everyone is served by period ten); and 0.9^40 is about 1.5 in a hundred.
- §7.1: you concede that memory raises the value of a manufactured claim and that the remedy is to attach memory to costly participation. H-1B registration is nearly free and is made by employers. Your visa verdict therefore lands in the case where your own concession bites hardest, and the paper does not say what the costly participation would be.

## 5. Claims that are false, overstated or insufficiently supported

- §1: H-1B wage weighting "made the lottery sensitive to a proxy for the strength of claims." Wage level is a proxy for the value of the job to the employer or economy, which is Broome's "other reasons for giving it to her, such as that doing so would be useful," not a claim. On your own §2 the reform did not fix either half.
- §1: "Institutions mostly side with Nevada." You list at least as many that forget (New Mexico, Wimbledon, H-1B, DV, most school lotteries) as remember. Drop "mostly."
- §1 and §5.1: US kidney allocation is "primarily a queue ordered by waiting time." It is a points system in which waiting time is one major component alongside sensitisation, longevity matching and paediatric priority; and §5.4's "weighted for urgency, as kidney allocation is" is doubtful, since it is liver allocation that is urgency-driven and kidney allocation has only a narrow medical-urgency category. Check both before a referee does.
- §1: "Colorado and Wyoming keep no lottery to speak of ... chance only breaks ties." Colorado's draw includes a random component for some hunt codes. Check.
- §3.4 and throughout: the paper assumes hunters, ballot entrants and visa registrants have equal claims in Broome's sense and never argues it. Given that claims are duties owed to the applicant and need is the paradigm, a referee will ask what duty Nevada owes anyone to let her shoot a sheep. There is an answer (equal standing of eligible citizens to a public resource), but it has to be given, and it may not extend to Wimbledon.
- §5.3: "the forgetting rule is dominated at no ex ante cost" and "this rule answers the newcomer objection completely." See note 4 on the 1.04.
- §1: "The theory of fair allocation has not connected this disagreement to the theory of claims." Fair on the philosophy side, but the economics and computer-science work on dynamic allocation with waiting lists (there is a literature on lotteries versus waitlists for housing and on dynamic matching in overloaded queues, and fn 8 concedes the temporal fair-division work) is on exactly the bottleneck question, and a referee from that side will notice its absence. I have not checked this against the web and am flagging it from memory; verify.
- §7.2: "Spiekermann's view, that a lottery is fair only if its result could easily have gone the other way." I could not confirm this is his view from the paper alone; make sure the attribution is his and not a reconstruction.

## 6. Length

The length is close to justified: the paper does five distinct things (argument, extinction reply, design analysis, bounds, objections) and none is padding. But it is over 10k, the revision has to add material (the 2x2, the derivation of the weight, the claims paragraph), and there is 1,200 to 1,500 words of cuttable text:

- §1, second paragraph: the catalogue of institutions is repeated in fn 3 and again in §5.1. Keep it in one place.
- §2, the paragraph beginning "The theory has been much criticized": the list of critics is a literature review that does no work; the paragraph's real content is its last four sentences.
- §3.4, the apportionment paragraph plus fn 8: a digression. Keep the footnote, cut the paragraph to one sentence.
- §6.4: two sentences.
- §7.2 (Expression): the weakest section. The reply is rhetorical ("the agency does not know who she is"), a Stone-style theorist will say that is impartiality, and the Spiekermann material is admitted to be "not yet mine." Cut to a paragraph or drop.
- Conclusion: the first paragraph re-derives the thesis sentence and the second restates the practical verdicts. Keep the third paragraph (the conscription point is the best single line in the paper) and one sentence of each of the others.

## 7. Filler, repetition, throat-clearing

- "and it is the relaxation that makes the problem hard; but the structure is easiest to see when the population is fixed" (§3.1). Cut.
- "This should not surprise for a comparative notion" (§3.2) is repeated in the conclusion as "That is not a surprising thing for a comparative notion to turn out to be." Once.
- A tic of telling the reader that a consequence is a virtue: "this is not a limitation of the view but the point of it" (6.2); "That the three are different is a merit of the view" (6.3); "The floor at zero is not a technicality" (5.2); "it is here that a thesis like mine is most exposed" (§6). Each is fine once; together they read as the author grading his own paper.
- "It fixed the wrong half, and the half it left unfixed is the one the Division Principle speaks to" (§5.4) is a flourish that is also, on your own §2, false (note 5).
- "I have no quarrel with this literature; it is about something else" (§4). Fine, but "it is about something else" has already been said by the sentence before it.
- Unresolved cross-references throughout ("(sec-division?)", "(tbl-churn?)"). Production issue, but a referee will see it as carelessness.
- The last sentence of §6 ("Nothing in the argument gives the persistently unlucky a claim on the world, and a view that did would be a different view") restates 6.1's luck-egalitarianism paragraph.
