**Answer the gambler's-fallacy charge the paper never faces: the churn rule is a balancing rule, so say what licenses balancing in recurrence and not in merit streams.**

**Where.** §3, "What memory tracks," and the Objections section, which has no reply to this.

**What is wrong.** A rule that raises a past loser's chance in proportion to her shortfall has the form of the pattern the judgment literature calls the gambler's fallacy. Where cases are independent merit decisions, judges grow likelier to deny after a run of grants (Chen, Moskowitz and Shue 2016; cf. Tversky and Kahneman 1971; both from memory; check). A reader trained there will say "she is due" is the fallacy in moral clothing.

**What is insulated.** The fixed-pool argument (@sec-division) is insulated. Rotation needs no belief about any device: once the order is drawn, each unit's holder is fixed by the procedure, and the fallacy has nothing to attach to. The churn argument is not insulated by its own premises. Its extra weight for losers rests on shortfall being a debt the allocator owes. That is the right answer, but the paper states it in one clause ("owed by the agency") and never sets it against the balancing charge.

**What to add.** Shortfall is defined only relative to a quantity shared among a set of claimants. An asylum docket has no such quantity, so there is no shortfall to balance. Where a fixed number of units per period goes to a pool of fixed size, balancing is division, and the debt sits in the allocator's ledger, not in the device. Add an objection saying so, and state that the thesis requires the same surface rule in the first case and forbids it in the second.

**An experiment.** Vignettes with identical histories and rules, crossed on two factors: units are either fixed tags for a shared pool or independent merit cases; the rule is described either as "the loser's chance rises" or as "the next tag goes to whoever has had fewer." The paper predicts endorsement of memory for tags under either wording and rejection for merit cases under either. A gambler's-fallacy account predicts that endorsement follows the wording and spreads to merit cases. If merit cases draw as much endorsement as tags, the cake intuition is about balancing, and the paper should rest on the Division Principle alone. If endorsement tracks the fixed quantity, the intuition supports the paper. Until someone has asked, the cake's "nobody thinks" is not evidence for the thesis, and the paper should say so.
