**Your surrogate constraint is an ex ante principle. Say so, and replace the §7 dilemma with a defence of when your ex ante is.**

The "Ex ante" reply offers two answers to when the ex ante is: the beginning of the practice, or of each period. There is a third, and it is yours. The surrogate constraint, that "no claimant's expected share, over whatever tenure she turns out to have, fall short of proportionality to her claims," is an expected-share floor. It is evaluated at each claimant's *entry*, with her tenure treated as known and her draws not. It is lexically prior to your ex post concern, and it settles most of your verdicts. It condemns Nevada, Arizona and Colorado, it caps κ near 1, and in bottlenecks it is the entire case for the visa lotteries. Only the case against forgetting in recurrence is ex post. So the paper is ex ante-ist where it matters and ex post-ist where it suits it.

A lexical hybrid, ex ante floor with ex post division above it, is respectable. But it carries two burdens.

First, defend the index. §5.2 motivates the floor by saying a short-stayer is owed "a chance, each period, proportional to their claim." Read per period, that floor forbids memory wherever exit is possible: anyone might leave, so nobody may fall below 1/N, so everyone gets exactly 1/N. Your recurrence result survives only on the entry reading, under which a past winner's weight can reach zero (one claimant-period in twenty at κ = 1). Why is that no breach? Because she holds a realized surplus. But that uses an ex post fact to discharge an ex ante debt, and you must say why that is allowed.

Second, reconcile the ledgers. §4 says "a chance is not a portion of the good," so memory counts units. Yet the floor treats expected units, which are summed chances, as what is owed. The strongest ex ante view lives in that gap. It is Machina's Mom (1989; from memory; check), who will not re-flip because the loser had his chance. That is fairness with memory, but the memory is of chances, and on that ledger New Mexico remembers perfectly. Your reply should be that a claimant is owed the best available approximation to proportional satisfaction: chances insofar as division may be unavailable to her, units insofar as it is available.

So rewrite the paragraph along these lines (about 230 words). Drop the second reply: the dynamic problem for Diamond's view is a standard topic. For prospect-fairness, cite Frick, not Otsuka and Voorhoeve 2009, which concerns the separateness of persons (from memory; check). Cut §1's Arizona/Colorado/H-1B detail (about 60 words) to make room.
