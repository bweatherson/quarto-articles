## Vong 2015

**1. "cannot be lost due to the results of a procedurally fair lottery" (paper: pp. 479–480)**
- Source, p. 480: "Another advantage of my view over Broome's is that on my view benefit claims cannot be lost due to the results of a procedurally fair lottery. This explains why people who lose the lottery still have benefit claims and therefore offers the best account of temporally extended cases where the number of benefits available for distribution increases, like Extra Buoy."
- The procedural half of the paper's sentence ("a *procedural* claim, once satisfied by a fair lottery, is satisfied") is p. 479: "On my dual structure view, the procedural claim has already been met by the first fair lottery and running a second lottery would invalidate a previously fair procedure for no reason."
- Verdict: wording OK (source has plural "benefit claims cannot be lost"; the paper's quoted span is verbatim). Page: the quotation itself is wholly on p. 480. "pp. 479–480" is defensible as covering both halves of the sentence; if the citation is meant for the quotation alone, change to "(p. 480)".

**2. "a new case of scarce benefit distribution" (paper: p. 483)**
- Source, p. 483: "When only the number of available benefits increases but one still cannot benefit all claimants, then the fair distribution depends on whether the initially available benefits have already been distributed or not. If the initially available benefits have already been distributed fairly, then one can treat the remaining claimants as a new case of scarce benefit distribution. Remaining claimants are lottery losers that previously received a fair chance of benefiting but did not actually receive the benefit they have a claim on. Fairness requires one to run an appropriate lottery between remaining claimants when new benefits become available. … Given scarcity, running a fair lottery to decide whom to benefit would best satisfy their dual claims."
- Verdict: OK. Page correct. The gloss "the losers … get a fresh fair lottery" is fair: Vong identifies the "remaining claimants" as "lottery losers" and prescribes "an appropriate lottery" / "a fair lottery" among them. Minor: Vong's prescription is conditional on the initial benefits having already been distributed; if they have not, he wants a new lottery among *all* claimants (p. 483). The paper's "when new units arrive" fits the first branch, which is the one it needs.

**3. Rejects diachronic weakening and strengthening (paper: pp. 477–479)**
- Definitions, p. 477: "On one such view, those who lose the lottery have their claim qualitatively weakened but not negated. Call this view diachronic weakening. On the other view, those who win the lottery have their claim qualitatively strengthened. Call this view diachronic strengthening."
- Rejection, p. 478: "While more plausible than Broome's synchronic view, these diachronic views should also be rejected. This is because both views also lead to the implausible conclusions of Broome's account in temporally extended cases like Stormy Seas." P. 479: "Diachronic strengthening has an even more implausible implication … For this reason diachronic strengthening is less plausible than diachronic weakening."
- Verdict: OK. Both defined and both rejected; pages 477–479 correct (definitions 477, table 478, rejection 478–479).

**4. Negative claim: Vong's later lottery is among non-beneficiaries; he never considers served claimants staying for later units**
- Recipients leaving: not stated in so many words, but built in. P. 479: "Benefit claims are satisfied, ex post, when the benefits are actually distributed." P. 483: after distribution the "remaining claimants are lottery losers". His cases are rescues (Stormy Seas, Extra Buoy, Defective Extra Buoy: one buoy saves one sailor once) and transplants (corneas, kidneys in passing), where a served claimant has no further claim.
- Later lottery among those who did not benefit: yes, p. 483 (quoted under item 2).
- Later distinct units among claimants who stay after being served: nowhere. Section III (pp. 482–486) varies the numbers of claimants and benefits and the quality of benefits over time, but always with a claimant's benefit claim discharged on receipt. The only "winner retains standing" discussion (pp. 481–482) concerns a lottery *winner who has not yet received the good* (B in Stormy Seas) when new claimants arrive, and Vong rejects the winner's complaint.
- "his objections, about new claimants for a single unit": his rejection of the two diachronic views runs entirely through Stormy Seas (subsequent claimants, one buoy), p. 478–479. His objections to Broome also use Extra Buoy (a second unit) and imperfect lotteries, but in none has anyone been served.
- Verdict: OK.

**5. Broome's suggestion in conversation (paper: p. 481)**
- Source, p. 481 (sec. II.iii): "Broome has objected that lottery winners of earlier, previously fair lotteries have a justified complaint that their earlier winnings are ignored. This complaint is particularly salient if earlier losers won a subsequent lottery. Broome has suggested in conversation that this may be avoided by running subsequent lotteries that take into account earlier results. They do so by only running subsequent lotteries between previous winners and subsequent claimants, while adjusting the probabilities of subsequent lotteries to ensure all claimants have an appropriate chance of benefiting." Worked example: B 50%, C and D 25% each in the second lottery, so that A, B, C, D each have a 25% chance of being saved; "A, a previous loser, cannot be saved by the results of a subsequent lottery".
- (A different "in conversation" suggestion by Broome, the specific-benefits view, is at p. 474–475; the paper does not cite it.)
- Verdict: page correct; "appropriate chance of benefiting" is verbatim; "in the winner's favour" is accurate for the per-lottery probabilities (winner 50% vs 25% for each newcomer). Two things the paper's sentence leaves out: (a) Broome's scheme also excludes previous losers from later lotteries entirely, which is the extinction thesis at work and arguably the more striking feature; (b) its aim is *equal* overall chances of benefiting for everyone, so "in the winner's favour" describes the mechanism, not the result. Also note Vong rejects Broome's premise that winners have a justified complaint (pp. 481–482). The paper's "it is the *winner's* standing that is protected" matches Broome's objection as Vong reports it.

**6. Corneas**
- The current draft does not mention corneas (the parenthesis was cut; see `_NOTES-6k.md` line 242). For the record: corneal transplantation is his principal real-world application (abstract p. 470; pp. 484–486), introduced as "the real world case of corneal transplantations" (p. 484); his central illustrative cases are Stormy Seas and Extra Buoy. Whether cornea recipients leave the pool is not stated, but follows from benefit claims being satisfied on receipt (p. 479). Kidneys appear once, in passing (p. 474). "Dialysis" does not occur in Vong.
- Verdict: nothing to check in the current text.

## Hersch & Rowe 2024 (author preprint)

**7. Endorsement of the Diversity Visa lottery (paper: 188)**
- Source, sec. 1 Introduction, preprint pp. 3–4: "An upshot of our discussion is that it entails that immigration visas to the US should be understood as a case of scarcity, and therefore it is the kind of case to which lotteries are more appropriate than queues. This is indeed what the US government more or less does through its Diversity Immigrant Visa Program, which makes up to 50,000 immigrant visas available annually, drawn from random selection among all entries to individuals who are from countries with low rates of immigration to the United States. Given that demand for immigration visas to the US far outstrips the supply, we treat it as a case of scarcity and consequently we view the current practice of distributing this subset of visas through a lottery is appropriate." Also sec. 2, preprint p. 5, lists among scarcity examples "green cards when there are more would-be immigrants than a country will allow".
- Verdict: OK. It is an endorsement ("appropriate"), and the stated reason is scarcity. Section placement: last paragraphs of sec. 1, immediately before sec. 2; consistent with p. 188 given sec. 1 at 186 and sec. 2 at 189. (H&R's name for the program is "Diversity Immigrant Visa Program"; "Diversity Visa lottery" is the ordinary name and not a quotation.)

**8. "arrives into the lottery pool early and is continuously unlucky" (paper: 199)**
- Source, sec. 4 Bottlenecks and Queues, preprint p. 17: "The only way to make sense of applying a lottery in bottleneck cases is to conceive of it as a lottery for the secondary good of less waiting time rather than for the primary good. The problem with such lotteries is that they can introduce large disparities in how long people end up waiting for the good. If a person arrives into the lottery pool early and is continuously unlucky, they might wait significantly longer than a person who just showed up and enjoyed good luck. A lottery system may also be unfair for bottleneck cases, for example, a lottery might be held for season tickets for a popular football team. If everyone that waits for such a ticket will eventually receive one, even if it takes years, holding periodic lotteries will likely mean that some will 'win' the lottery before those who have waited longer receive the ticket. In effect, the winners will not need to wait at all, while others wait, unluckily, for decades." Preprint p. 18: "Conducting a lottery to determine who will receive the good next in bottleneck cases in which people already have different waiting times, is unfair."
- Verdict: quotation verbatim; gloss "may wait far longer than a lucky newcomer" is a fair paraphrase of "might wait significantly longer than a person who just showed up and enjoyed good luck". Section 4, about two preprint pages after its start (preprint p. 15); consistent with p. 199 given sec. 4 at 198. The paper's framing ("a periodic lottery in a bottleneck is unfair because…") splices the "continuously unlucky" sentence with the "periodic lotteries … unfair" season-ticket sentence that follows; that is a fair reading of the passage. One caution: H&R state the remark under the explicit condition "If everyone that waits for such a ticket will eventually receive one", and their definition of a bottleneck is a case where everyone eventually gets the good (item 9). So the paper's "holds only where everyone will be served" is H&R's own restriction, not a qualification they missed; the sentence reads as a correction but is agreement. Since H&R themselves classify visas as scarcity, not bottleneck, they would not apply the remark to visas either.

**9. Bottleneck vs scarcity; "bottleneck" narrowly for queues everyone eventually clears (paper lines 65, 109)**
- Scarcity, sec. 2, preprint p. 5: "We consider cases of scarcity to arise when the demand for the good at a given time cannot be fully satisfied." Sec. 1, preprint p. 4: "Cases of scarcity arise when demand for the good cannot be met by the current supply."
- Bottleneck, sec. 2, preprint p. 6: "Bottleneck cases obtain when it is not possible for each person to receive the good they demand immediately. In bottleneck cases it would make everyone better off for the good to be allocated immediately, but various practicalities prevent this even though the good, over time, is abundant, thereby leading to a bottleneck. … At t0 the demand for the good is strictly greater than the supply for that good at t0. Yet at tn, the demand for the good is lesser or equal to the supply. … Bottlenecks operate like scarcity at t0, and like abundance at tn". Sec. 1, preprint p. 4: "When dealing with bottlenecks, everyone will eventually get the good". Sec. 4, preprint p. 17: "in bottleneck cases the likelihood that any given individual will receive the good is the same, and is equal to 1."
- Allocation: sec. 1, preprint p. 3: "lotteries are more appropriate in cases of scarcity, whereas queues are more appropriate in bottleneck cases"; abstract: "Queues are best suited to distribute the good of waiting time fairly."
- Verdict: OK on both counts. The paper's own use of "bottleneck" (she leaves the pool: a visa, a flat, a kidney) is broader than H&R's, and the parenthesis at line 65 is needed: H&R classify kidneys and green cards as *scarcity* (preprint p. 5). Two details for awareness: fn. 12 (preprint p. 6) allows bottlenecks "in which the bottleneck never in fact turns into abundance, as in cases where there is a continuous queue that gets replenished with both demand and supply at some steady rate", still with each individual eventually served; and sec. 5 (preprint pp. 22–23) handles the mixed scarcity-plus-bottleneck case by lottery first, then queue among the winners.
- Aside, not a citation issue: sec. 5 of the 2024 paper (preprint pp. 23–24) already contains a recurrence example, the chocolate-bar case, where the parent says "it is only fair that the second child should get it" the second time round and H&R resist the child's "temporal separability" reply. The paper (line 23) credits the nearest statement of its thesis to HerschRowe2025; the 2024 chapter has a germ of it too.

## Wasserman 1996

**10. Expressive/procedural rather than distributive; "an impartial procedure is fair whatever last year's did"**
- His view, p. 31: "In this paper, I will offer an account of lottery fairness which is procedural but not exclusively prophylactic. I will argue that lotteries also serve an expressive function, which does require a minimal sort of epistemic equiprobability. … in order to express an equal commitment to each claimant's receiving the scarce good, those who control the allocation process must have no reason to believe that any claimant is more likely than any other to satisfy the payoff condition." Rejection of the distributive view, p. 31: "In Part III of the paper, I will point out several difficulties in understanding the fairness of lotteries in distributive terms"; pp. 43–45 (Part III.A); conclusion p. 48: "the fairness of a lottery among equally-entitled claimants to a scarce good cannot be understood in terms of such a distribution. Lotteries are fair if they respect the claimants' equal entitlements to the scarce good; they do so simply by distributing the scarce good in a way that cannot reasonably be seen by the allocator or the claimants as favoring any claimant."
- On Broome's partial equality of satisfaction, pp. 46–48: "What makes a lottery fair, however, is not that it distributes equal or proportionate odds, but that it provides partial equality of satisfaction (1990, p. 97) or surrogate satisfaction (p. 100). One question raised by Broome's analysis is how the partial equality of satisfaction differs from the equality of expected satisfaction claimed by Kornhauser and Sager. Kornhauser and Sager do not suppose hungry claimants can actually eat chances, and it is not clear how the consideration claimants receive by getting a chance is less misleadingly described as a partial equality of satisfaction claims than as an equality of expected satisfaction" (p. 47); "Broome's qualified endorsement of proportional lotteries seems to assume that unfairness in the allocation of actual goods can be offset by the distribution of expected value. And that seems to require the distributive view of lottery fairness that Broom[e] rejects" (p. 48).
- Verdict on the grouping: OK. Wasserman's account is expressive and procedural; "exclude" in the paper's "express or exclude" fits Stone's sanitizing view rather than Wasserman, but as a description of the group it is accurate (Wasserman also discusses, and partly incorporates, the "prophylactic" function of preventing bias, pp. 31, 35–37).
- Repeated lotteries and past losers. Nothing in Wasserman gives a past loser a claim on a later draw. The two places that touch repetition both cut the other way:
  - pp. 41–42 (repeated drawings): "repeated drawings may have a disproportionate impact on identifiable groups"; "the attempt to 'correct' such a lottery to avoid disproportion is a cure worse than the disease: we would have to hold corrective lotteries to eliminate some members of the over-conscripted group and replace them with people from other groups … Instead, we simply insist on the procedure least likely to yield any such disproportion - a statistically equiprobable lottery" (p. 41–42); "the selection of any particular individual is no less desirable in distributive terms than the selection of any other" (p. 42). This is a group-level, prospective concern about a biased mechanism, answered by insisting on an impartial procedure rather than by remembering results. It supports, rather than undercuts, "fair whatever last year's did".
  - fn. 13, p. 45: "Broome denies that it is reasonable to wait as long as possible to hold an equiprobable lottery, or that it is fair to hold a second lottery after the first selects a winner, in order to prolong the expectation of equal utility. But Broome's stated objection to a second lottery is too narrow - that it constitutes 'tampering with the results of a fair random process' (p. 628). If the lottery was intended from the outset to have two rounds, there would be no tampering in holding the second." This concerns multi-stage lotteries for a single good (the semi-final-draws puzzle, pp. 44–45), and his own verdict is that "on a procedural view … there would be no reason to regard the number of stages in the lottery as affecting its fairness" (p. 45). Not about later units or past losers.
  - Also p. 40: claimants "would have no complaint on discovering that the die was innocently biased, at least if it was to be used only on this one occasion" — repetition matters to him only for statistical bias, not for the memory of outcomes.
- Verdict: OK. Wasserman never addresses the recurrence question directly, so "reach the same verdict" is an inference from his procedural view; pp. 41–42 make the inference explicit and nothing in the article resists it.
