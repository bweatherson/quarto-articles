## Addendum (2026-10-13, after Brian supplied the PDFs): Wasserman 1996, read in full with printed pages; Vong read in full from HTML

### Wasserman 1996 (E&P 12(1): 29-49), VERIFIED against the PDF
- Thesis: He rejects the *distributive* view of lotteries (Kornhauser & Sager; Broome's "partial equality of satisfaction"), on which a lottery equally divides an intangible good (a chance). His own view is *procedural but not merely prophylactic*: a lottery has an *expressive* function that needs only epistemic equiprobability, namely the allocator has "no reason to believe that any claimant is more likely than any other to satisfy the payoff condition" (p. 31). Part III lists difficulties with the "probabilistic division" idea.
- The "eat chances" idea is his, in two places:
  - p. 47: "Kornhauser and Sager do not suppose hungry claimants can actually eat chances, and it is not clear how the consideration claimants receive by getting a chance is less misleadingly described as a partial equality of satisfaction claims than as an equality of expected satisfaction." So the earlier "hungry claimants ... actually eat chances (p. 47)" fragment is real. But it is Wasserman's remark *about K&S*, not a free-standing thesis.
  - p. 49 (final paragraph): if we see a lottery as partially satisfying demand, "we may be more inclined to give the undivided good to one claimant and let the rest eat chances".
- Consequence for the paper's sentence ("claimants cannot eat chances ... is the beginning of an argument for memory rather than an objection to lotteries"): Wasserman's complaint is against calling a chance a partial satisfaction. The paper's own view relies on Broome's "surrogate satisfaction" reading, which is exactly the thing Wasserman presses against. So the sentence is a stretch. It also risks misrepresenting him. Safer: "Wasserman (1996, 47, 49) presses the worry that a chance is not something a claimant can eat, and a view on which chances are only surrogate satisfaction owes a reply. Memory is one such reply." Or cut.
- Repeated lotteries: the only passage is p. 41: a poorly mixed hopper might not matter for a single drawing, "But repeated drawings may have a disproportionate impact on identifiable groups" (long-run distributional effects). No weighting by past losses.
- Footnote 13 (p. 45, on Broome 1984, pp. 627-9): Broome denies that it is fair "to hold a second lottery after the first selects a winner"; Wasserman says "Broome's stated objection to a second lottery is too narrow - that it constitutes 'tampering with the results of a fair random process' (p. 628). If the lottery was intended from the outset to have two rounds, there would be no tampering in holding the second." This is a closer antecedent for multi-round lotteries and the paper should read it.
- NEW PAGE EVIDENCE FOR BROOME 1990: Wasserman cites Broome's "Fairness" at p. 97 for "partial equality of satisfaction", p. 100 for "surrogate satisfaction", and p. 99 for the proportional-lottery passages ("each person's chance should be in proportion to the strength of her claim: the lottery should be unequally weighted"; "the likelihood of this unfair result must be weighed against the contribution to fairness of the lottery itself", p. 99). The paper cites "97-98" for the surrogate passage, and Stone (via secondary source) gives 98 for the phrase. These disagree: Wasserman p. 100 vs Stone p. 98. Needs the Broome PDF. (Wasserman also lists the article as "Supplemental Vol. 91:87-101" and as 1990.)
- Wasserman cites Elster's "Taming Chance" as the 1989 book chapter (Solomonic Judgements), pp. 43-6 and 47. That is a reprint of the 1988 Tanner lecture and a possible source for page numbers.

### Vong 2015 (read in full on Cambridge Core HTML; printed pages NOT available in that view)
- "Diachronic weakening" = losers' claims are weakened but not negated; "diachronic strengthening" = winners' claims are strengthened (sec. II.i). Vong rejects both. So the paper's gloss "rejects views on which losing weakens a claim and on which winning strengthens it" is ACCURATE in content. 
- I found no passage considering a view where a past loss leaves *more* claim outstanding for later units. His own view keeps every loser's benefit claim equal ("benefit claims cannot be lost due to the results of a procedurally fair lottery", sec. II.ii; verbatim). The paper's negative claim looks right, but it should be put as "I find no discussion in Vong of ...".
- "a new case of scarce benefit distribution" (sec. III) is a verbatim substring of: "one can treat the remaining claimants as a new case of scarce benefit distribution. Remaining claimants are lottery losers that previously received a fair chance of benefiting but did not actually receive the benefit they have a claim on. Fairness requires one to run an appropriate lottery between remaining claimants when new benefits become available." This is the forgetting rule, stated by Vong himself. In this scenario the winners are the ones who have already received benefits, so they are not "remaining".
- Vong also says: "At least the remaining claimants previously received a fair positive chance of benefiting" (sec. III). He uses past chances to explain why subsequent claimants are worse off, not to give losers extra weight. That is a quotable passage for the paper's "Vong's reasons" paragraph.
- Cases: corneas (extended discussion) and a passing mention of kidneys for renal failure in sec. I. "Dialysis" does not appear. Fix "dialysis and organs" to "corneas (and, in passing, kidneys)", and check that the paper's verdict on "winners leave the pool" matches (cornea recipients leave the pool; yes).
- The pages 477-479, 479-480 and 483 still need the PDF, so these are unconfirmed (Feldblyum n. 5 supports 477-79 for weakening and strengthening, and n. 6 supports 479-80 for the extinction quote).
- Broome's source for the synchronic view: Vong cites Broome 1984 "Uncertainty and Fairness" (EJ 94, 624-32) and "Fairness" (Ethics Out of Economics, 1999, 111-22). Broome's weighting suggestion came "in conversation" (sec. II.iii) and that is what Feldblyum develops.

## Addendum 2: Broome 1990, "Fairness", read from the OUP PDF (printed pages from the running heads)

- The PDF has 16 pages, i.e. printed pp. 87-102, matching Crossref (the bib's 87--101 is wrong; use 87--102). Year: the publisher's date is 1991; the key says 1990 and the bib says 1991. Pick one form, and rename the key if you pick 1991.
- Maxims, p. 95: VERIFIED verbatim. "What, then, does fairness require? It requires, I suggest, that claims should be satisfied in proportion to their strength. I do not mean 'proportion' to be taken too precisely. But I do mean that equal claims require equal satisfaction, that stronger claims require more satisfaction than weaker ones, and also—very importantly—that weaker claims require some satisfaction. Weaker claims must not simply be overridden by stronger ones." The paper's quotation (2) and the page cite are right. The third maxim is "weaker claims require some satisfaction", not a threshold. The paper's use of "floor" for it should be flagged as the paper's gloss.
- "should be divided", p. 95: it appears as "horizontal equity says it should be divided", after: "imagine two people have equal claims to some good, but that, if the good is divided between them, less benefit will be produced in total than if it is all given to one. Then maximizing implies it should all go to one, but horizontal equity says it should be divided." So the page and the "even when giving it all to one would do more good" content are right. But "a quantity of food" is the paper's own example (the word "food" does not appear in Broome 1990). Say "equal claims to some good". Also, the quoted phrase is Broome's report of what horizontal equity (the economists' principle) says, which he is extending.
- Surrogate passage, pp. 97-98: VERIFIED, and the page cite 97-98 is right. "But a sort of partial equality in satisfaction can be achieved. Each person can be given [p. 98] a sort of surrogate satisfaction. By holding a lottery, each can be given an equal chance of getting the good. This is not a perfect fairness, but it meets the requirement of fairness to some extent. / It does so, of course, only if giving a person a chance of getting the good counts as a surrogate satisfaction of her claim. This seems plausible to me. After all, if you have a chance of getting the good you may actually get it."  Note: (a) Broome himself endorses the conditional ("This seems plausible to me"), so the paper can say he holds that a chance is a surrogate satisfaction, citing the justification "if you have a chance of getting the good you may actually get it"; (b) the passage as the paper quotes it merges "a sort of partial equality in satisfaction" (end of a sentence) into a lead-in. Fine as a partial quote, but "each person can be given" begins a new sentence, so the lower-case "each" should be "[E]ach" or be set apart. (c) He says it again on p. 100: "A lottery provides at least a surrogate satisfaction: a chance. But the rule of picking the youngest gives no sort of satisfaction at all." Wasserman's p. 100 is this second occurrence; Stone's 98 is the first. The two are consistent. 
- Not unfair to fail to satisfy, p. 95: "Everyone's claim to a good should, prima facie, be satisfied... Call this the 'satisfaction requirement'." and "it is not unfair if they are not [satisfied], provided everyone is treated proportionally". Fairness is "concerned only with relative satisfaction, not absolute satisfaction." Broome says fairness and the satisfaction requirement are separate: claims give rise to "two separate requirements: they should be satisfied, and they should be satisfied proportionally" (p. 96). This distinction matters for the paper's "priority of the floor over division" point flagged by the v3 screener.
- Claim definition: Broome's gloss that claims are "duties owed to the person" is at pp. 91-93 (he takes it "for granted that some [reasons] are duties owed to the person", p. 93). He declines to say which reasons are claims (p. 93: "I am not going to engage in controversy over which reasons are claims"), mentioning desert and need as possibilities (need "controversial", e.g. a kidney). So the paper's quotation (1)/(5) page cites 90-92 and 92-93 should be "91-93"; recheck against the exact wording used. The "useful" contrast: "other reasons for giving it to her" is the paper's paraphrase.
- Nothing in Broome 1990 on repeated or sequential lotteries, promises, or agreement as sources of claims. He mentions that Elster's "Taming chance" gives two examples of weighted lotteries in practice (fn. 10, p. 98), which is another Elster pointer.
- Games and mission examples (pp. 90): a lottery at the start of games; dangerous mission. Neither is repeated.
- Broome 1984 and 1984b page cites remain unchecked (not supplied). Wasserman (fn. 13, p. 45) gives Broome 1984b: pp. 627-9 and the "tampering with the results of a fair random process" quotation at p. 628, which fits the paper's 628-629 range.

## Addendum 3: Broome 1984, "Uncertainty and Fairness", Economic Journal 94(375), 624-632 (read from JSTOR PDF; printed pages from the running heads)

- Quotation VERIFIED verbatim: "once Michael has won the first lottery he has a perfect claim to the treatment, and Maggie has none. Consequently it is not at all fair to hold a second lottery." The sentence starts at the top of p. 629 ("For instance, in example (b)" ends p. 628), so the cite "628-629" is right.
- Context: Broome's example (a)/(b): both Michael and Maggie need haemodialysis and only one machine is available; the lottery has been held and Michael won. Action B in (b) is "to ignore the first lottery and hold another". He calls that absurd: "action A is actually strictly better than B even in the softened version (b), because ignoring the results of the first lottery is tampering with the results of a fair random process" (p. 628). This is what Wasserman's fn. 13 quotes.
- This is a *single-unit* case. It concerns re-running a lottery for the same unit between the same people. It does not address a new unit arriving later. The paper should keep its use of this passage to "a second lottery for the same unit", and make sure no sentence implies Broome rules out unit-by-unit fresh draws.
- Fn. 1, p. 628 (verbatim): "A referee has pointed out to me that it is not obviously absurd to want to delay the lottery as long [as] possible. If you were in favour of equalising expected utilities that is presumably what you would want to do." Wasserman discusses the same point. 
- "Dialysis" is Broome's example, not Vong's: this explains the paper's "dialysis and organs" slip for Vong. For Vong, use corneas (and, in passing, kidneys).
- Broome 1984 (Ethics) "Selecting People Randomly", p. 48 passage: still unchecked (PDF not supplied).

## Addendum 5: Broome 1984, "Selecting People Randomly", Ethics 95(1): 38-55 (PDF read; printed pages from the running heads)

- The Division Principle passage is VERBATIM but on printed **p. 47** (bottom of the page), not p. 48: "The good, for instance, of avoiding military service could be divided by conscripting everyone for a short time rather than a few people for a long time. If people's claims to a good are equal, then fairness requires that the good should be divided between them if it can be. That is the only way to treat them with perfect equality." The thought continues onto p. 48: "But this may be very damaging to the general good; short-term soldiers are presumably very inefficient. The damage may outweigh the value of fairness, so that it is best not to make an equal division. Furthermore, the loss in fairness can be mitigated by distributing the undivided good randomly. This achieves some fairness, though, as I have said, a fairness that is less than perfect." So change the cite to "47-48" (or 47). The paper's second use of the passage (rotation, "what dividing the food is") should carry the same page.
- The food example is Broome's, p. 48 (sec. V): "Suppose some good, say food for a week, is to be given to either of two people but for some reason it cannot be divided." That is about an *indivisible* good and *unequal* claims, and it says "if the food could be divided, they should each get some but the needier person more." So the paper's "if two people have equal claims to a quantity of food, the food 'should be divided'" mixes the p. 47 equal-claims statement with the p. 48 food example. The quotation marks cover only "should be divided", which is on p. 47 ("the good should be divided between them if it can be"). Better: "if two people have equal claims to a good that can be divided, 'fairness requires that the good should be divided between them' (Broome 1984, 47)".
- Broome adds that dividing may "be very damaging to the general good" and so may be outweighed, which supports the paper's "even when giving it all to one would do more good", but the damage-point is a qualification (the Division Principle can lose against efficiency) that the paper should keep in view when it says "requires".
- Also on p. 47: "considerations of fairness alone might sometimes be in balance... two chess players before a game have an equal claim to play white", and "If people's claims to a good are equal, it will be right to select randomly, provided the occasion is one where fairness is important enough to outweigh the damage done to the general good."
- All sources on the NOTES list are now checked against full text except: Balinski & Young (book), Hersch & Rowe 2024 (preprint only; no OUP pages), Elster 1988 (keyword-read; editor name unchecked), and Solomonic Judgements 1989.

## Addendum 6: Hersch & Rowe 2024, OUP chapter proofs (OSPP 10, pp. 186-210), read from the PDF with printed pages
Section numbers match the preprint: 1 Introduction (p. 186); 2 Abundance, Scarcity, and Bottlenecks (p. 189); 3 Scarcity and Lotteries (p. 192); 4 Bottlenecks and Queues (p. 198); 5 Complications (p. 202); 6 Conclusion (p. 206).
- (i) "we lose something that matters for fairness": VERIFIED verbatim, sec. 5, **p. 205**: "When we accept the temporal separability of goods by indexing goods to a time, we lose the distributional depth that raises the distributional problem. If goods are always indexed to time, then we lose something that matters for fairness."
