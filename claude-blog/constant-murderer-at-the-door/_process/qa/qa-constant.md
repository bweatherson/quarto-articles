# Referee report: "Constant's Key: Principles, Harm and Pretended Laws" (v3)

## Summary judgement

Reading the example through Constant's theory of principles is right, and the 1797 quotations are accurate (I checked pp. 6, 50, 56–57, 62–68, 72–78, 80–87). The central historical claim fails, though. The civil-religion note is not "of 1815", and it is not "neglected". It is already in the 1806 *Principes*, and its editor connected it to the Kant controversy in 1980. The paper also misdescribes chapter IX, misses Constant's 1806 texts on refuge, denunciation and enforced oaths, and dates to 1815 a retreat that is visible by 1806 and already traced by Freller. The philosophy may survive as explicit reconstruction; the history must be rebuilt.

## Major issues

**1. Provenance and novelty of the note.** The note is in the 1806 manuscript as Book VIII, Constant's Note A (Hofmann ed., trans. O'Keeffe, Liberty Fund 2003, p. 147): "When so-called laws demand the truth from us only to banish us, we do not owe them the truth." Hofmann's editorial note 19 says it "obviously recalls the controversy Constant raised against Kant in *Des réactions politiques*". It refers to his thesis (Hofmann 1980, t. I, Première partie, ch. 2, p. 143). "Neglected", "never connected with the exchange", "a note of 1815", "In 1815 he did" and "between 1797 and 1815" must go. The texts also differ: 1806 adds "friends" to wife and children, and the translation has "banish". The paper's "purpose" condition depends on "*proscrire*", so the author must check whether Hofmann's French reads *bannir* or *proscrire*. The footnote misdescribes the source: according to the Liberty Fund introduction, Constant left the 1815 *Principes* out of his 1818–20 *Cours* and Laboulaye added them in 1861. *Fix:* date the note to 1806, cite Hofmann, quote both versions from the *Œuvres complètes* with variants, and present the contribution as analysis, not discovery.

**2. Chapter IX is misdescribed.**
- The argument is prudential and aimed at governments. Its three theses concern the arbitrary's effects on a government's institution, on its action, and on "la sûreté des individus qui le composent" (84). "L'arbitraire n'offre aux individus aucun asyle" (87) is about members of the government, whose enemies will repay them in kind (87–88). The paper reads it as about subjects.
- "Réciprocité de devoirs" (86) is contractual reciprocity between a people and a government (institutions "ne sont que des contrats", 84), not chapter VIII's correlation of a duty with another's right. "Here duties are again correlated with rights" conflates the two, and a collective relation does not transfer to a speaker and a questioner without argument.
- "In 1797 the republican government was … the protector of the proscribed, not their pursuer" ignores two passages. Chapter IX treats the republic's own military commissions and tribunals of an III–an V as a self-perpetuating chain of the arbitrary (88–89). Chapter VII asks republicans to protect the government from "la ressource enivrante et destructive de l'arbitraire" (57).
- Fructidor (September 1797) is omitted: Constant backed a republican proscription, with renewed laws against émigrés and priests and oaths of hatred of royalty. That bears directly on a note about compelled professions.

*Fix:* describe chapter IX accurately; take the normative bridge from 1806 Book XVIII, ch. 1: the duties of individuals "diminish in proportion to the government's encroachments on aspects of individual life outside its jurisdiction" (trans. p. 397).

**3. The closest texts are missing.** Book XVIII, ch. 2 of the 1806 *Principes* (trans. pp. 401–3) uses the same phrase, "these so-called laws". Constant published it himself in the *Cours* "Additions et notes", Note V (Laboulaye 1872, I, 353–55): "Toute loi qui ordonne la délation, la dénonciation, n'est pas une loi; toute loi portant atteinte à ce penchant qui commande à l'homme de donner un refuge à quiconque lui demande asile, n'est pas une loi." Citizens may be enlisted only against crimes against life, not "artificial" offences. That is the door case with the state as pursuer. It answers the witness objection in Constant's own terms, and it makes illegitimacy turn on what the law demands, not only on title and purpose. Book VII, ch. 2 (trans. p. 104, Notes B–E): "Enforced oaths are an invitation to hypocrisy"; Scottish suspects were punished for "silence or hesitation". The note belongs to this cluster on compelled declarations, where Constant's first claim is a right to silence. *Fix:* engage these texts, and revise the two-condition reading in their light.

**4. Moving from the note to the door is reconstruction, but the paper presents it as exegesis.** In the note, the truth concerns the speaker's own beliefs and his own proscription, "dans cette circonstance". Its point is that the compeller is to blame and the lie "loin d'être un crime". To say "only the second condition has work to do", the paper must generalize "pour nous proscrire" to "in order to wrong" anyone, and no text supports that step. The hedge "I know of no evidence…" is then contradicted by the abstract and the conclusion ("His second [answer]", "Constant improved his key"). Chapter XI also cuts against the purpose condition. The executor "n'a point à rechercher si l'objet de l'ordre … mérite ou non d'être arrêté" (1872, 92–93 n.): agents judge title, not purpose. *Fix:* label the extension as reconstruction everywhere, and argue for the generalization.

**5. Chapter XI and the abandoned aspiration.** The quotations (1872, 91–95) are accurate, but the contrast with 1797 is unfair.
- 1797 leaves room for discretion. "Exclut tout arbitraire et toute incertitude" concerns the truth example only, and chapter IX distinguishes "latitude … déterminée" from the arbitrary and warns against constitutions so narrow that they "entravent tous les mouvemens que nécessitent les circonstances" (94–97).
- The retreat is explicit by 1806. Book XVIII says that "abstract, general maxims, which seem to oust personal judgment" are "useless and sterile in application" (trans. p. 398). It also says that with "the jurisdiction of the law … it is much less easy to say what constitutes a transgression" (also p. 398). This undercuts the claim that a law's being "pretended" is judged "by public criteria".

Freller (USP thesis 2020; *Quand il faut décider*, 2023) argues this trajectory, dating the turn to Fructidor. *Fix:* re-date the change, cite Freller, and address the 1806 passage on jurisdiction.

## Minor issues

- After note 1, bare page numbers are meant to cite 1797, but "(129)", "(92–93 n. 1)" and "(93)" cite 1872.
- "Nuit à autrui" is the idiom of 1789, art. 4. The Year III Declaration, which the pamphlet defends, reads "ce qui ne nuit pas aux droits d'autrui" (*Droits*, art. 2). §3 should consider whether "harm" here marks the limit of a right rather than a forecast of consequences.
- §3's objections are already in Timmermann (ch. VI nn. 9–11), with Kettner and Annen; discuss Campagna's rival repair (2003: 80). Kant's concession that the questioner is not wronged is Timmermann's n. 10.
- *Religion* 6:187 condemns professing as certain, "as before God", what one does not know. That is not a disbeliever's false avowal under threat, so "the same kind of case" overstates the parallel. Kant 8:426 (unjust compulsion) is the closer text.
- "Plus approfondie" (73) means "more deeply studied", not "more advanced".
- The 1797 OCR shows no "10 germinal" signature. Give a source for the date (e.g. *OCBC* I).
- The endnote to p. 50 quotes royalists proscribing Tennis Court oath-takers as "parjures": oaths and proscription were already linked in 1797.
- Missing literature: Benton 1982 (JHI; the closing "not expediency" answers him without naming him); Hofmann 1980; Freller 2023; Holmes 1984 on intermediate principles; Jaume 1997 on individual judgement of laws; Rosenblatt 2008, whose chapter "Constant becomes constant" the closing pun echoes. Boituzat is cited only through Timmermann.

## Recommendation
