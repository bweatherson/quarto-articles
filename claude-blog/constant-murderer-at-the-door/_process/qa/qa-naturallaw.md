# Referee report: "Constant's Key: Principles, Harm and Pretended Laws"

## Summary judgement

The paper argues that Constant's 1797 harm clause fails his own standard of "fixity". It says a neglected note from the *Principes de politique*, keyed to the legitimacy and purpose of the demand, does better. What separates Constant from Kant in the end, on its account, is the correlation of duty with right.

The reading of chapters VIII–IX is careful, and the note belongs in the debate. But the central claims do not survive:

- The note is neither unconnected with the exchange nor from 1815.
- At the door, the title-and-purpose key collapses into forfeiture.
- Its advantage in decidability does not transfer to the door.
- The concluding diagnosis is Kant's own and Timmermann's, and the paper misdescribes it.

The paper also never mentions Grotius, although its taxonomy needs the natural-law framework. And it ignores Kant's lecture-era view, which has exactly the shape of the 1815 key.

## Major issues

**1. Novelty and date of the note.** The note already appears in the 1806 *Principes de politique*, Book VIII, ch. 2, as Constant's Note A. It is printed in O'Keeffe's translation (Liberty Fund, 2003) of Hofmann's critical edition (Droz, 1980). There, editorial n. 19 says it "obviously recalls the controversy Constant raised against Kant in *Des réactions politiques*" and cites Hofmann's thesis (Première Partie, ch. 2, p. 143).

So "never connected with the exchange" is false, and the note dates from the 1806 manuscript, revised to about 1810, not from 1815. The Napoleonic setting bears on what *prétendues lois* means. I checked the English only. *Fix:* verify the French in Hofmann, re-date the note, cite Hofmann, and present the contribution as analysis rather than discovery.

**2. At the door, the 1815 key is not a third key.** The paper concedes that "at the door only the second condition has work to do". What remains is forfeiture by wrongful purpose, which the paper itself calls "close to Timmermann's paraphrase"; the difference is verbal.

- (a) **Title governs the duty to answer, not the permission to lie.** The paper's own stranger example shows that lack of title does not license lying. Title is therefore idle on the lying question everywhere. The tradition draws this line. Aquinas (ST II-II q.69 a.1): the accused need not answer a judge who exceeds the order of law, but may not lie. Kant (6:190n) separates candour (*Offenherzigkeit*) from sincerity (*Aufrichtigkeit*). The 1815 case fuses the two only because silence is penalised.
- (b) **Chapter IX's reciprocity is itself forfeiture.** "Les gouvernemens donnent les mêmes droits qu'ils prennent" (86) is talionic reciprocity, that is, forfeiture in kind.

*Fix:* recast the taxonomy in terms of rights theory, with three separate mechanisms:

- (i) **Limiting the right's object.** The 1797 clause says no one has a right to harmful truths. That is a specification, not a forfeiture.
- (ii) **Forfeiture through the holder's own conduct.**
- (iii) **Lack of standing to demand.** In Hohfeldian terms, the pretended law lacks the power to impose a duty of avowal, and the citizen has an immunity.

The paper could then make the argument it now lacks: in 1815 the forfeiting wrong is the demand itself. That answers Timmermann's objection (¶30) that forfeiture is implausible when the forfeiting wrong is unrelated and prospective.

**3. Kant's own demand-keyed view.** In the Kaehler/Collins notes (MoKae 413; cf. 27:447–8), falsification is permitted when three conditions hold: the statement is extorted by force, it is to be misused, and silence is impossible (Timmermann ¶6). That is the 1815 shape.

The Religion passage Kloocke pinpoints (6:4, not 6:187) speaks of an avowal "rechtmäßig abgefordert" (legitimately demanded). The 1797 essay frames the case as "ungerechter Zwang" (unjust compulsion) and concedes the coercer is not wronged (8:426). So the residue is not merely correlation. It is why Kant abandoned the extortion exception: why the duty to humanity survives a defect in the demand.

**4. Harm versus wrong, and Grotius.**

- (a) **Revolutionary French reads *nuire* in terms of rights.** Article 4 of 1789 glosses harm through others' enjoyment of their rights. Article 2 of the Year III Declaration, attached to the constitution the pamphlet defends, reads "ce qui ne nuit pas aux droits d'autrui". On that reading the witness objection fails, and the gap between 1797 and 1815 narrows. The paper must argue against this reading.
- (b) **Constant inverts the jurists' definition, and Grotius already mixed keys.** At 8:426 Kant attacks the jurists' definition, *mendacium est falsiloquium in praeiudicium alterius* (Wolff: "zu des andern Schaden gereichet"). Constant's clause inverts it. Grotius (III.1.11–16) already mixes grounds of harm, wrong, consent and superior authority. If I recall correctly, Pufendorf (DNG IV.1) ties veracity to the right to know. Constant's *définir le principe* moves the Grotian definition from "lie" to "duty".

**5. Fixity.**

- (a) **The 1794 letter targets subjectivity, not uncertainty.** Its complaint is that happiness "differs in the imagination of each individual", not that forecasts are uncertain. Death is not subjective in that sense.
- (b) **Determinacy is not decidability.** Separate normative determinacy (given the facts, one verdict) from epistemic accessibility. The harm clause is determinate.
- (c) **At the door, purpose and harm stand or fall together.** Both are inferred from the same evidence, and "*only* in order to wrong" is harder to establish. The 1815 case's advantage is that the law itself publishes its proscriptive purpose. That does not transfer to the door.
- (d) **The untested objection is about reliance.** Kant's objection is that one exception makes the law "schwankend und unnütz" (8:427; cf. Constant's "vacillant", 78). A legitimate questioner cannot count on a speaker who may judge his law "pretended". The 1815 key is never tested against this.
- (e) **The decidability objection is not new.** It is Michaelis's, and it is already in Timmermann (¶¶12, 47).

**6. The final claim is familiar and misdescribed.** Kant states it himself (8:425). Timmermann (¶¶24, 30) concludes in so many words that the two agree the pursuer has no right and disagree about making veracity depend on the addressee's right.

Nor is the residual dispute one of directed versus undirected duty:

- Kant's formal duty is owed "gegen jeden" (towards everyone), and the wrong is done to humanity as bearer of right (¶29). The duty is directed; only the counterpart differs.
- Constant's 1815 creditor is "the laws" ("nous ne leur devons pas"). That is an institutional counterpart close to Kant's *Rechtsquelle*. The real residue is whether truthfulness owed to the juridical order is owed to a defective one.
- The abstract says the duty is owed "to those with a right", while §1 says "*only*" to them. Kant denies only the "only".
- 6:187 concerns professing as certain what one cannot know, "even merely inner": the ethical duty Kant brackets at 8:426n, not a duty owed to whoever asks.

## The view from theories of directed duty and specification

- **Thompson and Darwall.** "Where there are no rights, there are no duties" denies every non-relational duty, including duties to oneself. Does Constant need that? Darwall's distinction between the authority to demand and the claim not to be deceived is the tool for issue 2.
- **Richardson.** Kant's contrast between *nähere Bestimmung* and *Ausnahme* is the contrast between specification and exception. Specification never promised decidability, so the paper needs a criterion for when a "to whom" clause is admissible.
- **Shiffrin.** Her "justified suspended contexts" are a modern Grotian alternative. As Timmermann reports, she notes that coercion is not salient in the murderer's enquiry (2014: 30), which bears on transferring the 1815 key to the door.

## Minor issues

- Translate *prétendues lois* as "so-called laws", and cite the critical editions.
- Kant's first objection, that a right to *truth* is "ein Wort ohne Sinn" (8:426), is never discussed.
- At 6:190n Kant writes "fordern *können*"; the paper's "must be demanded" drops the modal.
- The paper revives Kloocke's hypothesis without answering Hinz.
- Two arguments in the note need separating. "Vous seul qui le forcez" is an argument from duress, an excuse; "nous ne leur devons pas" is a justification.
- Benton 1982 and Hare 1981 are in the bibliography but uncited.

## Recommendation

**Major revision.** Once issue 1 is corrected, the contribution must rest on analysis, and issues 2, 3 and 6 currently undercut it. As it stands, the paper is not publishable in a top generalist journal.
