# Editor's assessment: "Constant's Key: Principles, Harm and Pretended Laws"

**Recommendation:** send out after three changes (§7). Not a desk reject: the textual work is careful, the 1815 note looks new to this debate, and the argument has a clear spine. But it reads as a specialist correction to one book (Timmermann 2025, cited about a dozen times, often for sources the author has not read directly), and it never says why a generalist should care.

## 1. Main claim and contribution

The claim is recoverable by the end of §1, but only from a dense roadmap built on an undefined metaphor, "key". My one-sentence version: *Constant's 1797 answer to the murderer at the door, that no one has a right to a truth that harms others, fails by his own idea of a principle as fixed and reliable; an overlooked 1815 note shows him using a better criterion, based on the illegitimacy and wrongful purpose of the demand, and that criterion isolates the real disagreement with Kant: whether truthfulness is owed only to those with a right to it.*

More than a note? Only just, and two problems shrink it further:

- **The payoff at the door is the commonplace view.** The paper concedes that at the door the political condition "has no work to do". What remains, "those who demand the truth only in order to wrong are not owed it", is close to the forfeiture "commonplace" Timmermann already attributes to Constant, and to the natural-law tradition from Grotius onward, which the paper never mentions. What does the note add beyond showing that the later Constant held it?
- **Kant made the internal critique first.** At 8:428 he says that "der gute Mann" himself abandoned the principle of truthfulness because of its danger, since he "could not discover an intermediate principle … and here none can be inserted". §3 offers a version of this argument without acknowledging Kant's.

The idea of general interest, that a principle's essence is fixity (reliability, protection against the arbitrary) rather than generality, surfaces only in the last paragraph.

## 2. Ease of following

A non-specialist will stumble on:

- **"Key"/"keyed"**, which is never defined.
- **French left untranslated or unexplained**: *Des réactions politiques*, "Des principes", "De l'arbitraire", *Principes de politique*, *Cours de politique constitutionnelle*, and "the republic of year III". "The duty is still owed, 'nous ne leur devons pas'" reads as a self-contradiction.
- **Names never identified**: Cramer, Hinz, Kloocke, Michaelis, Schaumann, Kettner and Annen, Charrière.
- **"Title"** used as a term of art.
- **Kant's distinction between juridical and ethical duties**, which §5 assumes without explaining.
- **Kant's argument, never summarised**: a "right to the truth" is "a word without sense" (8:426), truth is not a possession (8:428), harming differs from wronging, and the liar answers for the consequences.

Jumps:

1. From forced profession of one's own religious beliefs to the murderer case: three disanalogies are listed, then set aside without argument.
2. From subordinate officials to "juries who judge each case once" (uncited).
3. The *Religion* passage first appears in the conclusion.

## 3. Overselling and underselling

Overclaims:

- "The murderer at the door is Benjamin Constant's example." §4 calls it "a stock one" and traces it to Schaumann, and Cramer's note credits Michaelis with the view.
- "Kant accepts the framework." Kant calls one remark "wohldenkend und zugleich richtig" ("right-minded", rather than "well thought") and then says Constant abandoned his own maxim. Kant would hardly accept that a principle is "the general result of a certain number of particular facts".
- "Applies it to truthfulness" and "Constant improved his key between 1797 and 1815" conflict with "I know of no evidence that Constant had the 1797 exchange in mind."
- "As Constant came to admit": the admission concerns officials carrying out orders, not truthfulness.
- "It is that view, not expediency, that Kant set out to refute": nothing in the paper mentions expediency.

Check rather than hedge: "I have not been able to check whether it already appears in the unpublished *Principes* of 1806." That text has been in print since 1980 (Hofmann), and a modern critical edition should supplement Laboulaye (1872).

There is also a substantive problem. The harm clause is said to fail fixity's third aspect, application without judgement. But §4 shows that Constant gave that standard up, and Kant thought nothing could meet it (8:275). Failing an unmeetable standard is no great fault. The argument should be comparative: the harm clause needs forecasts of others' free choices; the purpose test does not.

Undersold: the fixity/generality contrast and its link to the rule of law.

## 4. Structure and proportion

The sections are roughly right, but:

- §1 needs a thesis and stakes. Its roadmap promises "five steps" across four sections, and assigns the judgement point to §5, though it is made in §4.
- §2 can lose the paragraph on Burke and Constant's opponents.
- §4 is overloaded (1797 context, chapter IX, the note, the door, witness, stranger, fixity, officials, juries). Split it into "Pretended laws" and "Judgement".
- The *Religion* comparison in §5 is the paper's most accessible point for a generalist and deserves more space.
- Missing material:
  - a summary of Kant's reply;
  - the natural-law background of the premise that duties correspond to rights;
  - Korsgaard's "The Right to Lie" (1986);
  - a paragraph on why it matters: whether one may lie to illegitimate authority (compare Shiffrin 2014).

## 5. Ten weakest sentences

1. "Then the example (74), introduced as a dilemma." → "Constant then introduces the example (74) as a dilemma."
2. "What the modification is keyed to matters. It is keyed to harm." → "Everything depends on what the modification turns on, and it turns on harm."
3. "It fails the third aspect of fixity, and fails the second through the third …" → "It fails the third test of fixity, and therefore the second: if each speaker must judge whether a truth will harm someone, no one can count on what he says."
4. "That is the standard by which his resolution of the example should be judged." → "His resolution should be judged by whether it can be applied without such judgement."
5. "Read this way, the principle Constant's later work supplies for the 1797 case is: …" → "Applied to the 1797 case, the later principle would be: we do not owe the truth to those who demand it only in order to commit a wrong."
6. "His paraphrase describes the principle Constant came to better than the one he stated." → "His paraphrase fits Constant's 1815 principle better than his 1797 one."
7. "It must not be read as making title the only source of the right, …" together with "The note's structure is a claim …" → "The principle does not make official standing a condition of being owed the truth: a stranger asking the way is still owed a true answer. The truth is ordinarily owed, and two features of a demand can defeat that claim."
8. "The duty is still owed, 'nous ne leur devons pas', …" → "The note still speaks of truth as owed ('we do not owe it to them'), so chapter VIII's pairing of duties with rights survives."
9. "The later key is better than the earlier one, but by a weaker standard …" → "The later key is better, but it meets only a weaker standard, one that accepts, with Kant, that applying a rule requires judgement."
10. "His view … was constant from 1797 to 1815, and it is that view, not expediency, that Kant set out to refute." → "What did not change was Constant's view that truthfulness is owed to those with a claim on it, and that view is Kant's real target." This also drops the pun on "constant".

## 6. Title and abstract

"Constant's Key" tells a generalist nothing; it mentions neither lying, Kant nor the murderer. Try "Who Is Owed the Truth? Constant on Principles, Harm and Pretended Laws".

The abstract is accurate, and quoting the 1815 sentence works, but it is 190 words of compressed argument resting on two undefined terms, "fixity" and "key". It should open with the question and its stakes.

## 7. Decision

Send out after revision. Required first:

1. **Answer the worry that the contribution is small.**
   - Engage Kant's internal critique (8:427–428).
   - Acknowledge the natural-law background.
   - State what the 1815 note establishes beyond the forfeiture view.
   - Make the fixity argument comparative.
2. **Secure the evidence.**
   - Check the 1806 *Principes*.
   - Cite a critical edition of the 1815 text.
   - Cite secondary and earlier sources directly rather than through Timmermann.
   - Align claims about Constant's development with the admission that the note may be unconnected to the exchange.
3. **Rewrite for a generalist reader.**
   - A new title.
   - An introduction stating the thesis and why it matters beyond Kant scholarship.
   - A summary of Kant's reply.
   - Define or drop "key", and gloss the French and the names.
   - Split §4.
