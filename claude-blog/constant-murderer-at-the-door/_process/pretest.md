# Predictability pre-test: Constant, "Des principes" (1797)

## Phase 1 (blind)

Read in full: *Des réactions politiques* (An V), ch. VIII, pp. 64–79. Skimmed: chs. I–VII, IX–X and the notes. Also read: Kant, AA 8:425–430. Page numbers are the 1797 ones. Literature is cited from memory and was not checked during this phase.

### (a) Likely theses, ranked

1. **Contextual: the case is a by-product of a political argument (p ≈ 0.24).** The lie example illustrates a Directory-era defence of principles against the "hommes à circonstances" and Burke (named at p. 81). The primary example is consent to law mediated by representation (pp. 72–73), and Kant and Constant talk past each other. *Literature:* Holmes 1984; Fontana 1991; Vincent 2011; Jaume 1997; Raynaud's 1988 edition; Kloocke 1984; Hofmann 1980.
2. **Constant as theorist of application (p ≈ 0.16).** "Principes intermédiaires" are a theory of specification (neither exception nor weighing) that anticipates middle axioms and specificationism and does better than Kant on application. *Literature:* Richardson 1990; Ross; O'Neill and Herman on judgment; Korsgaard 1986; Kant's *Gemeinspruch* on the "Mittelglied" (AA 8:275).
3. **Natural-law genealogy (p ≈ 0.13).** Constant's view that duty correlates with right, and his "droit à la vérité", restate the Grotius–Pufendorf definition of the lie as a falsehood that violates the addressee's right. Kant's "Wort ohne Sinn" and his jab at the jurists' *falsiloquium in praeiudicium alterius* (8:426) break with that tradition and with his own lectures. *Literature:* Grotius, *DJBP* III.1.11; Pufendorf, *DJNG* IV.1; Collins lectures (AA 27); Geismann & Oberer 1986; Mahon 2006ff.; Wood 2008.
4. **Constant pushed the debate into *Recht* (p ≈ 0.12).** Because Constant framed truthfulness as a duty correlative to a right, Kant answered as a jurist (note at 8:426). The essay therefore concerns imputation and declarations, not virtue. *Literature:* Paton 1954; Wood 2008; Varden 2010; Sussman; Ripstein.
5. **Who is the "philosophe allemand"? (p ≈ 0.10).** Kant, Michaelis, or hearsay? The question includes how Constant knew German ethics in 1796–97 and what the vagueness of the attribution does to the debate. *Literature:* Cramer's note and Kant's admission (8:425); Azouvi & Bourel 1991; Kloocke; Constant's years at Brunswick.
6. **Liberalism against *arbitraire* (p ≈ 0.10).** Constant's liberalism is principled, not pragmatic, and fixity of rule against *arbitraire* is its core value; the lie case shows how to keep a principle without catastrophe. *Literature:* Holmes; Rosenblatt (ed.) 2009; Kalyvas & Katznelson 2008; Todorov 1997.
7. **Epistemology of principles (p ≈ 0.08).** Constant's inductivist, Idéologue-style principle ("résultat général d'un certain nombre de faits particuliers", p. 66) collides with Kant's "a priori anerkannter, mithin apodiktischer" principle (8:429). *Literature:* Welch 1984; Azouvi & Bourel; Kloocke.
8. **Rhetorical/political reading (p ≈ 0.05).** The German philosopher fills the slot of the inept legislator (p. 72), or of the royalist "logiciens sévères" who exaggerate principles to make them inapplicable (ch. VII, pp. 57, 61). *Literature:* as for 1, plus histories of the Thermidorian press.
9. **Other (p ≈ 0.02).** For example, lying in politics (Koyré, Arendt, Derrida) or a contemporary defence of Constant (Shiffrin 2014).

### (b) My best thesis

Constant is usually cast as common sense against a rigorist, but chapter VIII shows him to be a *rival rigorist*. His conclusion, which Kant quotes approvingly, is that a principle "reconnu vrai, ne doit donc jamais être abandonné" (p. 77). He also has a definite theory of what makes something a principle: "l'essence d'un principe n'est pas d'être général, ni applicable à beaucoup de cas, mais d'être fixe" (pp. 67–68). Kant's last sentence asserts the opposite: principles bear the name only because of their *Allgemeinheit* (8:430). Kant never quotes the fixity passage, but it is the real point of disagreement. Neither man allows exceptions. Constant thinks a principle can be narrowed without loss of fixity, by "defining" it and linking it to a neighbouring principle. Kant thinks any narrowing that depends on *who is asking* is a disguised exception. The question between them is whether specification indexed to persons destroys a principle, not whether one may lie to the murderer.

Read this way, Constant's solution fails by his own standard. His intermediate principle, "nul homme n'a droit à la vérité qui nuit à autrui" (pp. 75–76), is keyed to **harm**, not to wrongdoing, and it says nothing about the assassins' intent. To apply it one must judge the circumstances (who is harmed, and how much). That is exactly the condition he calls *arbitraire*, in which "chacun est juge des circonstances" (p. 78). Chapter IX makes the stakes explicit: morality is "un assemblage de règles, sur lesquelles les individus doivent pouvoir compter mutuellement" (p. 82). A duty of truthfulness keyed to harm is not one that anyone can count on. That is Kant's argument (8:426), so his distinction between *nocere* and *laedere* (8:428) is an *internal* objection. To meet his own standard of fixity, Constant needed a principle keyed to wrongful coercion, that is, forfeiture. Sympathetic readers since Korsgaard have credited him with that principle, but he did not state it.

The excessive breadth comes from the political analogy that carries the chapter. In politics, representation mediates "nul homme ne peut être lié que par les loix auxquelles il a concouru" (p. 72) through a rule for *institutions*, not for cases. Constant transposed a method of constitutional design, where intermediate principles are rules for legislators, to individual conscience, where the agent must apply them on the spot. The murderer case is where that transposition fails. A paper centred on Constant should defend him as the better theorist of what principles are *for*, namely reliability against arbitrary power. It should then show that his own resolution of the case breaks that theory, and that Kant's reply, read carefully, says so.

### (c) Features the literature most neglects

1. **The definition of a principle (pp. 66–68).** Constant derives principles from facts by induction and makes their essence fixity rather than generality. Each gap in the chain has "qu'un seul anneau" (p. 67). This is overlooked because Kant does not quote it and anthologies reprint only the lie paragraphs or Kant's German excerpts. Yet it is the exact contrary of 8:430.
2. **The two-horned structure (p. 75).** "Si vous le rejetez, la société n'en sera pas moins détruite, car toutes les bases de la morale seront renversées." Constant shares Kant's premise that truthfulness is a foundation of society, and each says the other's view makes society impossible. The usual framing of Constant against Kant hides this common ground.
3. **A harm principle, not forfeiture (pp. 75–76).** Readers import forfeiture because they meet the case through Kant's restatement and Korsgaard's reconstruction.
4. **Constant's case lacks Kant's stipulations.** It has "assassins" (plural), "votre ami", no forced yes-or-no answer, no neighbours and no escape route. Kant adds "Aussagen, die man nicht umgehen kann" (8:426) and the friend slipping out (8:427). Kant read Cramer's German (pp. 121–25), and most commentators read Constant through Kant.
5. **The method claim (pp. 74–77).** Intermediate principles are found by *definition*, "en parcourant toute sa circonférence", and Constant calls this "une idée peut-être neuve". It is an attempt to replace judgment by a complete chain of rules, so it should be set against Kant's "Mittelglied" of judgment (8:275) and the regress at A133/B172. Historians of liberalism treat the passage as rhetoric, and moral philosophers stop reading at the lie.
6. **Kant's misdescription and the criterion both men share.** Kant says Constant "refutes" the principle (8:425) and "could not discover" an intermediate principle (8:428). Constant offers the right-correlation clause *as* that intermediate principle. Both accept that intermediate principles give "nähere Bestimmung", never "Ausnahmen" (8:430). The dispute is over classification.
7. **The surrounding chapters, and Kant's agreement on politics.** Chapter VII condemns the "exagération des principes" as "le moyen le plus infaillible de les rendre inapplicables" (p. 61), and chapter IX targets Burke and the gap between theory and practice (p. 81). Kant calls the political example "wohldenkend und zugleich richtig" and recasts it as axiom, postulate and representative "problem" (8:427–29). Their agreement on politics is rarely noted.
8. **Morality as the better-developed science (p. 73).** On Constant's account, the German philosopher regresses behind ordinary moral knowledge: the first principle "a pu être reçu sans inconvéniens" only through intermediate principles (p. 74). The case shows that isolating a principle is ruinous even where its intermediate principles are already known.

### 2. Is T1 correct? The strongest objection

T1 is right about Constant, and the passages check out. It is wrong about Kant in one respect. In 8:430, *Allgemeinheit* means **strict universality**, that is, exceptionlessness. It does not mean breadth, because the same sentence expressly allows the "nähere Bestimmung ihrer Anwendung". So "Kant: universal and general" over-reads him. The right axis is the one Kant marks when he inserts "a priori anerkannter, mithin apodiktischer" (8:429). In KrV B3–4, experience yields only "comparative Allgemeinheit (durch Induction)", which almost exactly matches Constant's "général que d'une manière relative" (p. 66).

**Strongest objection: Kant's decisive charges do not depend on any theory of what a principle is.** "Abandoned … wegen der Gefahr" (8:428) and making the duty "bedingt" and subordinate to "andern Rücksichten" (8:429) are aimed at the fact that Constant's clause is keyed to **harm** ("qui nuit à autrui"). That is where *nocere*/*laedere* does its work. Kant would reject a principle keyed to harm however universal, fixed and specific it was, for three reasons: truth is not a possession; the harm is *casus*; and the lie wrongs humanity by spoiling the *Rechtsquelle* (8:426). "Makes no distinction between persons" (8:429) is a first-order claim about to whom the duty is owed ("gegen jeden"), not a claim about what principles are. So the clash of conceptions is at most half the dispute.

There is a second problem. By Constant's own standard, a clause keyed to harm is not fixed. Applying it makes "chacun … juge des circonstances" (p. 78), and it fails chapter IX's test of reliance, that rules be ones "sur lesquelles les individus doivent pouvoir compter" (p. 82). T1 should report Constant's claim that the clause "exclut tout arbitraire" (p. 77), not endorse it. This also exposes a weakness in current readings. Cambridge's summary of Timmermann 2025, ch. VI, has Constant holding that the murderer "forfeited" his right. The 1797 text has no forfeiture, only harm to a third party.

### 3. The Hare link

The link is partly sound. Two passages support it:
- "Ce résultat est toujours pareil, toutes les fois que les combinaisons sont les mêmes" (p. 66) is close to "same features, same verdict".
- Denying that a principle must be "applicable à beaucoup de cas" is exactly Hare's point: "generality is the opposite of specificity, whereas universalizability is compatible with specificity" (*Moral Thinking*, 1981, p. 41; this is the only Hare passage I checked).

Constant's restricted principle is therefore universal but specific. There are three limits:
1. Constant's sameness clause is a **causal-regularity** thesis about "combinaisons d'existences ou d'événemens", not a logical thesis about moral predicates.
2. "Fixité" also means stability over time and something people can count on ("c'est en elle que réside toute son utilité", p. 68). That is a value of the rule of law, not a logical property.
3. The anachronism lies on Kant's side. Casting Kant as a theorist of generality is inaccurate, since he too is a theorist of universality.

Use the period's own vocabulary as the frame: strict against comparative universality, and *nähere Bestimmung* against *Ausnahme*. Bring Hare in only as a later gloss, and confirm whether the 1963 or 1972 texts make the distinction before claiming them.

### 4. Recommendation

**Combine T1 and T2, and revise T1.** The dispute is not universality against fixity, since both men demand principles without exceptions. It is over whether a specification indexed to persons counts as a *nähere Bestimmung* or as an *Ausnahme*.
- In 1797 Constant keys the specification to harm. That fails his own test of fixity, so Kant's *nocere*/*laedere* is an internal objection.
- T2 supplies the repair. In the climate of proscription that the pamphlet itself describes, the paradigm questioner is the agent of outlawry. The fixed principle Constant needed was therefore keyed to the illegitimacy of the authority demanding the truth, as in the 1815 formula.
- I could not find the 1815 quotation in open sources, and the OLL text is the 1806 *Principes*. Verify it in the *Œuvres complètes* before relying on it.

**One sentence:** Constant's murderer is a test of what makes a principle a principle (fixity, not breadth); his own harm-keyed specification fails that test, while the illegitimate-authority formula his anti-proscription politics required passes it, so Kant's reply becomes an internal critique rather than absolutism against expediency.
