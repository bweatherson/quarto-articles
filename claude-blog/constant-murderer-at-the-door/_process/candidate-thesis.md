# Candidate thesis T1 (Constant paper). Do not open this file before Phase 1 of any blind test is saved.

**Working title:** "What a Principle Is: Constant's Murderer at the Door"

## Thesis
Constant introduced the murderer at the door as a test case for a theory of what principles are. On that theory:
- a principle is "the general result of a certain number of particular facts", general "only relatively, not absolutely";
- "the essence of a principle is not to be general, nor applicable to many cases, but to be fixed" (1797, 66–67);
- "secondary principles are just as immutable as first principles", and "each circumstance only calls up the principle proper to it".

So when Constant restricts the duty of truthfulness to those who have a right to the truth, he is not making an exception to a principle. He offers a more specific principle that is just as fixed, and he claims that it "excludes all arbitrariness and all uncertainty" (76–77). He insists that a principle recognised as true "must never be abandoned".

Kant's reply grants the methodological frame: intermediate principles may contain "the closer determination of their application to cases". But he insists they may "never" contain exceptions, "because these destroy the universality on account of which alone they bear the name of principles" (AA 8:430). He also inserts "a priori recognised, hence apodictic" into Constant's maxim (8:429).

The two men therefore disagree about what a principle is:
- Kant: a principle is essentially universal and, for him, general;
- Constant: a principle is essentially fixed, with generality relative.

The murderer case is where the two conceptions part. Kant's charge that Constant "abandoned" the principle, and his objection that the duty "makes no distinction between persons", presuppose Kant's conception.

Constant's conception is close to a later distinction: *universality*, meaning no reference to individuals and same features, same verdict, as against *generality*, meaning a principle that is not specific. Hare drew this distinction; the history of the distinction needs checking. On it, Constant's restricted principle is fully universal. This exposes a tension in Kant: maxims tested by the Formula of Universal Law may be highly specific (Korsgaard 1986 on the murderer's maxim).

## Historical support to develop
- **Constant's text.** The full chapter VIII, "Des principes" (pp. 64–79 of the 1797 first edition): the definition of a principle; the chain and its links; the popular sovereignty/representation analogy; the claim of novelty ("une idée peut-être neuve").
- **Sources of the conception.** Constant's notion of a principle as the general result of facts may have Scottish-Newtonian sources (he studied at Edinburgh, 1783–85). This needs checking; do not overclaim.
- **The transmission.** Kant read the chapter through Cramer's translation. He cites S. 121–125; the definitional pages are earlier in the chapter and may be S. 117–120. Kant's reply quotes nothing from the definitional opening. Caution: we lack Cramer's text.
- **The later Constant.** In 1815 (*Principes de politique*, ch. XVII, note on Rousseau's civil religion), Constant writes: "Quand de prétendues lois n'exigent de nous la vérité que pour nous proscrire, nous ne leur devons pas la vérité." This uses the same relational "devoir" language.

## Contemporary payoff
- The case is usually read as a stand-off between an absolutist and a consequentialist. On Constant's own terms it is a dispute between two anti-arbitrariness theories of principles.
- Constant's view is a precursor of specificationism (Richardson 1990), with a correlativity constraint.
- Constant's view is not particularism. "Tout dans l'univers a ses principes" is a codifiability thesis.

## Rival T2 (fallback): the proscription reading
The paradigm questioner in 1797 is the agent of political proscription: the house-searches of the Terror ("visites domiciliaires"), outlawry ("hors la loi"), and the reactions of 1795–97. Staël's account of hiding Narbonne (1818) is an example, but it postdates the text. The 1815 note confirms the reading. On this reading Constant's "right to truth" is the moral face of limited sovereignty, and the dispute with Kant maps onto the dispute over resistance to unjust authority. Risk: Rousselière 2018 ("political legalism" versus "principled pragmatism").
