**Medina's objection does touch f_A: §5 should name the premise the Proposition forces him to defend, and answer his reply that "use-based" grading is auto-epistemic grading.**

*Where.* §5, from "That objection does not touch f_A" to "she does not."

*What is wrong.*

1. To grade a credence "at every state where it is used" is to grade it by the prior conditional on *having learned* p. That is the auto-epistemic evaluation Medina rejects (§§5, 6.1), applied to outputs that depend only on p. Your §6 concedes that f_A is meta-conditionalization for Olivia. So it is for Dana at 15: f_A({15, 16}) is certainty of 15, i.e. π(· | L_{15,16}). In both cases f_A makes the agent certain of what she learned, which Medina's §5 says is what makes a-e conditionalization "inconsistent with externalism". It does so by responding to "information that he does not have" (§6.1, quoting Greaves and Wallace): that the truth is not on the rim. f_A escapes the letter of epistemic admissibility, not its point.

2. "Credits conditionalization with its accuracy at states where it is never used" proves too much. Any conditional plan whose condition can be true without being learned is graded where it is never carried out, Medina's umbrella and poker plans included (§§6.1, 7.1). His grading follows from conditional dominance plus expected-value evaluation of unconditional plans (§7.2, Appendix). His best reply: which of these does your grading violate, and why only for agents who know their margins? His grading does not come from counting learners, so your edge-learner charge against Meehan and Zhang misses him. The two-handed Dana and impartiality paragraphs hit Meehan and Zhang, not Medina.

3. The "support function" reading of §8.3 ignores §8.5. There Medina claims the agent's choice: a non-conditionalizer fails "to implement the plan that's best by your own lights."

*Does the paper survive?* Yes, with a different claim. The Proposition stands, and Medina's omniscience-plan move (§4.2) is unavailable, since f_A is constant. So he must hold that a plan for p is graded at all p-worlds, not where it will be carried out. He must hold this even when the agent knows that a rule she can follow dominates at the latter. Say so; it is §5's sharpest point.

Then note the convergence. Medina rejects f_A for the reason your §6 does: it gives certainty in what is not learned. His epistemic admissibility is a knowledge constraint in decision-theoretic dress, and it fixes conditionalization for every prior, whereas §6 leaves slack. Either claim him as an ally (the constraint on conditions, not accuracy, does the selecting) or say why his derivation is accuracy at work.
