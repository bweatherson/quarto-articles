## 1. Verdict: PARTLY ANTICIPATED

**Already in print, or standard:**
- **The headline, that self-binding works through other people rather than on the self.** Elster says this descriptively [MEM]. Long calls operators "enlistees to enforce the Ulysses pact" (2023, p. 427). Tester X predicted it unprompted.
- **The evidential reading of directives.** It is standard for incapacitated patients (MCA 2005 s.4). For self-binding directives, the legal position is that they "should inform but not fetter such decision makers" (Stephenson et al. 2026, pp. 333–4).
- **The informational reading of self-exclusion specifically.** Long states it and rejects it in print: self-exclusion "may simply exist to allow problem gamblers to self-identify for exclusion" (2023, p. 434). His ground is that the UK has no forced exclusion for gamblers who do not self-exclude, which "surely refutes" that view (p. 435).
- **Childbirth analgesia as a precommitment example.** It is old: Schelling; Elster citing Christensen-Szalanski 1984 [MEM]. It is also discussed in clinical ethics (Ben 2019).

**Not found anywhere:**
- the specific mechanism: testimony addressed to a third party, whose force is that party's independent duty not to exploit a vulnerability now known to it;
- the claim that this is sufficient even against a competent later self;
- the gambling/epidural minimal pair;
- the three predictions: exit asymmetry, refund versus forfeiture, and the epidural divergence.

No source makes the anti-exploitation duty the whole story. Nothing turned up under "self-testimony" or "self-binding as delegation" in this sense.

## 2. Closest prior works

1. **Long, B., "Theorising Gambling Self-Exclusion Agreements: The Inadequacy of Procedural Autonomy", *Canadian J. Law & Jurisprudence* 36 (2023): 407–35, open access [PART: sections I and III–VI read; II skimmed].**
   - Main claim: procedural autonomy cannot favour t1 over t2. Hierarchical, historical (Christman, Levy) and resolution-preference (Davis) accounts fail either through regress or because operators cannot tell which preference is authentic (pp. 426–9).
   - So "self-exclusion can only be based on a normative position" (p. 434), and "value-partiality is therefore an inevitability" (p. 435).
   - He considers the self-identification reading in one paragraph (pp. 434–5) and treats it as consequentialist forced exclusion, on which "the self-excluder's consent would be irrelevant" (p. 434). He dismisses it on the UK point above.
   - He never considers an operator's duty arising from what it knows, exploitation, exit terms, or remedies.
   - **This is the single closest source.** It names the rival T3 must displace, and it raises in print the objection T3 must answer.
2. **Long, B., *Towards a Normative Theory of Gambling Self-Exclusion Agreements*, PhD thesis, UCC, 2024 [ABSTRACT; embargoed to 2030].**
   - Positive view: self-exclusion is "a form of opt-in paternalism".
   - This is the paternalist model in its most developed form, which T3 must distinguish itself from. Only the abstract is accessible.
3. **Franklin-Hall, A., "Binding the Self: The Ethics of Ulysses Contracts", *Ethics* 134 (2023): 57–88 (Crossref gives these pages; the author's page says 57–76) [ABSTRACT].**
   - It asks "why does A's will at t2 lack the authority it had at t1?"
   - Answer: the t1 will has authority only insofar as it is "a better expression of the values" still attributable at t2.
   - This is a conditional precedent-authority view, the top-venue foil. T3 denies the premise of the question: the t2 will keeps its authority, and the operator's duties do the work.
   - The abstract does not show whether Franklin-Hall discusses third-party duties or evidential readings. **Obtain the paper.**
4. **Stephenson, Scholten, Kane & Owen, "Ulysses looks back", *Irish J. Psych. Med.* 43 (2026): 333–42 [PART].**
   - Legally, a self-binding directive should "inform but not fetter" decision-makers (pp. 333–4).
   - The authors themselves argue for the "primacy" of the well self, which is the opposite of T3.
   - It gives T3 its psychiatric data point but not its thesis.
5. **Hallich, "Selbstbindung und medizinischer Paternalismus", *Ethik in der Medizin* c. 2011 [ABSTRACT].**
   - Following a Ulysses instruction is paternalism, justified by anticipated later consent.
   - It shares T3's denial of t1 authority but grounds the arrangement in consent, not in duties arising from what the third party knows.
6. **Other related work.**
   - Sun, *Phil. Imprint* 2025 [ABSTRACT]: consent must be revocable, because one lacks authority to deny one's future self protection of bodily integrity.
   - Schaber, talk, "Consent to Ulysses Contracts" [ABSTRACT]: prior consent survives only if the person is incompetent to withdraw it.
   - Spellecy 2003 [ABSTRACT]: enforcement is weak paternalism.
   - Davis 2004 [PART, via Long's summary]: the resolution preference.
   - Walker 2012 [not accessed].
   - Gremmen et al. 2008, *JME* [MEM]: "a matter of good care", care-ethical.
   - Dworkin, *Life's Dominion*, and Dresser [MEM]: the authority-versus-interests debate over directives for incapacitated patients.
   - None of these takes T3's route.
7. **Kakavas v Crown Melbourne [2013] HCA 25 [ABSTRACT, Wikipedia summary].**
   - This is a legal analogue of T3's mechanism: the doctrine of unconscionable dealing requires knowing exploitation of a special disadvantage.
   - The High Court found no special disadvantage and no knowing victimisation.
   - It also endorsed the view that, absent legislation, a casino has no general duty to protect gamblers from themselves.
   - Calvert v William Hill (cited by the scout) is the English counterpart.

## 3. What a paper must add or answer to be new

1. **Answer Long pp. 434–5 directly.** He has already canvassed the informational reading and rejected it on the ground that the UK lacks forced exclusion. T3 needs one of two replies:
   - knowledge-triggered duties do exist without self-report (the UK Gambling Commission's customer-interaction code [MEM, verify LCCP SR 3.4.3]), so his premise is false; or
   - anti-exploitation duties forbid serving a known vulnerable customer, not refusing to exclude one, so forced exclusion is not implied.
2. **Distinguish T3 from Long's "opt-in paternalism" and from Franklin-Hall's value-continuity view.** Say which cases each gets wrong.
3. **Make the epidural divergence non-evaluative.** The best candidate found here is structural: exploitation requires that the third party gain from the vulnerability. The casino profits from the loss; the anaesthetist does not profit from the patient's pain. If that does the work, credibility judgements drop out, and the paternalism objection is avoided rather than answered. It changes the thesis, though: the duty, not the testimony, carries the load.
4. **Shore up the exit-asymmetry prediction.** A cooling-off delay is predicted by procedural-safeguard views too (Spellecy; tester Y's #2). T3 must also say why a later "I have recovered" does not defeat the earlier testimony. One candidate: the earlier testimony is a higher-order defeater for reports made in the predicted state. The paper must then show that this is not the Ulysses model in disguise.
5. **Verify, then argue for, the refund/forfeiture facts.** The Australian refund ruling (possibly the NT decision "Luxbet v S" surfaced in search) and the New Jersey forfeiture rule are scout claims not checked here. Then show that opt-in paternalism does not also predict refund.
6. **Address the statutory character of the GAMSTOP duty.** It is conditioned on registration, not on knowledge.

## 4. Are the testers' objections fatal?

- **"Long 2023 covers it."** No. Long argues *against* procedural autonomy and *for* a substantive normative basis. He does not propose T3. But he states and rejects its nearest cousin in one paragraph, so the paper must engage him explicitly. This is serious but answerable.
- **The paternalism smuggle (both testers).** This is the most serious objection, because Long's central claim is exactly that value-partiality is unavoidable. As T3 is currently built ("pain is a reason, craving is not"), the objection lands. It is not fatal if the divergence is re-grounded structurally, as in 3.3 above.
- **Fresh testimony defeats old testimony (X).** Serious for the exit-asymmetry prediction, which is the weakest of the three.
- **The duty is statutory, so this is paternalism one step removed (Y).** Moderate. It needs the claim that the statute codifies a pre-legal duty arising from knowledge, with Calvert (the duty arose on acceptance) as support. Kakavas cuts both ways.

Overall: not fatal. The novelty is in the mechanism and the predictions, not in the headline. The paper is viable only if it opens against Long and Franklin-Hall rather than against Elster and Dworkin.
