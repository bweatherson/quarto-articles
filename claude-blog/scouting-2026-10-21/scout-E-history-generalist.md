## 1. Why it is all right to stop grieving: Chrysippus's "fresh opinion"

**Question.** Grief fades, and that seems all right. But the loss does not fade. So what, if anything, makes diminishing grief fitting rather than a lapse?

**Candidate thesis.** The reasons for grief expire because grief's object is the loss *as fresh* (a change in one's world not yet absorbed), not the standing fact of absence; freshness is a tensed feature of the loss itself, so grief fades for the same reason relief and surprise fade, and the puzzle of diminishing grief rests on misidentifying grief's object.

**Core argument or case.** The Stoic definition of distress is "a fresh (*prosphatos*/*recens*) opinion that something bad is present" (DL 7.111; Cicero, *Tusc.* 3.75, 4.14) [MEM; Cicero 3.75 and the "recens" condition confirmed via Englert 2017]. Chrysippus himself raised the modern puzzle: why does distress slacken with time when the judgement that the thing is bad remains? His answer: what lapses is the *freshness*, not the evaluation; Cicero's Artemisia keeps her grief only by keeping the loss fresh (*Tusc.* 3.74–75) [PART]. Galen reports Chrysippus confessing he cannot fully explain the slackening, and Posidonius mocking him (*PHP* 4.7) [MEM, INCOMPLETE]. The contemporary debate reproduces the puzzle without the resource.
Minimal pair: (A) Ann learns today that her mother died ten years ago; (B) Beth's mother died ten years ago and Beth has known throughout. Same loss, same temporal distance. Fitting grief: intense for Ann, faded for Beth. The hardline view (Howard 2022) gets B wrong; temporal-distance partiality (Bykvist, as discussed by Marušić) gets A wrong; Moller's "no fit, functional reset" view gives up on both. Freshness-for-the-subject explains both, and also Marušić's Clive Wearing case (grief recurring afresh each time the loss is news again). Supporting resource the debate also ignores: Prior's "Thank goodness that's over" shows a familiar emotion whose reason is a tensed fact that lapses without any underlying fact changing (Merlo, PPR 2025, on tensed relief [ABSTRACT]). The lasting attitude to the standing absence (sorrow, missing, continuing bonds) is a different attitude, and Howard is right that *it* need never fade.

**The obvious answer.** Either "we adapt; it's a healing process" (process views) or "the fading is arational, a functional reset" (Moller). The thesis is neither: no process need complete and nothing arational happens; the reason itself is tensed. It also rejects Marušić's explicit claim that grief's object is "her being dead, rather than the event of her dying."

**Closest prior work.**
- Marušić, "Do Reasons Expire? An Essay on Grief," *Phil. Imprint* 2018 [PART via summary]; *On the Temporality of Emotions*, OUP 2022 [ABSTRACT]. Considers hardline, temporal partiality, process, grief-as-work; rejects all; "double vision." No Stoics, no freshness.
- Moller, "The Rationality of Emotions Across Time," *Dialogue* 2025 [PART via summary]. Sceptical solution; dismisses the surprise analogy because surprise "does not involve a persistent underlying fact." No Stoics.
- Howard, "Forever Fitting Feelings," *PPR* 2022 [ABSTRACT]: fitting feelings stay fitting forever.
- Na'aman, "The Rationality of Emotional Change: Toward a Process View," *Noûs* 2021 [MEM]; Mason, "Emotional Fittingness over Time," *Ethics* 2026 [ABSTRACT]: motivational view; Ratcliffe, Richardson & Millar, *JAPA* 2022 [PART via summary]; Cholbi, *Grief* 2022 [MEM]; Friedman, "A Permissive View of Fitting Emotional Change" (PhilArchive) [title only].
- Graver, *Stoicism and Emotion* 2007, on *prosphatos* [MEM, INCOMPLETE: her reading of "fresh" as "still in force" rather than "recent" must be addressed].

**Generalist appeal.** Everyone who has grieved has wondered whether stopping is a betrayal.

**Biggest risk.** Moller's objection, generalised: "You have just written time into the object. Why is the object 'recent loss' rather than 'loss'?" The paper must earn the tensed object with the minimal pair and the praemeditatio cases (foreseen loss grieves less, *Tusc.* 3.28–31 [MEM]), not stipulate it. Secondary risk: being read as commentary on a small literature.

**Confidence.** Medium.

## 2. In doubt, judge well: Aquinas on judging persons and the doxastic-wronging debate

**Question.** When the evidence about someone's character is inconclusive, what should you believe about them? Can what you believe, or decline to believe, wrong them?

**Candidate thesis.** Judgements of a person's character fall under justice, not under the epistemology of belief: on inconclusive evidence one is required to believe well of the person while remaining free to take precautions in action, because the cost of error falls on the person judged rather than on the judge; so the correct norm is a directional presumption, not a stakes-raised threshold (moral encroachment) and not neutral suspension.

**Core argument or case.** Aquinas, *ST* II-II q.60 aa.3–4 [PART, via full-article summary]. (i) Suspicion is "evil thinking on slight indications," with three degrees: doubting a person's goodness (venial), holding their wickedness certain (grave, because it involves contempt), condemning (injustice). (ii) Doubts are to be interpreted for the best. Reply 1, the asymmetry: it is better to err often by thinking well of a bad man than rarely by thinking ill of a good one, because the second error injures someone and the first injures no one. Reply 2, things vs persons: in judging things nothing is at stake for the thing, so judge by the probabilities; in judging persons their standing is at stake, so judge them good unless there is evident proof. Reply 3, supposition vs determination: one may *take the worst for granted* in choosing a remedy (precaution) without *determining* the worst as a verdict.
Minimal pair: two shopkeepers with the same thin evidence about a customer. One believes the customer honest and watches the till. The other suspends judgement, or believes the customer a thief, and does nothing. Encroachment and suspension views rank these the wrong way round; the action-based critics (Gardiner, Enoch & Spectre) say the only wrong is in the watching. The presumption view says the first shopkeeper does nothing wrong and the second does. This also dissolves the new dilemma in the debate: if suspension can wrong (Schroeder 2025, Ranalli), raising thresholds offers no escape; a directional presumption does.

**The obvious answer.** "Proportion belief to evidence; let morality govern action, not belief" (evidentialism), or "the stakes raise the bar for the damaging belief, so suspend" (encroachment). The thesis reverses the allocation: belief is where the favour is owed, action is where caution is permitted.

**Closest prior work.**
- Basu & Schroeder, "Doxastic Wronging," 2019; Basu, "The Wrongs of Racist Beliefs," 2019; Basu, "A Tale of Two Doctrines," 2023 [MEM/ABSTRACT].
- Schroeder, "Agnostic Wrongs and Pragmatic Disencroachment," *PPR* 2025 [title only; INCOMPLETE]. Ranalli, "Suspensive Wronging," forthcoming [ABSTRACT]: suspension can wrong. Quanbeck, "Belief, Blame, and Inquiry," *Phil Studies* 2023 [ABSTRACT + notes]: suspension wrongs by opening inquiry.
- Stroud, "Epistemic Respect," *PPR* 2025 [ABSTRACT]: a duty to believe anyone's testimony absent reasons against. Closest sibling, but about believing what people *say*, not thinking well of what they *are*; no asymmetry argument.
- Williams, "Epistemic Charity Examined," *PQ* 2026 [ABSTRACT]: charity ("most favourable reading the evidence allows") faces a triviality problem unless the mind is fragmented. The persons/things restriction and the supposition/determination split are a different repair.
- Preston-Roedder, "Faith in Humanity," *PPR* 2013; Stroud 2006 and Keller 2004 on partiality to friends [MEM]: optional virtues or friendship-restricted, not a general duty of justice.
- Ferzan, "#BelieveWomen and the Presumption of Innocence" [ABSTRACT; INCOMPLETE]; Bolinger on the same [title only].

**Generalist appeal.** "Judge not" is a norm everyone has been told; whether it is a norm of belief or only of conduct is a real and open question.

**Biggest risk.** Two-person cases: believing well of the accused seems to mean believing ill of the accuser. The paper must show the presumption is symmetric across persons and so directs one to the reading that impugns neither character (honest error) until evidence is manifest, and must say what "manifest" means without collapsing into a threshold view. Also: the thesis coincides with folk wisdom, so the contribution has to be the justification structure, which a hostile reader may call exegesis.

**Confidence.** Medium-low to medium.

## 3. Concealment as a wrong of its own: Cicero's Rhodes case against the saying/not-saying asymmetry

**Question.** Is keeping silent, so that someone remains ignorant of something to their disadvantage and your gain, a wrong of the same kind as lying to them?

**Candidate thesis.** Concealment (*celare*: wanting those whose interest it is to know to stay ignorant, for your own advantage, of what you know) is wrong for the same reason lying is wrong, namely the exploitation of another's ignorance for gain; so the asymmetry between saying and not saying that organises the lying/misleading debate carries no moral weight of its own.

**Core argument or case.** Cicero, *De Officiis* III.50–57 [MEM; INCOMPLETE]: a merchant brings grain to famine-struck Rhodes knowing more ships are coming. Diogenes of Babylon: silence is not concealment; he need not tell. Antipater of Tarsus: he must, since the buyer's interest is his. Cicero sides with Antipater and gives the definition above (III.57), adding the house-with-defects case (III.54–55) and Roman law requiring sellers to declare known defects (III.65–67). The modern law-and-economics answer (Kronman 1978: deliberately acquired information need not be disclosed) and contract law (*Laidlaw v. Organ*, 1817, same facts) side with Diogenes [MEM].
Minimal pair: the merchant who says "no other ships are coming" (lie), the one who says "the harbour has been empty for weeks" (true but misleading), and the one who says nothing. The lying/misleading literature spends its effort on the gap between the first two. Cicero's definition puts all three on one side of the line that matters (exploiting ignorance for gain) and puts the merchant who stays silent because the information is not his to give, or because the buyer has no interest in it, on the other.

**The obvious answer.** "Silence is not deception; absent a special duty (fiduciary, professional), there is no wrong in not volunteering information." The thesis denies that any special duty is needed: the wrong is constituted by the combination of the other's interest in knowing and one's own advantage from their ignorance.

**Closest prior work.**
- Fallis, "Lying and Omissions," *Oxford Handbook of Lying* 2018 [ABSTRACT]: silence can deceive when it functions as communication (Gricean). Narrower: Cicero's merchant communicates nothing.
- Saul, *Lying, Misleading, and What Is Said* 2012; Stokke, *Lying and Insincerity* 2018; Viebahn, "The Lying–Misleading Distinction," *J Phil* 2021; Berstler, "What's the Good of Language?" *Ethics* 2019 [all MEM]: the assertion-centred debate.
- "Non-Epistemic Deniability" and "Exploiting Ambiguity," *Mind* 2026 [titles only; INCOMPLETE]; Navarro, "Secrecy: An Epistemological Account," *Noûs* 2026 [ABSTRACT]: secrets as intentional disruptions of the flow of knowledge, with no interest/advantage conditions.
- Shiffrin, *Speech Matters* 2014 [MEM]: duties of sincerity, not disclosure. Kronman 1978; Strudler 1995 [MEM].
- Search for recent philosophy on non-disclosure found mainly medical ethics; INCOMPLETE.

**Generalist appeal.** Everyone has kept quiet to their own advantage and wondered whether that was as bad as lying.

**Biggest risk.** (a) Possible overlap with "Persuasion in Daylight" if that paper concerns transparency; not checked. (b) Reads as business ethics unless the Rhodes case is kept as a case about ordinary honesty. (c) The economic incentive objection (Kronman) must be answered without the paper becoming a reply to law-and-economics. (d) Priority check incomplete.

**Confidence.** Low to medium.
