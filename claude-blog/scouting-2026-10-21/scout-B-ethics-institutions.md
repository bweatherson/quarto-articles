# Scout B: ethics and institutions (21 Oct 2026)

Method. I started from institutional practices I know (gambling regulation, criminal appeals, apology statutes, mental-health law) and asked which established theory handles each badly. Every practice below is verified against a primary or near-primary source this session. Priority searches were run on each thesis in its own vocabulary (web search, Crossref, PhilPapers/PhilArchive pages, publisher pages). Marks: [READ] read in full this session; [PART] read in part or via a detailed summary of the text; [ABSTRACT] abstract or review only; [MEM] from memory, not re-checked this session.

Dead ends checked and dropped: credit for time served (Kolber 2013; Ferzan draft c. 2022; Donelson 2022: fully occupied, including the compensation reading); "already punished enough" (Husak 1990; Donelson 2022); victims' duties to wrongdoers (Gardner 2018 vs Fabre 2023 [PART]); mass exoneration under the Post Office Act 2024 (collapses into the predictable windfall-vs-wrong asymmetry); the "not proven"/acquitted-conduct cluster (too close to Duff 2013 and to the series' closure paper).

## 1. Self-binding is self-testimony

**Question.** Can you bind your future self? Gambling self-exclusion is the largest live experiment in self-binding: over half a million people on GAMSTOP alone, with operators legally required to refuse them. What, if anything, makes the earlier request authoritative over the later, competent, insistent one?

**Candidate thesis.** Self-binding arrangements do not bind the self at all; a self-exclusion is testimony about oneself, addressed to a third party, and its whole force is the force of that party's independent duty not to exploit a vulnerability it now knows about.

**Core argument / cases.**
- *Minimal pair.* (a) A self-excluded gambler walks into a casino, competent and insistent; she is refused, by law. (b) A woman in labour who wrote "do not give me an epidural even if I beg" begs; she is given the epidural, in every maternity unit I know of. Both are precommitments against a predicted change of mind in a predicted state. The Ulysses model (earlier self has authority) cannot distinguish them; the paternalist model distinguishes them only by smuggling in a judgment that gambling is bad and analgesia good. The testimony model distinguishes them cleanly: the gambler's statement ("my later request will be a symptom") is credible self-report that makes serving her exploitation; the labouring woman's statement ("my later request will be unreliable") is not credible, because pain is a reason for analgesia, not a corrupter of the request.
- *Exit asymmetry.* GAMSTOP terms [READ]: no deactivation before the minimum period; after it, exclusion continues "for up to a further seven years" until the user asks, then a 24-hour delay. Entry is instant, exit is slow and never automatic. On the Ulysses model this is unexplained (the earlier self chose six months, not seven years). On the testimony model it is predicted: you can retract an assertion, but the hearer cannot un-know what you told them.
- *Who owes what.* Calvert v William Hill [2008] EWHC 454 (Ch), affd [2008] EWCA Civ 1427 [PART]: the duty of care arose from the operator's acceptance of the self-exclusion; breach found; no damages because he would have ruined himself elsewhere. The duty is the operator's, triggered by notice. Australia: an operator was ordered to refund a self-excluded customer's losses. New Jersey 2023: self-excluded players' winnings are forfeited to the state. The testimony model says the refund is right and the forfeiture a category error (it treats self-exclusion as a self-imposed status, like minority).
- *Psychiatry.* Stephenson, Scholten, Kane & Owen 2026, *Irish J Psych Med* 43: 333–42 [PART]: "Jessica" made a self-binding directive; when manic she repudiated it; clinicians admitted her under the Mental Health Act, and the directive "was rarely referenced". The directive did no normative work; it informed a decision the clinicians had independent authority to make. That is the general pattern.

**The obvious answer.** Precedent autonomy (Ulysses: the earlier self had the standing to decide), or soft paternalism (the later request is addiction-driven), or consent (Walker 2012: the later treatment counts as consented to). My thesis denies all three any role: the earlier self has no authority over a competent later self, and nothing in the practice requires that it does. The practice is explained entirely by what third parties may do once told.

**Closest prior work.**
- Long, "Theorising Gambling Self-Exclusion Agreements: The Inadequacy of Procedural Autonomy", *Canadian J Law & Jurisprudence* 36 (2023) [PART, ~90%]. Argues procedural autonomy cannot justify enforcing t1 over t2; a substantive judgment that abstinence is better is needed. Does not consider the operator's duty, the epistemic role of the register, or exit asymmetry. The thesis above is the alternative he does not canvass.
- Walker, "Ulysses Contracts in Medicine", *Law and Philosophy* 31 (2012) [ABSTRACT]: modified Ulysses contracts make later treatment consented-to; should be legally enforceable.
- Spellecy, "Reviving Ulysses Contracts", *Kennedy Inst. Ethics J* 13 (2003) [ABSTRACT]: enforcement is weak, not strong, paternalism.
- Hallich (Ethik in der Medizin, c. 2011) [ABSTRACT]: justification by anticipated later consent.
- Elster, *Ulysses Unbound* (2000) [MEM]: self-binding often works by delegation to others; the *normative* claim that delegation is the whole story is not there as far as I recall. Must be re-read before committing.
- The "evidentiary" reading of advance directives for *incapacitated* patients is standard (MCA 2005 s.4). The novelty is applying it to a *competent* later self and showing it is sufficient.

**Generalist appeal.** "Can you bind your future self?" is a question every philosopher recognises (Hobbes, Parfit, Elster); the answer "no, you can only tell others about her" is one line.

**Biggest risk.** A screener reads it as the paternalism debate with a gambling example, or as a notational variant ("evidence model") of a known view. The defence is the predictions (exit asymmetry, refund vs forfeiture, the epidural divergence), which none of the existing views makes.

**Confidence.** Medium.

## 2. The vigilante state: desert is not a title

**Question.** When a conviction is quashed, does the prisoner's possible guilt give the state any claim to the years it took? Sam Hallam served seven years for murder; Victor Nealon seventeen for rape; both convictions were quashed as unsafe; both were refused compensation because they could not show "beyond reasonable doubt" that they did not commit the offence (CJA 1988 s.133(1ZA), as amended 2014). The Grand Chamber upheld the refusals 12–5 (Nealon and Hallam v UK, 11 June 2024) [PART].

**Candidate thesis.** Factual guilt gives the state no title to hardship imposed without a valid conviction: once the conviction is quashed, the state stands to the prisoner as a vigilante does, and owes compensation for the whole term regardless of guilt, unless a fresh conviction reaches back and claims the time.

**Core argument / cases.**
- *The state already concedes the time was its to account for.* If Hallam had been retried and reconvicted, the seven years would have been credited against the new sentence: North Carolina v Pearce (1969) makes this constitutionally mandatory in the US as "punishment already exacted"; the Criminal Appeal Act 1968 Sch 2 does the same in England [MEM, to verify]. So the state counts the years as punishment when it secures a new title and as nothing when it does not. The thesis removes the inconsistency: the years are the state's wrong until a valid conviction retroactively claims them, and credit is compensation-in-kind.
- *Vigilante analogy.* A private person who locks up a murderer for seven years, correctly believing him guilty, has falsely imprisoned him; the murderer's guilt does not reduce the damages. The state without a valid conviction has no more title than the vigilante; desert is a reason for punishment through a licensed process, not a licence to keep what was taken without one.
- *Minimal pair.* Two men, both guilty, both convicted on fabricated evidence, both quashed. One is retried and reconvicted: seven years credited. The other cannot be retried (the fabrication was the whole case): seven years, nothing. Current law, and the "harsh treatment counts" view (Kolber), cannot say why the same years are punishment in one case and a non-event in the other.
- *Adams* [2011] UKSC 18 [MEM]: Lord Phillips's four categories of quashed conviction; the majority compensated the first two, the dissent only the first. The thesis says all four, for a reason no judge gave.

**The obvious answer.** "No: only the innocent are wronged by punishment; the guilty exoneree is owed at most a remedy for the procedural defect" (forfeiture theory, and the UK rule). Or "yes, because the presumption of innocence revives, so treat him as innocent" (the dissenters). Mine is neither: compensation is owed even if we *know* he is guilty, because the ground is the state's lack of title, not his innocence.

**Closest prior work.**
- Wellman, *Rights Forfeiture and Punishment* (OUP 2017) [PART via NDPR review]: wrongdoers forfeit rights against punishment by anyone; Hitler has "no grounds to object" to a vigilante. The thesis is a direct counterexample drawn from practice; Wellman's "relatedness" problem and his punishment/hard-treatment distinction are the pressure points.
- Kolber, "Against Proportional Punishment", *Vand. L. Rev.* 66 (2013) [PART]; Ferzan, "Rethinking Credit for Time Served" (draft, c. 2022) [PART]; Donelson, "Natural Punishment" (2022) [ABSTRACT]: all on pretrial detention credit, including "time transformations" (Donelson) and Kolber's objection to retroactive recharacterisation. None addresses the quashed-conviction case or compensation of the possibly guilty.
- Duff, Farmer, Marshall & Tadros, *The Trial on Trial* vol. 3 (2007) [MEM]: punishment after an unfair trial lacks legitimacy even for the guilty. The compensation consequence is not drawn there, as far as I recall.
- Duff, "Who Must Presume Whom to Be Innocent of What?", *NJLP* 42 (2013) [not accessed; paywalled].
- Nealon and Hallam v UK, GC 2024; Strasbourg Observers commentary [PART].

**Generalist appeal.** "Can the guilty be wronged by their punishment?" is a question about desert and authority that any moral philosopher has a view on; the practice makes the state answer it both ways.

**Biggest risk.** The "murderer gets a million" bullet. The reply (the state can retry if it can prove guilt; otherwise "factually guilty" is a God's-eye label it cannot use; the vigilante case shows we already accept the principle) must be in the paper, not deferred. Second risk: judged as legal theory rather than philosophy. Mitigation: frame as a counterexample to forfeiture theory.

**Confidence.** Medium.

## 3. Sorry is not a confession

**Question.** Does the sincerity of an apology depend on what it costs the apologiser? Apology statutes (Compensation Act 2006 s.2; Apologies (Scotland) Act 2016 [PART]; Ontario Apology Act 2009 [MEM], which covers apologies "whether or not" they admit fault) bar apologies from being used as evidence of liability. Critics say this subverts apology by severing it from accountability.

**Candidate thesis.** An apology is a single-addressee act, and its sincerity is a matter of address rather than cost: a statute that bars evidential use does not cheapen an apology but stops the same words from being conscripted into a second speech act (an admission) addressed to a party the apologiser never spoke to.

**Core argument / cases.**
- *Minimal pair.* A surgeon says to a patient, at the bedside, "I was negligent, I am sorry, and we are changing the protocol". The same words are read out in court as an admission. The first is an apology; the second is a confession extracted from it. Scotland protects the undertaking to prevent recurrence as part of the apology; England protects only "an offer of treatment or other redress" and the bare apology. The thesis explains why the Scottish scope is right: the undertaking is addressed to the patient.
- *The two-audience point is already modelled* by economists as a signalling problem (Varela, working paper c. 2021 [PART], two audiences, plaintiff and judge; apology laws "cheapen" apologies). The philosophical thesis contradicts the economists' moral: cost is not what makes an apology sincere, address is. Evidence: victims report protected apologies as satisfying (Robbennolt 2003 [MEM]); the confessional seal and the "without prejudice" rule are the same device in other settings.

**The obvious answer.** "Protected apologies are cheap talk, so the laws subvert apology" (Taft 2000; Smith 2014), or "a pragmatic trade-off that buys more apologies at the price of some insincere ones". Mine says the laws *improve* apologies, and for a reason about speech acts, not about incentives.

**Closest prior work.**
- Helmreich, "Does 'Sorry' Incriminate?", *Cornell JLPP* 21 (2012) [PART]: apologies do not imply fault, so protect them (FRE 409 model). Closest predecessor; differs in mechanism (he says the words never admitted fault; I say they do, to the victim, and that is not an admission to the court). His view cannot explain protecting *fault-admitting* apologies, which Ontario does.
- Smith, *Justice Through Apologies* (CUP 2014) [MEM]: apology laws encourage less-than-categorical apologies. Taft, "Apology Subverted", *Yale LJ* 109 (2000) [MEM]. Helmreich, "The Apologetic Stance", *PPA* 43 (2015) [MEM].

**Generalist appeal.** Speech-act theory, sincerity, and what an apology is are of interest well beyond legal philosophy.

**Biggest risk.** Reads as an intervention in a small Smith/Helmreich debate; a screener could say the thesis is Helmreich with a new mechanism. The paper has to make "sincerity is address, not cost" the headline and apology law the test case.

**Confidence.** Low–medium.

## Ranking

1. **Self-binding is self-testimony.** Best fit to the "practice the theory handles badly" shape; vivid minimal pair; predictions the rival views do not make; the one-line answer to a question everyone recognises.
2. **The vigilante state.** Sharpest thesis and clearest foil (forfeiture theory), with the state's own credit practice as the lever; risk is that it reads as legal theory, and the bullet must be bitten in the open.
3. **Sorry is not a confession.** Sound, but the debate is small and the closest predecessor is close.
