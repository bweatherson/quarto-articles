## 1. Grief runs on two clocks

**Question.** Is it ever unfitting to go on grieving? The death never stops having happened, so why isn't grief fitting forever?

**Candidate thesis.** Grief is not fitting forever. Its other-regarding part, sorrow for what the dead person is missing, is fitting only until the date on which they would otherwise have died. So the premise both sides of the "forever-fitting" debate share is false: grief's object does not lie wholly in an unchanging past.

**Core argument and case.**
1. **Two components.** Grief has an other-regarding component (sorrow on the dead person's behalf) and a self-regarding one (one's own loss). We grieve more for a death at 30 than one at 95 even when our own loss is the same.
2. **The fitting-maker.** The other-regarding component is fitting only if, and while, the dead person is badly off in being dead.
3. **Timing.** On subsequentism about death's badness (Bradley), death is bad for a person at the times when they would have been living. So that fitting-maker is a *present* fact until the counterfactual death date, and then it is gone.
4. **Minimal pair.** Twin brothers die at 40 in the same crash. The autopsy shows that one had a cancer that would have killed him within a year. Their sister's own loss is the same in both cases. Eighteen months on, the consolation "he would have been gone by now anyway" is apt for one twin and not the other. The Hardline View cannot explain why the passing of a counterfactual date consoles, and neither can eternalism about death's badness.
5. **Payoffs.** The argument gives a new, emotion-based case for subsequentism over eternalism. It explains why a parent's lifelong grief for a child is fitting, while lifelong acute grief for someone who died at 95 is odd. It also matches Hieronymi's account of resentment, on which resentment protests a wrong that "persists as a threat" and so lapses when the threat ends. Grief gets the same structure, with the dead person's life setting the clock.

**The obvious answer, and why the thesis is different.**
- **Obvious answer.** Grief fades for psychological reasons. Either it may permissibly be let go (Fritz; Fan), or the griever's situation changes (Marušić; Na'aman).
- **Why mine differs.** All of these keep the object of grief fixed in the past. Mine puts one clock in the dead person's counterfactual life, so that component has a determinate expiry date indexed to them, not to the griever.

**Closest prior work.**
- Kim & Kim, "Is grief fitting forever?", *Analysis* 2025 [ABSTRACT]. Argues the Hardline argument equivocates on "unchangeable past object". Gives no positive account of when grief stops being fitting.
- Fritz, "A Permissive View of Fitting Emotional Change", *AJP* 2025, and Fan, "Solving the puzzle of forever fitting emotions", *Phil Studies* 2026 [TITLE only]. Permissive solutions: grief stays fitting but stops being required.
- Atkins, "Empathy for the Dead", *Phil Imprint* 2024 [ABSTRACT; full text blocked]. Argues that profound grief stems largely from empathy for the dead person's view of death's harm. This is the nearest neighbour and must be read in full.
- Marušić, *On the Temporality of Emotions* (OUP 2022) [M; known through reviews in *Mind* 2023 and *Ethics* 2024]. Reasons for grief do not expire; its diminution is explained some other way.
- Bradley, "When Is Death Bad for the One Who Dies?", *Noûs* 2004, and *Well-Being and Death* (2009) [M]. The source of subsequentism.

**Generalist appeal.** Everyone grieves, and the thesis links the Epicurean timing puzzle to the rationality of emotion.

**Biggest risk.**
- Bradley or Marušić may already draw this inference about feeling sorry for the dead. That is the decisive check.
- Eternalists will reply that only the aptness of a *consolation* changes, not the fittingness of grief. The paper has to show that sorrow on someone's behalf is present-tensed.

**Confidence.** Medium-low.

## 2. Caution per choice is recklessness per relationship

**Question.** When you take risks for someone whose attitude to risk you don't know, should you play safe?

**Candidate thesis.** The principle "avoid risk for others" is false for *standing* proxies such as guardians, trustees, parents and doctors managing chronic illness. Respect for another person's risk attitude attaches to the whole relationship, and for every reasonable attitude that requires near-risk-neutral choice in each small, independent decision.

**Core argument and case.**
- **Minimal pair.** A one-off proxy and a standing proxy face the same menu: £100 for sure, or a 50/50 chance of £0 or £250.
  - Current principles give the same verdict to both: play safe.
  - Suppose the standing proxy makes 200 such choices a year for a ward. Safe every time yields £20,000. The risky policy has mean £25,000 and standard deviation of about £1,770, so it falls short of £20,000 with probability of roughly 0.2%.
  - Any ward who isn't pathologically risk-averse prefers the risky *policy*. Act-level caution is therefore a reckless policy.
- **Institutional evidence.** Trust law learned this lesson. The Restatement (Third) of Trusts (1992) and the Uniform Prudent Investor Act (1994) replaced item-by-item prudence with portfolio prudence, declaring that "no category or type of investment is inherently imprudent" [M].
- **Moral.** Risk-respect is owed to policies within relationships. Read act by act, the risk-avoidance principle gets the most common proxy cases backwards.

**The obvious answer, and why the thesis is different.**
- **Obvious answer.** Be cautious with other people's stakes (Buchak 2017; Fiat & Nissan-Rozen 2025).
- **Why mine differs.** Caution is right for the one-off proxy, but the standing proxy should be close to risk-neutral.

**Closest prior work.**
- Fiat & Nissan-Rozen, "Risk attitudes when choosing for others", *Analysis* 2025 [ABSTRACT]. Defends "avoid risk for others", phrased per choice.
- Buchak, "Taking Risks Behind the Veil of Ignorance", *Ethics* 2017 [M]. The risk-avoidance principle. Her global evaluation may already imply my verdict, so this needs checking.
- Thoma, "Risk Aversion and the Long Run", *Ethics* 2019 [M]. Draws the long-run moral, but for one's own choices.
- Mogensen, "Respect for others' risk attitudes and the long-run future", *Noûs* 2024 [ABSTRACT]. About future generations, not standing proxies.

**Generalist appeal.** Medicine, parenting and trusteeship.

**Biggest risk.**
- This is close to "known long-run result plus an application", a shape that came out EXPECTED in earlier rounds, and Thoma has drawn the self-regarding moral.
- Samuelson-type preferences have to be ruled unreasonable without leaning on Rabin's calibration theorem, which would be an imported result.

**Confidence.** Low-medium.

## 3. The avoidance test for requests (weak)

**Question.** Is it wrong to cross the street to avoid someone who will ask you for help?

**Candidate thesis.** Avoiding being asked is permissible because, and only when, a request would *create* a reason rather than reveal one. That makes avoidance the test between creation accounts of requests and the epistemic account, and the test favours creation.

**Core argument and case.**
- **Minimal pair.**
  - *Collector*: you cross the street to dodge a charity collector. This is permissible, if not admirable.
  - *Pond*: you take another path so as not to learn whether a child is drowning. This is wrong.
- **The test.** On the epistemic account (Weltman), both cases are wilful ignorance of existing reasons. The asymmetry is explained only if the collector's request would create a new reason.
- **Within creation accounts.** The test also favours Enoch over Mossé. On Mossé's view a request creates a reason because it helps you comply with antecedent duties, which makes avoiding requests pro tanto bad.

**The obvious answer, and why the thesis is different.**
- **Obvious answer.** Avoiding the collector is shabby but permissible, because beneficence is an imperfect duty.
- **Why mine differs.** The permission comes from the structure of a normative power, not from imperfection. Otherwise *Pond* would be permitted too.

**Closest prior work.**
- Weltman, "What Makes Requests Normative? The Epistemic Account Defended", *Ergo* 2023 [ABSTRACT].
- Mossé, "How Requests Create Reasons", *PPR* 2026 [ABSTRACT].
- Enoch, "Giving Practical Reasons", *Phil Imprint* 2011 [M].
- On rescue precautions: Rulli & Millum [M; title not verified].

**Generalist appeal.** High: everyone has dodged a collector.

**Biggest risk.**
- Intuitions about *Collector* are soft.
- The literature is small (four or five papers).
- Enoch may already discuss avoidance.

**Confidence.** Low. I include it only because three were asked for. I would not pursue it unless #1 fails.
