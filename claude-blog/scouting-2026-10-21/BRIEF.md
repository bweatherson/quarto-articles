# Scouting brief: a topic from inside philosophy (round 2, 21 Oct 2026)

## The job
We need a topic for a philosophy paper of at most 6,000 words. It is for the AI Philosophy Competition (deadline
31 Oct 2026; blind judging). The judging criteria are:
- clarity of thesis and reasoning;
- argumentation quality;
- significance;
- originality;
- engagement with the literature;
- accuracy in representing sources.

The paper is screened against a strict desk-review standard. NOT READY results from any one of these:
- no positive thesis statable in one substantive sentence;
- commentary on a small literature;
- an unanswered well-known objection;
- a thesis of the form "X can be Y" or "X is compatible with Y";
- weak generalist appeal (would a philosopher outside the subfield find the *problem itself* interesting?).

Your task is to find **three** candidate topics, each with a candidate thesis.

## Lessons from earlier rounds (these are binding)
Earlier attempts failed in identifiable ways:
1. **Do not import formal results.** Do not propose a "bridge" topic whose main content imports a theorem or
   result from economics, statistics, computer science or linguistics into philosophy. In every such case the
   source field's authors had already drawn the philosophical moral. Formal tools are fine *in service of* a
   philosophical question that comes first.
2. **Avoid the shapes that came out EXPECTED.** Two shapes repeatedly did so:
   - "known formula or theorem plus an application";
   - "the norms are silent on X".
   Narrowing a thesis after concessions also made it expected.
3. **The one paper judged INTERESTING** was an intervention in a live debate, with a vivid minimal pair of cases
   that the existing views get wrong. A related success ("Fairness Has Memory") started from real institutional
   practice (visa and hunting-licence lotteries) that an established theory (Broome on fair lotteries) handles
   badly.
4. **Predictability kills.** A fresh reader shown only the question should *not* produce your thesis as the
   obvious answer. For each candidate, say what the obvious answer is and why yours is different.
5. **Priority.** Before proposing a thesis, search for it in the topic's own vocabulary (PhilPapers, Google
   Scholar via web search, recent journal issues), and name the closest prior work.

## Do not overlap with existing papers in this series
- "Almost"
- "Retiring the Question" (dissolving questions)
- "Exhaustion and Closure" (inquiry)
- "Fairness Has Memory" (fair lotteries over time)
- "Findable, Not Simple"
- "The Oracle Never Says Yes"
- "Persuasion in Daylight"
- "A Counterexample to Arrhenius's Sixth Impossibility Theorem"
- the abandoned topics: multiple guesses; Stein's paradox and treating people as individuals; what a track record
  shows about expertise and chance

## How to search
Look at what is live *inside* philosophy now:
- recent issues, 2023–2026, of Phil Review, Mind, Noûs, PPR, Phil Imprint, Ergo, AJP, Phil Studies, Analysis,
  J Phil, Phil Quarterly, Synthese, Ethics, PPA;
- recent SEP revisions;
- PhilPapers trending and recent books;
- Daily Nous discussions.

Find a question where the debate is stuck, where a case breaks the standard views, or where a widely held claim
looks false. Use WebSearch and WebFetch. Do not use mirrors, pirate sites or Sci-Hub; if something is paywalled,
say so. If an API asks for an email, use research@example.org.

## Output format (save to the file named in your prompt)
For each of your three candidates:
- **Question** (one or two sentences, in terms a generalist would recognise as a real philosophical question).
- **Candidate thesis** (one substantive sentence of the form "X is Y" or "X is false because ...").
- **Core argument or case** (5–10 lines; include the vivid case or minimal pair if there is one).
- **The obvious answer** a competent philosopher would give on seeing the question, and why the thesis is not that.
- **Closest prior work** (2–5 items), each marked [READ], [PART] or [ABSTRACT], with what it already says.
- **Generalist appeal** (one line).
- **Biggest risk** (one or two lines: what could make it NOT READY).
- **Your confidence** (low / medium / high) that a 6,000-word paper on this could pass a hostile desk screen.

Then rank your three. Keep the file under about 2,000 words. Do not pad: a candidate you do not believe in is
worse than two you do.
