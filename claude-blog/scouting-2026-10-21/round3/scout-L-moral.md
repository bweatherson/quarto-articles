## Candidate 1. Asking binds the asker

**Question.** If you ask someone's permission to do what you were entitled to do anyway, and they say no, may you go ahead? If you may not, where did their veto come from?

**Candidate thesis.** Asking someone's permission gives them a veto over what you asked to do, even when you needed no permission. The veto comes from the asking itself, an act of deferral that hands the other person a share in the decision. It does not come from an authority they already had (Darwall, Caponetto) or from an implied promise (Scanlon).

**Core argument or case.**
- **The minimal pair.** Ann and Bob share an office, and Ann knows Bob dislikes draughts.
  - (a) She opens the window unasked. This is within shared-space norms; at worst it is mildly inconsiderate.
  - (b) She asks, "Mind if I open the window?" Bob says, "I'd rather you didn't," and she opens it. This is plainly wrong: "Then why did you ask?"
  - Ann's knowledge is fixed across the pair, so only the asking differs.
- **Variants.**
  - (c) Ann asked sincerely and then changed her mind. Going ahead is still wrong, so the wrong is not deception.
  - (d) Bob sought no assurance and relied on nothing.
  - (e) The yes side. If Bob says "go ahead" and the papers blow away, "you said I could" makes him a co-author of the decision. A promise by Ann could not do this.
  - (f) Bob can decline the deferral: "Don't ask me." Ann can withdraw the question before the answer, but not after.
- **Real practice.**
  - Data-protection guidance bars a controller who asked for consent from switching to another lawful basis after a refusal. The ICO calls such asking "misleading and inherently unfair" [MEM; search hits unopened].
  - The AAP (1995) says the physician "should not solicit a patient's views without intending to weigh them seriously" [exact-phrase search hit].
  - Both treat asking as binding the asker, and both explain this by deception. Case (c) shows deception is not the ground.
- **Upshot.** Deferral is the mirror image of consent: consent hands over a permission, asking hands over a veto. The burdens of being offered a choice (Dworkin, Velleman) are the cost side of the same transfer.

**Canonical treatments.**
- **Austin 1962; Searle 1969/1979** [MEM]. Authority is a preparatory condition, so a permission request to someone without authority misfires. They predict no veto.
- **Darwall 2006** [MEM]. Requests presuppose the addressee's standing and leave the addressee free. Nothing in his account binds the asker.
- **Scanlon 1998, ch. 7** [MEM].
  - Principle F needs the promisee to want assurance and the promisor to aim to give it. Neither holds in (b).
  - Principle D needs reliance loss. There is none.
- **Raz 1977; Owens 2012** [MEM].
  - Normative powers serve interests, and Bob had no prior interest in a veto.
  - Raz's value-of-control framework could house deferral. The paper should argue within it that deferral is not promising.
- **Enoch 2011** [MEM]. Requests give the addressee reasons. Enoch is silent on any effect on the asker.
- **Dworkin 1982; Velleman 1992** [MEM]. Offered options burden the chooser. Neither addresses whether the offerer is bound.

**Recent closest work.**
- **Caponetto 2023, *Synthese*** [ABSTRACT]. Proper refusals of permission requests require speaker authority, which predicts that Bob's refusal is void. She is the foil.
- **Lewis 2018, *Phil Imprint*** [ABSTRACT]. Requests give the addressee discretionary reasons; nothing on the asker.
- I found no work on permission requests binding the asker.

**Obvious answer.** "Asking implies you will abide. Going ahead breaks an implied promise, shows the question was a sham, or is just inconsiderate now that you know." The thesis differs on four points:
- the pair holds knowledge fixed;
- (c) rules out deception;
- (d) rules out Scanlonian assurance;
- no promise explains the co-authorship in (e).

**Biggest risk.**
- **The conventionalist reply.** A Hume–Rawls conventionalist will say "May I?" is a conventional device for undertaking a conditional commitment, a species of promising. The defence rests on (e), and on the point that the asker communicates deference, not an undertaking.
- **"Too small."** This charge must be met by framing deferral as consent's mirror image.

**Confidence.** Medium.

## Candidate 2. Revoking gifts for ingratitude

**Question.** If someone you gave a gift to later gravely wrongs you, may you take it back? Does a benefactor keep any power over a benefit once it is given?

**Candidate thesis.** A donor may revoke a gift when the donee gravely wrongs her. So the benefactor's correlate of a debt of gratitude is not a claim to thanks (Wellman rightly denies there is one) or an "imperfect right" to remonstrate (Manela). It is a power to undo the benefit, held only against wrongs to the benefactor and extinguished by forgiveness.

**Core argument or case.**
- **The triple.** An aunt gives her nephew £10,000 for a deposit.
  - (a) Later he defrauds her of £3,000. She may ask for the gift back, not just the £3,000.
  - (b) They drift apart. She may not.
  - (c) He defrauds a stranger. She may not.
- **What the cases rule out.**
  - (b) excludes a failed-basis reading.
  - (c) excludes general forfeiture.
  - Riggs v Palmer does not apply, because the gift was not obtained by the wrong.
- **Real practice.**
  - Roman law, C. 8.55.10 [MEM].
  - BGB §530 [MEM]: revocation for gross ingratitude, via a serious wrong against the donor or a close relative.
  - BGB §532 [READ]: revocation is excluded once the donor "has forgiven" the donee, one year after the donor knows, or after the donee's death.
  - BGB §534 [MEM]: gifts made from moral duty or decency cannot be revoked.
  - Code civil arts. 953–955 [MEM].
  - The common law has no such power.
- **The fit.** The German scheme is what a gratitude-based power predicts: it is directed at wrongs to the donor, extinguished by forgiveness, and absent for gifts made from duty.
- **Reply to Seneca.** His case against actions for ingratitude ("benefits become loans") targets failure to return. Revocation answers Aquinas's gravest grade, "returning evil for good", and demands no return.

**Canonical treatments.**
- **Seneca, *De Beneficiis*.**
  - III.6–17 [MEM]: ingratitude should not be actionable.
  - I.5 [PART]: a benefit's "province is the mind", and it outlasts its object. This is a direct objection.
- **Aquinas, *ST* II-II qq. 106–107; Hume, *Treatise* 3.1.1; Kant, *MdS* 6:454–6** [MEM; Hume and Kant PART via SEP]. Ingratitude is the gravest vice. None discusses revocation.
- **Manela, SEP "Gratitude"** [PART].
  - Wellman 1999: "no benefactor has a right to gratitude".
  - Darwall 2012: resenting ingratitude is fitting.
  - Walker 1980–81 and Manela 2015: "grateful nonmaleficence", a partial anticipation of the negative core.
  - The entry does not discuss revocation.
- **Berger 1975; Card 1988; McConnell 1993; Simmons 1979 ch. 7** [MEM/SEP]. Gratitude duties have latitude and no claim-right correlates.

**Recent closest work.**
- Manela 2015, *OSNE* 5 [via SEP; paywalled]: imperfect rights.
- McConnell 2017 (*ETMP*) and Macnamara 2019 [via SEP; unread].
- A search found legal, not philosophical, treatments of revocation.

**Obvious answer.** "A gift once given is the donee's. Redress the new wrong; reclaiming the gift treats it as a conditional loan." The thesis affirms reclaiming and grounds it in gratitude, not in conditions or forfeiture.

**Biggest risk.**
- **Contested intuitions.** Common-law readers may find reclaiming petty; civil-law practice only partly mitigates this.
- **Absorption** by an implied-condition reading, as with engagement rings.
- **Partial anticipation** by grateful nonmaleficence.
- **Seneca's "the benefit is in the mind" objection.**

**Confidence.** Low to medium.

## Ranking

1. **Asking binds the asker.** It has a widely shared pair and two regulatory practices, and the closest recent work (Caponetto) predicts the opposite verdict.
2. **Revoking gifts for ingratitude.** It has a rich institution, but it rests more on contested judgements and is partly anticipated.
