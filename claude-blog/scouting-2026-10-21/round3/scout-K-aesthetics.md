## Candidate A: "The intended emotion fixes what a 'true story' owes"

**Question.** When a performer says "this happened to me" and invents it, when is the audience wronged? Public cases: Mike Daisey's Foxconn monologue (broadcast by *This American Life*, retracted 2012); James Frey, *A Million Little Pieces* (2006); Hasan Minhaj's stand-up stories, exposed in 2024 and defended as "emotional truth". Everyone shares the verdicts: some invented anecdotes are fine and some are a betrayal. No account says what separates them.

**Candidate thesis.** A performer's duty of truth in a story presented as true attaches to whatever the intended belief-dependent emotion is about: invention is innocent where the emotion aimed at is belief-independent (amusement at absurdity) or directed at a kind of thing (a disclosed or evident composite), and wrongs the audience where it invites pity, admiration or resentment toward a particular person or event that did not occur.

**Core argument.**
- Minimal pair, content held constant. A comic tells a bureaucratic-absurdity story ("the DMV declared me legally dead") as something that happened to him. Version 1 is framed for the laugh. Version 2, with the same events, is framed as testimony to institutional cruelty, to earn solidarity and outrage. Learning the story was invented, audiences shrug at version 1 and feel conned by version 2. The speech-act frame, genre, stage and "this happened" are identical across the two, so any account on which genre or frame decides the norm gets the pair wrong.
- Explanation: outrage, pity and admiration have fittingness conditions that include the event's occurrence (D'Arms and Jacobson style). Amusement at absurdity does not. The audience is wronged when the telling is built to elicit an emotion that would be unfitting if the audience learned the truth. I call this the disclosure test.
- It explains why exaggeration ("three hours" for two) is harmless when the outrage survives the correction, and why a disclosed composite is fine when the emotion is directed at a kind of treatment, not at one event. That gives the "emotional truth" defence its proper, limited scope.
- It also fits cases with no third-party harm and no politics: an invented sick relative told for sympathy.
- This puts the paradox of fiction's own premise (emotions need belief) to work, in reverse: it fixes a norm on speakers.

**Canonical treatments.**
1. Searle 1975, "The Logical Status of Fictional Discourse" [MEM]. Fiction is pretended assertion. It leaves a binary: a stand-up anecdote is either assertion (truth throughout) or pretence (no norm). No within-frame gradient.
2. Carroll 1997, "Fiction, Non-Fiction, and the Film of Presumptive Assertion" [MEM]. Closest anticipation: nonfiction is fixed by the maker's intention that the audience take the content as asserted and respond with belief-oriented stances. It sorts works or frames, not emotion types within a frame. A hostile reader will say my thesis is its refinement.
3. Lamarque and Olsen 1994, *Truth, Fiction, and Literature* [MEM]. The fictive stance. Literary works, not performers' personal anecdotes.
4. Lejeune 1975, *Le Pacte autobiographique* [MEM]. A reader's contract fixes the norm. Same frame-level limitation.
5. Radford 1975 and Walton 1978 on belief and emotion, and Gendler and Kovakovich 2006 [MEM]. They supply the belief-dependence premise. They say nothing about speakers' duties.
6. Stokke 2018, *Lying and Insincerity* [MEM]. Lying requires assertion. It answers "was it a lie?" not "was the audience wronged?".

**Recent closest work.**
- Hanscomb 2022, "Truth and Autobiography in Stand-up Comedy and the Genius of Doug Stanhope", *Philosophy of Humor Yearbook* 3 [PART: full-text summary]. Treats truth as an audience expectation and says fabrication makes audiences feel "conned". Typology of stories, no principled test.
- Lintott 2017, "Why (not) philosophy of stand-up comedy?" [ABSTRACT]. Notes the neglect.
- Olin 2023, "On Lying and Joking" (Belmont symposium talk) [ABSTRACT]. Lying as a thick concept; comedic lies can be good. Also a PhilPapers record, key DADNSD (author and title not shown on the page I fetched), arguing that stand-up is not analogous to the fictive stance because it makes real-world claims [ABSTRACT].

**Obvious answer.** "The frame decides. In a show sold as his own life, a false anecdote is an assertion and a lie. In pure joke-telling there is licence. Minhaj failed because it was testimony." The thesis differs by predicting a split inside a single frame and speaker, and by locating the wrong in the audience's fitting-emotion entitlement, not in lying or genre.

**Biggest risk.** A rival absorbs the pair: "version 2 differs because it makes a serious claim about real institutions, so the wrong is third-party harm or misused testimonial credibility." I must show the split without a real-world target, which the invented-illness case attempts. Second: amusement may also depend on believing the story is true (Hanscomb says true stories engage more), which blurs the belief-independent side. Third: a hostile reviewer calls it a refinement of Carroll's presumptive assertion.

**Confidence.** Low to medium.

## Candidate B: "Nostalgia is for the world that formed you, not for the good one"

**Question.** Is nostalgia fitting only when the past was good and well remembered, as the "rosy retrospection" picture implies, or does it follow something else?

**Candidate thesis.** Nostalgia is fitting just when its object is a vanished world in which the subject's present attachments were formed and which is no longer accessible; whether that world was good, or accurately remembered in its details, is neither necessary nor sufficient.

**Core argument.**
- Real practice: Ostalgie (nostalgia for the GDR), Yugonostalgia, nostalgia for wartime and for army or student poverty. Subjects know the period was bad and remember it accurately. The standard picture (idealisation, goodness) calls this irrational or misreported. Yet we judge it intelligible.
- Minimal pair. Two people with identical, accurate memories of a hard year. For one it is where her friends, skills and loves came from; for the other it was a bleak interlude. Only the first is nostalgic, and fittingly so. Compare a pleasant but unformative year abroad, which provokes pleasant memory, not nostalgia.
- Predicts: nostalgia lapses when the world remains accessible (the hometown still there is homesickness, the original sense of the word), and "nostalgia for eras one never lived" is longing or admiration, not nostalgia.
- Howard's flagged puzzle (non-veridical Proustian memory) resolves: details may be wrong while the formative role is real.

**Canonical treatments.**
1. Boym 2001, *The Future of Nostalgia* [MEM]. Restorative and reflective nostalgia. Diagnoses and politics, no fittingness analysis.
2. Davis 1979, *Yearning for Yesterday* [MEM]. Sociological; nostalgia as identity continuity in transition. Close to my "formative world" idea.
3. Howard 2012, "Nostalgia", *Analysis* 72 [ABSTRACT]. Rejects comparison accounts; ends with the evaluative puzzle for non-veridical nostalgia. Closest philosophical anticipation, and it leaves the question open.
4. Sedikides and Wildschut, many papers [MEM]. Psychological: nostalgia as bittersweet social-connectedness emotion; no fittingness claim.
5. D'Arms and Jacobson 2000, "The Moralistic Fallacy" [MEM]. The framework for fittingness.

**Recent closest work.** Massantini 2023, *Philosophy of Nostalgia: Affectivity, Media and Politics*, Osnabrück dissertation [ABSTRACT]: nostalgia as "desire for the whole", displaced and collective nostalgia. Did not check its treatment of fittingness.

**Obvious answer.** "Nostalgia is fitting when the past really was good and is remembered without major distortion (Boym, Sedikides)." The thesis rejects goodness and accuracy as the tests.

**Biggest risk.** Rivals absorb it: "formative role is just a way the past was valuable to you." The topic also sits beside the abandoned grief-over-time topic (loss, attachment), and a reviewer may see the overlap. Unverified: whether Massantini or the *Philosophy of Nostalgia* literature already says this.

**Confidence.** Low.

## Ranking
1. Candidate A: a concrete test, a vivid pair, a live public controversy, and thin philosophical coverage. Main danger is Carroll.
2. Candidate B: a small literature that leaves the question open, but a weaker, more absorbable thesis.
