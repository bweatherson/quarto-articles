# Canon check: T8, "Asking permission confers a veto" (21 Oct 2026)

Read-status key: [READ], [PART], [ABSTRACT], [MEM] (from memory, unchecked). I used about 40 search and fetch calls, over the budget of 30. Fetches blocked: PhilArchive's Caponetto PDF and the zora.uzh.ch page (Anubis bot-block).

## 1. Verdict: DEAD (anticipated, and the minimal pair is unsound as built)

- **Anticipated.** Westlund, "Deference as a Normative Power" (*Phil Studies* 166, 2013, 455–74), already states T8's mechanism. Deference is a normative power. A directed act of deference, completed by the other's uptake, binds the deferrer, transfers "deliberative discretion", and is "akin (in some respects)" to promising. T8 adds only that a permission request is one way of deferring. That is a known framework plus an application, the shape that keeps failing.
- **The foils are the wrong foils.**
  - Scanlon's assurance view is the weakest form of promise. Owens's power-based promise (2006; 2012) is exactly "handing someone the right to decide". Its "authority interest" is the prior interest in a veto that the scout says Bob lacks.
  - Gilbert's joint commitment delivers variants (e) and (f) almost verbatim.
  - Caponetto does not predict that Bob's refusal is void.
- **Unsound as built.** The office pair is confounded in three ways:
  - Bob has a prior stake in a shared space.
  - Bob's "I'd rather you didn't" is itself a request.
  - "Mind if I…?" is a politeness formula.
  - Moreover, the "veto" fails for acts squarely in the asker's own sphere.

## 2. Canonical and closest works

**Closest: Westlund 2013** [PART: abstract and endnotes, via Springer].
- What it says (abstract paraphrased): voluntary authority rests on a promise-like voluntary obligation, created by a directed act of deference plus uptake. Deference is where "agents transfer deliberative discretion to others" (abstract). Attempts fail "unless they are given proper uptake by the would-be authority" (n. 2). The account is constrained by equal moral authority, following Darwall (n. 16).
- Bearing on T8: this is T8's "act of deferral that hands them a share in the decision", with the uptake condition behind (f). The notes do not discuss permission requests; the body text may. **Paywalled; must read §§1–5.**

**Owens, "A Simple Theory of Promising" (*Phil Review* 2006)** [PART: full text via White Rose, read through a summariser].
- What it says: promising serves an *authority interest*, the wish that "I, rather than you, have the authority to determine what you do" (p. 1).
  - People want this authority "for its own sake" (p. 1). So the scout's premise that Bob "had no prior interest in a veto" is false on the standard power-based view.
  - Promises are "valid only if accepted" (p. 18). Releasing differs from "refusing to accept it in the first place" (p. 19). This is (f).
  - Promises are welcomed as "a display of respect for their capacity to take decisions" (p. 18). This is deference, under the name of promise.
- Owens 2012 [ABSTRACT, TOC, NDPR review]: the book covers "consent and refusal, of promise and request" (OUP blurb); normative powers "open up new possibilities of wronging" (p. 11). Read chs. 5–7.

**Gilbert 2006 and *Rights and Demands* (2018)** [PART: NDPR review; MEM].
- What it says: joint commitment is "a genuine co-creation without parts" (2018, p. 164); the promisee is a "small-scale sovereign" (p. 78). [MEM] Joint commitments form through expressions of readiness, need all parties' concurrence to rescind, and agreements are joint decisions (Gilbert 1993, *Ethics*).
- Bearing on T8: Ann's question invites a joint decision and Bob's answer is uptake. She can withdraw before uptake but not after (f), and "you said I could" is joint authorship (e). The scout's two "non-promissory" variants are Gilbert's signature features.

**Raz, *Morality of Freedom* (1986) chs. 3–4; "Promises and Obligations" (1977)** [MEM; PART via Kolodny's lecture notes].
- What it says: the arbitrator binds because the parties referred the dispute to him (MF c. pp. 41–2) [MEM]. In ch. 4, consent to authority is a *one-sided* normative power, valid when there is value in being able to bind oneself, chiefly as an expression of trust or identification (c. pp. 80–94; Kolodny paraphrases it as binding "only if there is reason, on balance, to enable people…"). Raz 1977 makes promising communicating an intention to undertake an obligation by that very act [MEM].
- Bearing on T8: a permission request is a one-off Razian consent to authority. The scout conceded that Raz "could house deferral".

**Darwall 2006, chs. 1–3** [MEM; no verified quote]. Second-personal address presupposes the addressee's standing, and by "Fichte's point" the addresser is committed to it. So asking permission commits the asker to the addressee's standing to refuse. The scout's "nothing in his account binds the asker" is too quick.

**Scanlon, *What We Owe* (1998), ch. 7, Principle F (p. 304)** [MEM; F's clause quoted in Owens 2006, p. 10: "unless Y consents to X's not doing so"].
- T8 rightly says F does not fit (no assurance is sought). But this rebuts only the assurance theory of promising.

**Caponetto, "The Pragmatic Structure of Refusal" (*Synthese* 201, 2023)** [PART: Springer full text, read through a summariser].
- What it says: proper refusals, "unlike other family members, require speaker authority" (§3).
  - A request to someone who lacks authority is "defective, not a complete misfire" (§4.4).
  - "the obligation … is not created by my request, but by your granting it" (§2.2).
  - She is silent on the requester's position, and says nothing about courtesy requests.
- Bearing on T8: she is not a foil predicting a void refusal. T8 is a friendly supplement to her account, since the requester's act supplies the authority her felicity condition needs.

**Lewis 1979; Langton 2018, "The Authority of Hate Speech"; McGowan 2019** [MEM]. Presupposed authority becomes real by accommodation unless blocked. Ann's question presupposes Bob's authority, so these deliver "veto from the asking" with no new power.

**Requests literature** (Enoch 2011 [MEM]; Lewis 2018 [ABSTRACT]; Schaber 2021, *ETMP* [PART]; Cupit 1994 [MEM]). Requests give the addressee discretionary reasons: "It is up to the requestee to decide whether she complies" (Schaber §5). None discusses binding the asker. But it creates a confound (§3): Bob's "I'd rather you didn't" is itself a request.

**Others.** Silent on asking or not on point: Hurd 1996 [MEM]; Shiffrin 2008 [MEM]; Beyleveld and Brownsword 2007, via Parmigiani 2021 [PART]. Watson 2009 [MEM] treats promise as self-binding by declaration, which supports the "undertaking" reading of "May I?". Chang 2020 [PART] defines a power as "an appropriate, reflexive willing that things be thus and so" (p. 277) and cites Westlund 2013 (p. 300). I could not identify "Koch" or "Bruno" within budget.

**Practice** [MEM].
- **ICO and EDPB 05/2020:** a controller who asks for consent may not switch lawful basis after a refusal. The ICO's ground is that such asking is "misleading and inherently unfair": deception, not deferral.
- **Public law (*R (Moseley) v Haringey* [2014] UKSC 56):** a *voluntary* consultation must be fair, but it confers no veto.
- **Folk wisdom:** "Easier to ask forgiveness than permission"; "Ask for advice, not permission" (boz.com) [title only]. The core claim is a commonplace.

## 3. Soundness

**Is "deferral" a conditional promise, a Razian submission, or a Gilbertian joint commitment?**
Against Scanlon, no. Against Owens, Raz 1977, Watson and Westlund, yes: same genus. Each variant meant to separate deferral from promise is a mark of a power-based, uptake-requiring transaction:
- **(c) still bound after a change of mind:** the signature of any power-created obligation (Owens: binds regardless of later intentions).
- **(d) no assurance or reliance:** defeats only Scanlon.
- **(e) "you said I could":** volenti if the papers are Bob's (Hurd, Owens); joint decision if they are Ann's (Gilbert; Westlund 2009).
- **(f) decline; withdraw before the answer but not after:** offer, acceptance, irrevocability (Owens p. 19; Gilbert's concurrence condition; Westlund's uptake).

So "deferral, not promise" is a verbal dispute with the strongest rival, and Westlund has already named the genus.

**Do the minimal pairs work? No, not as built.**
1. **Shared office.** The window is a shared resource, so Bob arguably already has a claim to a say. The premise "needed no permission" is doubtful, and the pair is contaminated by prior authority, the very thing T8 excludes.
2. **Knowledge is not held fixed.** In (b) Ann learns Bob's current, avowed preference, and his reply is itself a request. The proper control is (a′): Bob volunteers "I'd rather you didn't", and Ann opens it anyway. (a′) is also somewhat rude. What is left between (a′) and (b) is "then why did you ask?", the expressive (sham-question) reading both pretesters flagged.
3. **Speech-act ambiguity.** "Mind if I…?" is conventionally a preference query or politeness formula (Searle on indirect speech acts) [MEM]. On that reading there is no permission request to defer with.
4. **The domain test sinks the generality.** An adult asks a parent, "Is it OK if I take the Leeds job?" The parent says "I'd rather you didn't." Going ahead is not wrong; at most there is a duty to engage and explain. The request's force tracks the asked person's prior stake and the asker's protected sphere, not the asking alone.
5. **Practice splits against T8.** The data-protection rule is veto-like but grounded in deception. Voluntary public-law consultation gives duties of fair consideration and no veto (*Moseley*).
6. **The veto is only defeasible** (Y3). That makes it either a pro tanto duty, which the expressive and requests accounts already give, or something that needs a theory of defeat, which is Westlund's constraint.

## 4. Generalist appeal

The problem has high appeal: an everyday puzzle, a vivid "then why did you ask?", and links to consent, authority and politeness. But the conclusion is folk wisdom. Its philosophical payoff lies in the grounding, and that grounding is Westlund 2013, on top of Owens and Gilbert. A desk reviewer who knows the normative-powers literature will see an application of "deference as a normative power".

## 5. Best version, if forced to proceed (not recommended)

**"A permission request is an attempted act of deference whose success is capped by the asker's protected sphere."** Outside that sphere, the "No" is a veto. Inside it, no authority transfers, and the request yields only a duty of consultation: to weigh the answer and explain an override. The evidence is the institutional pair of the EDPB/ICO no-switching rule against *Moseley*'s voluntary consultation.

First check Westlund 2013 §§3–5: her equal-moral-authority constraint may already deliver the cap. Also check Owens 2012 ch. 7. Even if it survives, it is an application of known frameworks. **Drop T8.**
