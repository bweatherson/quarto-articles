## Candidate 1: Silence about self-fulfilling side effects

**Question.** Must a doctor warn of a side effect when the warning itself would mostly cause it? More generally: can the duty to inform be met by silence when the information is self-fulfilling?

**Candidate thesis.** For side effects produced mainly by the patient's expecting them, the doctrine of material risk is fully met by silence. The risk counts as material only relative to the disclosure policy, and under silence it is not material. So the "nocebo dilemma" is not a conflict between autonomy and non-maleficence, and it needs no authorisation from the patient.

**Core argument / real practice.**
- *The practice.* Statins:
  - In SAMSON (NEJM 2020), about 90% of the symptom burden on statin was also present on placebo.
  - In ASCOT-LLA (Lancet 2017), excess muscle complaints appeared only once patients knew they were taking a statin.
  - In COVID vaccine trials, about three quarters of systemic adverse events after dose 1 were nocebo (Haas 2022).
  - Myers et al. (1987) found that the consent form itself caused side effects.
  - All of these are [MEM].
- *Premise 1.* Materiality (Canterbury; Montgomery) is significance to this patient's decision about her own prospects.
- *Premise 2.* For a side effect produced by expectation, her prospects depend on what the doctor says. The risk is high if she is told and low if she is not.
- *Premise 3.* So the only coherent test is a fixed-point test. A disclosure policy complies if it discloses every risk that is material *under that policy*. Full disclosure passes the test, and so does silence. Non-maleficence then chooses between them, at no cost to autonomy.
- *Prediction (i).* No meta-consent is needed, contrary to Wells & Kaptchuk's "authorized concealment".
- *Prediction (ii).* Disclosure becomes required exactly when the patient already expects the effect, which is when disclosure adds little harm.
- *Prediction (iii).* Drug-attributable risks, such as rhabdomyolysis, must be disclosed even when disclosure amplifies them. A pure non-maleficence view, like that of Fortunato et al., denies this.
- *Minimal pair.* Dr A prescribes a statin without mentioning aches to a patient with no expectations. Dr B does the same to a patient who says, "my brother got terrible aches on these." A complies; B does not.

**Canonical treatments.**
- Faden & Beauchamp, *A History and Theory of Informed Consent* (1986) [MEM]. Informed consent is autonomous authorisation, and it uses reasonable-person materiality. As far as I recall it has nothing on risks that depend on disclosure. It frames therapeutic privilege as an exception to autonomy, which is the view the thesis denies.
- Beauchamp & Childress, *Principles*, 8th ed. (2019) [MEM]. It treats therapeutic privilege as a narrow exception. Whether it mentions the nocebo effect is unchecked.
- *Canterbury v Spence* (1972) and *Montgomery v Lanarkshire* [2015] UKSC 11, [87]–[91] [MEM]. These give the materiality test and a narrow therapeutic exception. On their face, they treat listed side effects as material. The thesis claims they misapply their own test.
- O'Neill 2003, "Some limits of informed consent" (JME), and Manson & O'Neill 2007, *Rethinking Informed Consent* [MEM]. **Main canonical risk.** Their waiver/no-deception model may already permit non-disclosure. Reply: it permits it for every risk, so it cannot yield prediction (ii) or explain why the doctrine of material risk is satisfied. This must be read.
- Wells & Kaptchuk 2012, AJOB, "To tell the truth, the whole truth, may do patients harm" [MEM]. They name the self-fulfilling problem and propose contextualised consent and authorised concealment. They treat it as a real conflict.
- Cohen 2014, *Bioethics* 28:147 [ABSTRACT]. The dilemma is real and "especially pernicious" because the caregiver directly causes the harm.

**Recent closest work.**
- Fortunato, Wasserman & Menkes 2017, AJOB 17(6), with commentaries by Colloca and by Bryan & Dubljević [ABSTRACT]. Non-disclosure is permitted on grounds of non-maleficence, and autonomy "must be forestalled". This is the direct foil.
- Gelfand 2020, CQHE 29:223, "Taking autonomy seriously" [ABSTRACT]. The conflict is real and must be managed; he uses an autonomy scale.
- Not read: "Análisis ético de la entrega parcial de información…" (*Rev Méd Chile*) [title only], and the CQHE commentary "Harm, truth, and the nocebo effect" [title only].

**Obvious answer.** "A genuine autonomy/beneficence conflict, to be managed by framing, contextualised consent or authorised concealment." The thesis denies there is any conflict, and it gives different verdicts in (i)–(iii).

**Biggest risk.**
- A referee may insist that the patient is owed facts about the drug, or about her own susceptibility to suggestion, and not the risk relative to a policy.
- Manson & O'Neill may be said to anticipate the thesis.
- Leaflets and the internet mean silence seldom achieves a low expectation, which narrows the scope.
- The paper must argue from the structure of the norm, or it reads as "self-fulfilling prophecy plus an application".

**Confidence.** Medium-low to medium.

## Candidate 2: Velleman's burden argument favours the prescription model

**Question.** Does it matter whether assisted dying works by a prescription the patient keeps, or by a clinician at an appointed time? Behind that: can having an option harm you, and does it matter who sees that you have it?

**Candidate thesis.** Velleman's argument that a right to die burdens patients actually counts in favour of prescription models (Oregon, Victoria) and against clinician-administered models (Canada, New Zealand). Once the means are in the patient's private keeping, the option returns to the standing of the ever-available option of suicide, which Velleman grants leaves life the default. A clinician model keeps the option institutional, with a date, up to the moment of death.

**Core argument / real practice.**
- *What Velleman says* (1992, reprinted in *Beyond Price*, OBP 2015, pp. 5–20; [PART] via fetch):
  - The burden comes from being "seen to have" the option (p. 12).
  - Patients must "justify [their] continued existence" to family and doctors (pp. 12–14).
  - He does not count the bare availability of suicide as removing the default (p. 12).
  - He prefers "no policy at all", or a policy "weak and vague by design" (pp. 19–20).
- *The prescription model.* After the request:
  - the patient privately holds the means;
  - there is no date and no further decision point owed to anyone;
  - staying alive requires nothing.
- *The clinician model.* After the request:
  - there are further assessments and a scheduled date;
  - family gather, so going ahead becomes the course of events;
  - in Canada, the waiver of final consent can make death the default outcome.
- *Evidence.*
  - About a third of Oregon prescription holders never take the drug [MEM, Oregon DWDA reports].
  - Their stated reasons centre on control (Ganzini et al.; *Hospitalist* summaries [ABSTRACT]).
  - Very few Canadians withdraw once approved [MEM, Health Canada; check this].
- *Upshot.* The debate over self-administration (disability equality; *Shavelson v Bonta*) is mis-framed. What matters is possession without an appointment, not who performs the last act.

**Canonical treatments.**
- Velleman 1992, "Against the Right to Die" [PART]. **Risk:** his own "seen to have" point and his preference for "no policy" partly deliver the thesis. What is new is the claim that the prescription model is *more* default-preserving than "no policy" plus refusal of treatment, and that the clinician model is the worst case.
- G. Dworkin 1982, "Is more choice better than less?", and Schelling, *Strategy of Conflict* [MEM]. These are the sources of the idea that options can harm. They say nothing on dying models.
- Brock 1992, "Voluntary active euthanasia" (Hastings), and Sumner 2011, *Assisted Death* [MEM]. Both hold that there is no intrinsic moral difference between assisted suicide and euthanasia; the differences are in safeguards. That is the obvious answer, and the thesis rejects it.
- Battin, *Ending Life* (2005) [MEM]. She praises Oregon's patient control. It is unchecked whether she ties this to Velleman or to possession; it must be checked.
- SEP, "Voluntary Euthanasia" (Young) [MEM, unchecked].

**Recent closest work.**
- Symons & Chua 2020, *Bioethics* 34:288, "Alive by default" [ABSTRACT]. They defend Velleman's burden argument and call for research on burdens. They do not discuss models of assisted dying.
- Klim 2016 (*Voices in Bioethics*) [PART]: Oregon's safeguards "reduce" the pressure. Desai 2017 (*Rerum Causae*) [ABSTRACT]. Iordachescu 2026 (SPPE) [PART]: access independent of physicians, without Velleman.

**Obvious answer.** "Velleman's argument hits every legal right to die equally, or hits Oregon hardest, since the pills are always there." The thesis reverses this.

**Biggest risk.**
- The empirical premise: non-use may reflect early prescription and natural death, not the value of having the option.
- A referee may read it as Velleman's framework plus an application.
- Possession carries hazards (unwitnessed or coerced ingestion) that a hostile referee will press.
- The literature on assisted dying is huge, and the claim that Oregon gives patients "control" is a stock point.
- Health Canada withdrawal figures are unchecked.

**Confidence.** Low-medium.

## Ranking

1. **Candidate 1 (nocebo / silence).** It is a structural argument about a norm, it comes from a high-volume real practice, and it intervenes in a live, citable debate (Wells & Kaptchuk; Cohen; Fortunato; Gelfand) whose shared premise it denies. It also makes three divergent predictions. It must be read against Manson & O'Neill before anything else.
2. **Candidate 2 (Velleman reversed).** The canonical engagement is good and the institutional contrast is real. But it is partly delivered by Velleman's own "seen to have" point and depends on contestable empirical data.

**Decisive unread texts:**
- Manson & O'Neill 2007, chs. 3–4;
- Wells & Kaptchuk 2012 (full text);
- Cohen 2014 (full text);
- Battin 2005;
- Health Canada's MAID withdrawal data.
