# Canon check: T7 (invented "true" stories and the intended emotion)

About 32 calls. A quote marked [MEM] is a coined term I am confident of, not a checked sentence. Correction to scout K: the Minhaj exposé is Clare Malone, *New Yorker*, September 2023, not 2024 ("The emotional truth is first", TheWrap headline [READ]).

## 1. Verdict: DEAD

T7 is dead as stated. It is not anticipated sentence for sentence; I found no work that keys permissibility to the *type* of emotion invited. It dies for three other reasons.

1. **A standard framework delivers the minimal pair.** On Currie's intentionalism (1990), what matters is whether the teller intends belief. Aiming at a response that needs belief means intending belief, and so asserting. Apply Carroll's (1997) presumptive assertion claim by claim and the DMV pair falls out: the "reframing a rival absorbs" shape. Pretester Y3 also predicted "reliance on beliefs that matter" (40%).
2. **The core premise fails on the standard literature.** The dominant responses to Radford (thought theory, Gendler and Kovakovich, Matravers) deny that pity *as a type* needs belief. Amusement is often belief-dependent: the "true story" premium, and derision of real people. Emotion type is the wrong axis.
3. **The composite clause is unsound, and the cases are overdetermined.** Every verdict T7 gets right is also explained by lying plus materiality, by third-party harm, or by unearned credit. Where T7 departs from these, on undisclosed composites of real kinds, it contradicts journalism-ethics canon and is wrong.

## 2. The canonical and closest works

**Fiction and nonfiction**
- **Currie, *The Nature of Fiction* (1990), ch. 1 [MEM].** "fictive intent": fiction is fixed by the author's intention that the audience make-believe. A liar intends belief, so a lie is not fiction. This delivers T7's split. If the comic needs belief for the response he aims at, he asserts; if not, his "this happened" can be fictive. T7's "intended emotion" is just evidence of Currie's operative intention.
- **Carroll 1997, "Fiction, Non-fiction, and the Film of Presumptive Assertion" [MEM; paywalled].** "presumptive assertion": the maker intends the audience to entertain the content *as asserted*, which makes it answerable to standards of evidence. The scout's claim that Carroll "sorts works, not emotion types within a frame" is true but shallow. A per-claim version is a one-line extension, and Carroll's own thought theory (1990) supplies the belief/entertaining contrast that T7 relies on.
- **Plantinga 2005, "What a Documentary Is, After All", *JAAC* 63 [MEM].** "asserted veridical representation". It explicitly allows staged or reconstructed scenes so long as they are not asserted as veridical tokens. That *is* T7's "disclosed composite" clause, and it was already standard in documentary theory (the *Nanook* staging debates). See also **Currie 1999, "Visible Traces"** [MEM]: reenactments are not documentary traces.
- **Lamarque and Olsen 1994, ch. 2 [MEM].** "the fictive stance" is fixed by a practice, not by truth. A convention theorist will say stand-up's licence *is* such a practice. **Stock 2017 [MEM]**: extreme intentionalism, the same absorber as Currie.
- **Friend 2012, "Fiction as a Genre", *PAS* 112 [MEM].** No essential features separate fiction from nonfiction; truthfulness is a standard, not a necessary, feature of nonfiction. **Friend, "Criticising Misrepresentation in Fiction and Non-fiction"** (Aesthetics for Birds workshop 2020; Essex 2021) [ABSTRACT]: the usual objection "misidentifies the justification for criticising misrepresentations in non-fiction". This is the work closest to T7's *question*. Her positive account is not in the abstract, and I found no published version. **This must be checked**: the video is public, or email her. There is also her talk "Based on a True Story" (2019) [title only].
- **Matravers, *Fiction and Narrative* (2014) [PART, via the NDPR review].** Engagement with fiction and nonfiction narratives is the same: "The experience of reading de Quincey's 'The Revolt of the Tartars' is the same" (p. 78). If so, the *emotion* invited cannot be what separates permissible from impermissible invention. **Matravers 2022, "Non-Fictions and Narrative Truths", *CJP* 22** [ABSTRACT]: nonfictions resemble fictions more than the events they report.

**The paradox of fiction**
- **Radford 1975, *PASS* 49 [MEM].** The founding example is T7's "invented sick relative": a man tells a harrowing story about his sister, then reveals he made her up, and your feelings change. IEP's gloss [READ]: we feel "embarrassment for having been taken in to begin with". Radford treats the audience-side sting of invented pity stories as the *datum* the debate starts from. T7's "no third party" case is therefore not new evidence.
- **Walton 1978 and 1990, ch. 5 [MEM].** "quasi-fear": genuine emotion needs belief. No exemption for amusement, so it cuts against T7's type asymmetry.
- **Thought theory (Lamarque 1981, Carroll 1990) [MEM; IEP READ]; Gendler and Kovakovich 2006 [MEM].** To "entertain in thought" suffices, and fictional pity can be rational. So pity is not belief-dependent as a type.
- **Friend, "Fiction and Emotion: The Puzzle of Divergent Norms", *BJA* (c. 2020; verify) [ABSTRACT].** The puzzle is "a divergence in our normative assessments of emotions in different contexts". Any fittingness version of T7 must engage it.

**Sentimentality (missed by the scout)**
- **Tanner 1976–77; Jefferson 1983, "What's Wrong with Sentimentality?", *Mind* 92 [MEM].** The vice of sentimentality is eliciting emotion by falsifying the emotion's object. This is canonical precedent for tying a representational fault to the emotion invited. It is aesthetic and moral rather than a wrong to the audience, but a referee will cite it.

**Lying**
- **Bok, *Lying* (1978), ch. 5 (white lies) and ch. 7 (the "test of publicity") [MEM].** Version 1 of the DMV story is a low-stakes lie, not an innocent one. The audience's shrug shows that little was at stake, not that no duty applied.
- **Saul 2012 [MEM].** "warranting context": also frame-level. **Stokke 2018; Lasersohn 1999 [MEM]**: at-issue content and loose talk absorb T7's exaggeration point.

**The ethics of memoir, journalism and documentary**
- **Lejeune 1975 [MEM].** "pacte autobiographique": frame-level. **Couser 2004 and 2012 (on Frey) [MEM]**: the canonical worry is harm to the people represented, the strongest rival. **Eakin 2008 [MEM]**: Frey broke the "rules" of identity narrative.
- **Hersey 1980, "The Legend on the License" [MEM].** "NONE OF THIS WAS MADE UP". **Kovach and Rosenstiel 2001: "Do not add" [MEM].** Journalism bans undisclosed composites even when the kind is real, as in Janet Cooke's "Jimmy's World". T7 has to argue against this canon, not ignore it.
- **D'Agata and Fingal 2012; Nichols 1991 [MEM].** The "art licenses invention" defence; documentary as a "discourse of sobriety".

**Comedy and trust in art**
- **Hanscomb 2022, *Philosophy of Humor Yearbook* 3 [PART].** "The audience are, in a sense, being conned" (p. 68), said of *comic* fabrications. This undercuts T7's innocent side. No criterion.
- **PhilPapers DADNSD (author unseen) [ABSTRACT].** Stand-up is not the fictive stance; it refers to the actual world. **Munck 2024, *EJLW* 13 [ABSTRACT]**: on the demand for authenticity in stand-up.
- **Nguyen 2021, *Ergo* 8 [ABSTRACT].** The betrayal is when artists "sell out", acting for non-aesthetic reasons. This is aesthetic sincerity, not factual truth, so not close.

**Has anyone stated the move?** Not in any work I could find. But Radford supplies the case, Currie and Carroll supply the mechanism, Plantinga and Hersey supply the composite rule, and Bok supplies the low-stakes verdict.

## 3. Soundness

**Is amusement belief-independent?** Only in the cases where the frame already does the work.
- (a) Anecdotal comedy has a "you couldn't make it up" premium; Hanscomb says fabricated stories con the audience.
- (b) Derision of a real particular (an ex, an official, the prom date) is amusement whose fittingness needs the event. "Amusement" splits by *object*, not type.
- (c) Amusement is clearly independent of actuality only when invention is evident (tall tales), and then the frame explains the verdict.
- (d) Pity is not belief-dependent as a type either. What needs actuality is the downstream attitude toward real objects: blame, credit, solidarity, donating. The surviving criterion, "solicited attitudes toward actual particulars", is false light plus testimony, not emotion type.

**The cases.**
- **Frey.** T7 is right: admiration and hope rest on a real recovery. But lying plus materiality (the refund settlement) and third-party harm (he wrote himself into a real fatal train crash) also get it right.
- **Daisey.** T7 is right only through the "disclosed" condition. Much of his material displaced real abuses (n-hexane poisoning at Wintek, Suzhou), and outrage at Apple's supply chain largely survives correction, so T7's own disclosure test half-acquits him. Kind-level outrage is calibrated by token evidence, so invented tokens inflate beliefs about prevalence. The "kind" escape licenses illustrative propaganda.
- **Minhaj.** His defence *is* T7's composite clause (Islamophobia as a kind, "seeds of truth"). T7 rejects it only because the composites were undisclosed, so the frame does the work. The prom story's sting is third-party harm to an identifiable woman. In "Brother Eric" the laughs rest on the true FBI infiltration and the invented tackle carries the outrage, so laugh and pity do not separate.

**Simpler rivals that explain all the verdicts.**
- (i) Lying in a testimonial frame, weighted by materiality. T7's "disclosure test" (would the response survive learning the truth?) *is* the materiality test of misrepresentation law.
- (ii) Harm to third parties (Couser).
- (iii) Unearned credit. Sympathy, moral authority and sales extracted under false pretences explain why self-deprecating invention feels harmless and self-aggrandising invention does not.
- (iv) Presumptive assertion at the level of claims (Carroll and Plantinga).

## 4. Generalist appeal

The *question* has high appeal (famous scandals; the paradox of fiction in reverse). The *answer* reads as "Currie/Carroll plus reliance": a referee will say "materiality, renamed".

## 5. Best version, if one insists (not recommended for the week)

Drop emotion types and the composite clause, and argue at the level of the claim:

> In mixed-licence genres (stand-up, memoir, the monologue), which claims a teller presumptively asserts is fixed by which of the audience's solicited responses are meant to rest on belief, not by the genre's frame or explicit framing; so "emotional truth" excuses invention only where the solicited response takes no actual particular as its object.

It would still need a real case where explicit frame and solicited response *diverge* and the response wins. Joke-framed derision of a real person is the candidate, but satire law already handles it (*Hustler v. Falwell*; *Milkovich*). It would also need an answer to the materiality rival. I estimate it would not survive a hostile desk review.
