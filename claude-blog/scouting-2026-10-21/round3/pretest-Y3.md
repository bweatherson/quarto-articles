# PHASE 1: blind predictions (Q1-Q9)

## Q1. Lawful denials (spent/expunged convictions)
(a) Natural theses:
- 40%: Truth, not lie. The content of "convicted?" is fixed or restricted by the legal and social practice, so "No" is true relative to it.
- 25%: A literal falsehood the law permits, so a justified or excused lie. Rehabilitation interests override the duty of truthfulness.
- 15%: Not an assertion at all. It is a conventionalised or institutional speech act, so the lie/truth question lapses.
- 20%: Institutions can fix content only under limits (public notice, the asker's ability to reset it). The "must disclose" exception shows the asker retains the power.

(b) Literature (memory): Saul, *Lying, Misleading and What Is Said* (2012); Stokke, *Lying and Insincerity* (2018); Carson, *Lying and Deception* (2010); Fallis on lying; Sorensen on bald-faced lies; Williams, *Truth and Truthfulness*; Bok, *Lying*. Context-fixing: Lewis, "Scorekeeping" (accommodation); Stalnaker on common ground; Roberts on QUD; Burge and social externalism. Legal: Fuller, *Legal Fictions*; Bronston v US (literal-truth defence to perjury); Marmor and Soames on legal content and communication. Records: Jacobs, *The Eternal Criminal Record*; Jacobs and Larrauri 2012; Hoskins, *Beyond Punishment?* (2019); Mayer-Schönberger, *Delete*; Google Spain.

(c) Components are well-trodden (the definition of lying, legal fictions). The combination with expungement and institution-fixed question-content is fresh, and the "asker may reset" wrinkle is the freshest part.

## Q2. "People are saying"
(a) Natural theses:
- 40%: Hedges don't launder. You are answerable both for the report and for the uptake effect of circulating the accusation.
- 25%: Hedged assertion is a weaker commitment, with responsibility scaling to the evidence you disclose and its plausible uptake.
- 25%: You answer for the harm of amplification, not for the truth of the accusation.
- 10%: Only the Gricean implicatures or insinuations you carry.

(b) Literature (memory): Benton and van Elswyk, "Hedged assertion" (2020); Williamson (knowledge norm); Lackey, *Learning from Words*; Goldberg, *Assertion*; MacFarlane; Weiner 2005; evidentiality (Faller, Murray, Willett); Camp on insinuation; Saul on dogwhistles; Sunstein, *On Rumors*; Alfano and Robinson on gossip; Fricker; Moran and Hinchman on telling. Law: the common-law republication rule ("tale-bearers are as bad as tale-makers"), neutral reportage (Al-Fagih, Roberts v Gable), qualified privilege.

(c) Live. Hedged assertion is active and the accusation/gossip application is thinner. Law covers the territory well.

## Q3. Juries and residence
(a) Natural theses:
- 45%: Long-term residents should be eligible. Subjection to law and shared liability (tax, draft, subpoena) ground a jury role (all-subjected, reciprocity).
- 25%: Jury service is an exercise of popular sovereignty or political office, so it is limited to citizens (Miller-style).
- 15%: Eligibility and liability should be symmetrical (a burden/privilege parity argument).
- 15%: The defendant's right to a jury of peers, perhaps mixed (de medietate linguae revival), matters more than juror status.

(b) Literature (memory): Walzer, *Spheres of Justice*; Carens, *The Ethics of Immigration*; Goodin 2007 (all affected); Abizadeh; Song, *The Boundary Problem*; López-Guerra, *Democracy and Disenfranchisement*; Bauböck; Miller; Beckman. Jury: Duff, Farmer, Marshall and Tadros, *The Trial on Trial*; Abramson, *We, the Jury*; Amar, jury-as-political-institution; Tocqueville; Estlund, *Democratic Authority* and Condorcet; Motomura; Spiro; the historic jury *de medietate linguae*; Batson.

(c) Well-trodden in boundary-problem theory. The jury-specific angle is thinner, so partly live.

## Q4. Nocebo warnings
(a) Natural theses:
- 45%: Disclosure is a process. Elicit how much the patient wants to know, frame it, and use authorised concealment.
- 25%: A narrow therapeutic privilege is justified only with patient pre-authorisation.
- 20%: The duty to inform stays, but wording and framing change the nocebo load ("most tolerate it").
- 10%: Since the risk depends on the warning, telling does not inform about the baseline. Silence can satisfy the duty.

(b) Literature (memory): Wells and Kaptchuk, "To tell the truth..." (AJOB 2012); Colloca and Miller; Evers et al. consensus (2018); I. Glenn Cohen on nocebo and consent; Faden and Beauchamp; Beauchamp and Childress; Manson and O'Neill; Canterbury v Spence; Montgomery v Lanarkshire [2015]; Rogers v Whitaker; Bok on placebo and lying; the paternalism literature (Dworkin, Feinberg).

(c) Well-trodden in bioethics, with a live residue on the self-fulfilling structure.

## Q5. Models of assisted dying
(a) Natural theses:
- 40%: The administration model is morally minor. Both are assistance in intentional death, and the Carter-style reasoning applies to both.
- 30%: Self-administration is better. It preserves agency, reduces coercion and gives option value (many Oregon patients never use it).
- 15%: Clinician-administration is needed for equity, since many can't self-administer, but it carries more pressure and risk.
- 15%: Options can harm (burden, justification, pressure), and visibility matters.

(b) Literature (memory): Dworkin et al., "Philosophers' Brief" (1997); Dworkin, *Life's Dominion*; Rachels; Brock; Foot; Kamm; Velleman, "Against the right to die" (1992); G. Dworkin, "Is more choice better than less?" (1982); Battin; Sumner, *Assisted Death*; Hardwig, "Is there a duty to die?"; Keown; Downie; Carter v Canada (2015); Truchon.

(c) Well-trodden on permissibility and option-harm. The Oregon-vs-Canada contrast is fresher.

## Q6. Tainted eponyms
(a) Natural theses:
- 35%: Names are mostly referential. Keeping them need not honour, though the practice's expressive meaning is contextual.
- 30%: An eponym is a standing honour that persists, so rename or withdraw.
- 25%: Retention is neutral and renaming is costly (stability and confusion). Keep and contextualise.
- 10%: The botanists' slur/eponym asymmetry is defensible (slurs wound, eponyms honour).

(b) Literature (memory): Kripke and Millian reference; Appiah, *The Honor Code*; Darwall on recognition respect; Margalit, *Ethics of Memory*; Blustein; Levinson, *Written in Stone*; Matthes on monuments and heritage; Archer and Matheson, "When artists fall" (2019); Merton and Stigler on eponymy; Asperger and Reiter and Wegener renamings; Guedes et al. 2023, "Eponyms have no place..."; AOS bird renamings (2023); the ICZN and ICN Codes.

(c) Live. Monuments are well-trodden. Scientific nomenclature is fresher.

## Q7. Invented "true" stories
(a) Natural theses:
- 40%: Wronged when the "true" frame is relied on for beliefs that matter (public import). Entertainment is not.
- 30%: It's about genre convention. Memoir, journalism and stand-up differ in contract, and the wrong is breach of the understood convention.
- 20%: Telling and trust. The performer takes on responsibility for the claim, and "emotional truth" is no defence.
- 10%: A bald-faced lie or assertion-norm answer.

(b) Literature (memory): Lejeune, "The Autobiographical Pact"; Eakin; Walton, *Mimesis as Make-Believe*; Currie; Lamarque and Olsen; Searle on fictional discourse; Frankfurt, *On Bullshit*; Williams; Bok; Moran and Hinchman on telling; Owens on testimony; Baier and Hawley on trust; Carroll and Plantinga on nonfiction; Lintott and Smuts on comedy ethics; the Wilkomirski and Rosenblat cases.

(c) Live to well-trodden. The Minhaj case is fresh, and the theory is not.

## Q8. Asking permission
(a) Natural theses:
- 40%: The veto comes from your own act. Asking self-binds or confers a power (a promissory or estoppel analogue).
- 30%: There is no veto. Asking is courtesy or consultation, and you may proceed (with duties of explanation).
- 20%: It depends. Reliance and the expressive meaning of asking create the standing.
- 10%: The wrong is the insincere asking itself (pretended deference).

(b) Literature (memory): Hohfeld; Hurd, "The moral magic of consent" (1996); Alexander's reply; Owens, *Shaping the Normative Landscape* (2012); Dougherty on consent; Liberto on sexual promises; von Wright, Alchourrón and Bulygin on strong/weak permission; Raz on permissions; Dworkin on weak/strong discretion; Kamp on free-choice permission; Anscombe; Scanlon on the principle of fidelity; estoppel and waiver in law.

(c) Fresh as a specific puzzle. The consent and normative-power neighbourhood is dense, so any answer will be framed in Owens or Hurd terms.

## Q9. Revoking gifts
(a) Natural theses:
- 40%: Revocation is allowed for gross ingratitude because the gift presupposed a relationship (Roman law and BGB; an implied condition).
- 35%: Gifts are irrevocable. Title passes fully, and revocation would turn a gift into a loan (Seneca, Nozick-style).
- 15%: Relationship versus title. The benefactor has no power but there are residual moral reasons, and kinds of gift differ (heirlooms).
- 10%: Reciprocity and fairness give a claim, not a power.

(b) Literature (memory): Mauss; Derrida, *Given Time*; Seneca, *De Beneficiis*; Kant (virtue of gratitude); Smith, *TMS*; Berger, *Gratitude*; McConnell; Card; Manela on gratitude (2016, 2019); Posner on gratuitous promises; Eisenberg, *Donative Promises*; Benson on gift; Justinian (revocatio propter ingratitudinem); BGB 530; French Code civil art. 955; common-law irrevocability.

(c) Well-trodden in comparative law and gratitude theory. Largely fresh in analytic ethics (gratitude theory discusses obligations of the recipient, not the benefactor's retained power).
