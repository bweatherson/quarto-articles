# Scout G (philosophy of language): two candidates

Budget used: 23 search/fetch calls. Status tags: [READ] = read relevant part; [PART] = partial or read via a reliable summary/quotation; [ABSTRACT]; [MEM] = from memory, unchecked.

## Candidate 1 (ranked first): Lawful denials

**Question.** Many legal systems let people deny spent or expunged convictions. In Connecticut, a person whose record is erased is "deemed to have never been arrested" and "may so swear under oath". When such a person answers "No" to "Have you ever been convicted/arrested?", have they told a lie the law permits, or have they told the truth? Behind this is a general question: can an institution, rather than the person asking, fix what a question asks?

**Candidate thesis.** A question put within an institutional role (employer, insurer, licensing body) has its domain fixed by the institution's rules, not by the asker's intentions, so a statutorily licensed denial is a true answer to a restricted question, not a permitted lie.

**Core argument.**
- *Mechanism.* ROA 1974 s.4(2)(a) acts on the question itself: "the question shall be treated as not relating to spent convictions, and the answer may be framed accordingly." Liability is dealt with separately in s.4(2)(b).
- *Minimal pair.* Take the same person with the same history, asked the same words. A bank asks "Have you ever been convicted?" and the honest answer is "No". A school asks and the honest answer is "Yes". Under the Exceptions Order 1975 art. 3, the school gets the wider question only if the person "is informed at the time the question is asked that, by virtue of this Order, … spent convictions are to be disclosed." Saying something at the time of asking is how a quantifier domain is widened ("any beer, including the garage?"). It is not how a licence to lie would be withdrawn. The US N-400 likewise tells applicants to include sealed or expunged records [MEM].
- *Paradox for the rival view.* The courts' view is that the person is licensed to misrepresent: the statute "permits a person whose record has been expunged to misrepresent his past" (G.D. v Kenny, N.J. 2011) and "creates legal fictions" (Martin v Hearst, 2d Cir. 2015). If so, the state licenses false sworn statements. On the domain view there is no paradox. The newspaper's unrestricted "she was arrested" is true. Her sworn "I was never arrested" is also true, because it answers a question asked within the statutes' domain ("within the meaning of the general statutes").
- *Limit that makes the thesis do real work.* The domain is fixed only where an institutional role is in play. A new partner who asks has not had their question scoped, so denying a spent conviction to them is still a lie, whatever s.4 says about liability. The warranty also stays in force: denying an *unspent* conviction is still a lie.
- *Payoff.* This is a large-scale, real case in which content is fixed against the asker's intentions. That is evidence in the intentionalist versus conventionalist debate in metasemantics.

**Canonical treatments.**
- Mahon, SEP "The Definition of Lying and Deception" [PART]. Covers "not at home" as a conventional euphemism (Isenberg 1973; Coleman & Kay 1981), Carson's warranty account and Grotius. It says nothing on legally licensed denials or on question content fixed by an institution.
- Carson, *Lying and Deception* (2010) [PART via SEP]. Lying requires warranting truth, and some contexts carry no warranty. **This is the main threat of absorption.** Reply: the warranty survives, so the warranty view still has to say *which* content is warranted, and the answer is the restricted content. The claim about content therefore comes first.
- Isenberg 1973 [via SEP]; Sidgwick, *Methods* Bk III [MEM]. A conventional falsehood is not a lie when the convention is mutually understood. That does not cover an employer who doesn't know the law, which is the case the thesis needs.
- Fuller, *Legal Fictions* (1967) [MEM]. A fiction is a falsehood that everyone knows to be false. The courts' "legal fiction" reading is Fuller's; the thesis says the denial is not a fiction at all.
- Stanley & Szabó 2000; King 2014 (coordination account); Lepore & Stone 2015 [all MEM]. These are the main works on domain restriction and metasemantics. None of them, as I recall, discusses a domain fixed by a third party such as a statute.
- Primary sources: ROA s.4 [READ]; Exceptions Order art. 3 [READ]; Martin v Hearst [PART]; G.D. v Kenny and Bahr v Statesman Journal ("It does not make that representation true"), both as quoted in Martin n.6 [PART].

**Recent closest work.** I found none in philosophy (two targeted searches; the SEP entry is silent). Legal writing treats the matter as policy (e.g., a Cambridge Law Journal article on reformed offenders' disclosures [title only]).

**Obvious answer.** A court would say it is a lie the law permits (a legal fiction). A philosopher of lying would say it is not a lie, because of convention or because there is no warranty. The thesis differs from both. There is no licence and no mutual convention. The answer is true because the question was restricted. The decisive evidence is structural: the informing requirement, and the fact that the domain view dissolves the problem of licensed sworn statements.

**Biggest risk.** A referee may call this a reframing that Carson absorbs ("content versus warranty"), or say it is obvious once you read s.4(2)(a). The paper must show cases where the verdicts diverge. Examples are sworn denials and perjury, an employer who doesn't know the law, the partner case, and the N-400 applicant whose "No" is false but not a lie. A second risk: after "apology statutes" and "quashed convictions", BW may be wary of topics driven by statutes.

**Confidence.** Medium.

## Candidate 2 (ranked second): "People are saying"

**Question.** When you pass on an accusation marked as hearsay ("I've heard…", "allegedly", "people are saying"), what are you answerable for? Is it the wording, the fact that people are saying it, or the accusation itself?

**Candidate thesis.** A report of an accusation that is offered as bearing on whether the accusation is true has to meet the epistemic standard of that question. It is proper only if the reporter has reason to think that someone in the chain vouches for the accusation on grounds other than its circulation. So "I'm only telling you what people are saying" is no defence.

**Core argument.**
- *Minimal pair.* "There's a rumour you're taking bribes; thought you should know," said to Jones, is fine. The identical sentence said to Jones's boss is improper unless the speaker has grounds. Content, truth, knowledge and hedge are all the same. A knowledge norm on content predicts no difference. The difference tracks the question being addressed: what is being said about Jones, versus whether Jones is corrupt. An explicit disclaimer ("no idea if it's true") does not repair the second case, so the problem is not an implicature.
- *Structural argument.* Circulation counts as evidence only if relayers mostly relay on independent grounds. A norm that let circulation alone license relaying would let every accusation license its own spread, and would destroy the very evidential value it relies on. This explains how rumour can be evidence (Coady; Dentith) without being a warrant to pass it on.
- *Institutional convergence.* Defamation law's repetition rule says the same. Lord Devlin: "for the purpose of the law of libel a hearsay statement is the same as a direct statement". Lord Reid: "repeating someone else's libellous statement is just as bad as making the statement directly" (Lewis v Daily Telegraph, quoted in Curistan [2] [READ]). Justification must rest on underlying facts, "not merely … some second-hand report" (Shah, quoted at [53] [READ]); see also the Chase levels ([9] [READ]). The exceptions track the question being addressed: reportage of a dispute (Defamation Act 2013 s.4(3) [MEM]), privileged reports of proceedings (Curistan [59]–[60], [84] [READ]), and reputation evidence (FRE 405 [MEM]).

**Canonical treatments.**
- Simons 2007, "Observations on embedding verbs…" [PART]. In "Henry said p" given as an answer, p is the main point, but only the attribution is asserted. With "I heard", "the claim is as reliable as my sources". This supplies the pragmatics; its normative upshot, deferring to the sources, is what the thesis denies.
- Brandom, *Making It Explicit* (1994) [MEM]. A reasserter can defer justificatory challenges to the source. The thesis allows deferral only to sources who vouch.
- Williamson 2000, ch. 11 [MEM]. The knowledge norm is satisfied by "people say p", so it mispredicts the minimal pair.
- E. Fricker 2012, "Stating and insinuating" [MEM]. Insinuation lets speakers escape accountability. The thesis differs because the violation survives explicit cancellation.
- Stokke 2018, *Lying and Insincerity* [MEM]. Misleading is defined via the question under discussion, but only for speakers who believe the implied answer false. It does not cover the agnostic relayer.
- Coady 2006, "Rumour has it" [MEM]; Dentith, "Have you heard? The rumour as reliable" [title only]. Both are compatible with the thesis.

**Recent closest work.** Frigerio & Tenchini 2023, "Sharing", *Ratio* [PART]: reporters are "usually answerable only for faithfulness". It surveys Marsili 2021, Rini 2017, and Pepp, Michaelson & Sterken. Cuonzo 2026, *Studia UBB Philosophia* [PART]: a rumour's disclaimer "passes the responsibility for evidence along". These are the views the thesis targets.

**Obvious answer.** "You assert only that it's being said. You answer for the report's accuracy, perhaps for an implied claim that it's credible, and for harm (the ethics of gossip)." The thesis differs in three ways: it states an epistemic norm keyed to the question, the norm survives cancellation, and the bootstrapping argument specifies what grounds are needed.

**Biggest risk.** A referee may say it is absorbed by Simons, Fricker and Stokke together ("main-point content is governed by norms anyway"), or by context-sensitive norm views (Goldberg 2015, *Assertion* [MEM]). It may also be read as gossip ethics. It has more canonical neighbours than Candidate 1, and I have not checked them all.

**Confidence.** Medium-low to medium.

---

## Ranking

1. **Lawful denials.** It has a clear target (the courts' "legal fiction" reading), structural evidence (the requirement to inform at the time of asking) and no philosophical treatment I could find. Its pull for generalists is somewhat weaker.
2. **"People are saying".** It has broader appeal and richer literature, and it intervenes in the live debate about assertion and sharing. It has more canonical works that could absorb it.

Both start from real legal practice, and their arguments rest on structure rather than on contested intuitions about rare cases.
