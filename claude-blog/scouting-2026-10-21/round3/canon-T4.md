# Canon check: T4 (nocebo side effects; silence meets material-risk doctrine), 21 Oct 2026

About 28 search or fetch calls. I got full texts of PMC items through the NCBI E-utilities API. Status marks: [READ], [PART], [ABSTRACT], [MEM] (from memory, unchecked).

## 1. Verdict: DEAD (anticipated, and unsound as legal doctrine)

- **The core argument is in print, in the paper T4 treats as its foil.** Wells & Kaptchuk 2012 already argue three things:
  - the side-effect profile has no "truth" that is independent of what the doctor says;
  - full and limited disclosure are *both* truthful relative to the outcomes they produce;
  - so the doctor should pick the less harmful one, and the "perceived tension" is resolved.

  That is T4's Premises 2–3 and its conclusion, put in terms of truthfulness rather than materiality.
- **The "no conflict, no explicit authorisation" conclusion is also in print.** Specker Sullivan & Niker 2017 argue that the nocebo "conflict dissolves" (by a different route).
- **What is left is a relabelling.** Swapping "truthful relative to the outcome" for "material relative to the policy" is a reframing the rivals can absorb.
- **The scout misdescribed the sources, which would fail the accuracy criterion.**
  - "Authorized concealment" is not in Wells & Kaptchuk; a full-text search finds no occurrence. The term comes from Colloca & Miller 2011.
  - Wells & Kaptchuk do *not* treat the problem as a real conflict.
- **The legal half fails on the doctrine's own wording:**
  - *Rogers* sets materiality by what the patient would think "if warned".
  - *Montgomery* requires disclosure of "variant treatments", and silent versus disclosed prescribing are variants.
  - *Canterbury* already files this exact case under the therapeutic privilege.

## 2. Canonical and closest works

**Wells & Kaptchuk 2012, *AJOB* 12(3):22–29 [READ, PMC3352765]**
- In the section "What Is an Accurate Description of Adverse Effects?", they deny there is any "abstract 'truth' about a medication's side effect profile".
- Their Patient A/B pair is T4's minimal pair. A is told everything; B is told only the drug-specific effects. "Patient B was also told the truth", and so "the best option is the one that involves the least harm."
- B's being told the *drug-specific* effects already gives T4's prediction (iii).
- Under "Contextualized Informed Consent" they consider asking patients first. They object: "how would patients be able to weigh the benefit of gained information" without knowing the effects in question? This anticipates T4's case against meta-consent.
- They cite Manson & O'Neill as support ("adequately accurate" information, as against "illusory completeness").

**Colloca & Miller 2011, *Psychosom Med* 73:598–603 [READ, PMC3167012]**
- From the same premise ("not necessarily independent of information disclosure") they infer a *tension*. They propose that "a technique of 'authorized concealment' merits consideration."
- On their account, authorised concealment "constitutes a valid waiver by the patient."
- Serious risks are never concealed. This is T4's prediction (iii) again, so (iii) does not separate T4 from its rivals.
- They raise the key counter-case: concealed effects that would lead the patient to refuse, given her values.

**Miller & Colloca 2011, *Theor Med Bioeth* 32:229–243 [ABSTRACT]**
- Risks and benefits "can be shaped powerfully by information about these treatments."
- So risk assessment and consent are interdependent. This is T4's Premise 2, stated as a general thesis.
- As quoted in Colloca 2017, they hold that a waiver "does not constitute informed consent" but can be valid consent.

**Alfano 2015, *AJOB* 15(10):3–12 [ABSTRACT]**
- "The process of informing cannot be considered independently from the potential effects."
- He takes this to *support* authorised concealment and authorised deception, which is the opposite of T4's inference.

**Cohen 2014, *Bioethics* 28:147–154 [ABSTRACT]; Cohen 2017, *AJOB* 17(6):44–46 [title only]**
- The dilemma is "especially pernicious", since the caregiver directly causes the harm.
- The 2017 title is "Preventing nocebo effects… without paternalism". A Semantic Scholar snippet (source unverified) argues that non-disclosure here is not paternalistic, so putting non-maleficence first is "trivially" right. The author must read this; it may be a third statement of T4's conclusion.

**Fortunato, Wasserman & Menkes 2017, *AJOB* 17(6):36–42 [ABSTRACT]**
- "Autonomy vis-à-vis informed consent must be forestalled."
- This is the override view T4 opposes. It drew nine commentaries.

**Specker Sullivan & Niker 2017, *AJOB* 17(6):52–54 [READ, author's manuscript, Stirling repository]**
- With relational autonomy, "this conflict dissolves."
- Non-disclosure need not violate autonomy "without an explicit expression" of the patient's will.
- This gives T4's conclusion: no dilemma, and no authorisation needed.

**Colloca 2017, *AJOB* 17(6):46–48 [READ, PMC5545888]**
- She rejects forestalling autonomy and defends authorised concealment, framing and nocebo education.
- She holds that nocebo can be reduced "without compromising the patient rights to receive honest information."

**Schoene-Seifert 2017, "Beware of nocebo-paternalism" [title]; Bryan & Dubljević 2017 [title]; Barilan 2012, "Ulysses contracts and the nocebo effect" [title]; Miller 2012, "Clarifying the nocebo effect and its ethical implications" (*AJOB* 12(3):30–31) [title]**
- Miller 2012 is unread, and is probably the direct critique of Wells & Kaptchuk's "no truth" claim. It is the critique T4 would inherit.

**Blease 2015, "Authorized concealment and authorized deception: well-intended secrets are likely to induce nocebo effects" [title]**
- If concealment is itself nocebogenic once patients know the practice, then a *public* policy of silence may not have the low-expectation fixed point T4 assumes.

**Gelfand 2020, *CQHE* 29:223 [ABSTRACT]; Ho 2020, *CQHE* 29:236 [PART: opening and notes]**
- Both treat the conflict as real.
- Ho: patients cannot act autonomously if information is withheld "(without their consent, perhaps)."

**Howick 2021, *JME* 47:590–594 [ABSTRACT]**
- Nocebo is overestimated: adverse events in placebo arms may be natural history, so a no-treatment arm is needed.
- "Non-maleficence must be taken into account alongside autonomy", which is a balancing view.
- This weakens T4's empirical carve-out of side effects "produced mainly by expectation".

**Barnes et al. 2019, *Front Pharmacol* 10:167 [title]; Webster et al. 2018, *PEC* [title]**
- Positive framing reduces nocebo, so "full listing versus silence" is a false binary.

**Sieg et al. 2024, *Eur J Pain* [ABSTRACT]; Clemens et al. 2024, *Pain* [ABSTRACT]**
- In a sample of 2,766 people, 65–76% favour disclosure of *all* potential side effects, and 84% put autonomy above non-maleficence.
- People want the information less when side effects are described as rarer or milder.

**Canonical bioethics [MEM]**
- **Faden & Beauchamp 1986.** Consent is autonomous authorisation, with understanding (not disclosure) at its core. Waiver is the patient's own act, and therapeutic privilege is an exception. Their framework lets one argue that the patient need not understand trivial risks, but it gives no policy-relative materiality.
- **Beauchamp & Childress, 8th ed., ch. 4 ("Intentional nondisclosure").** The privilege is narrow, and they endorse *authorised* deception for placebos. That is the authorisation model T4 denies.
- **Manson & O'Neill 2007, chs. 2–4.** The transactional model replaces the conduit-and-container model. Consent waives specific prohibitions and needs only no deception or coercion, not completeness. This already permits non-deceptive silence about mild risks without any materiality detour, and Wells & Kaptchuk invoke it for exactly that.

**Law**
- ***Montgomery* [2015] UKSC 11 [READ, National Archives XML]:**
  - [85]: waiver is triggered by the patient (she "does not wish to be informed").
  - [87]: materiality is whether the patient "would be likely to attach significance to the risk"; the doctor must also disclose "reasonable alternative or variant treatments".
  - [88]: the exception covers what is "seriously detrimental to the patient's health".
  - [89]: materiality "cannot be reduced to percentages".
  - [91]: the exception is "not intended to subvert that principle".
- ***Rogers v Whitaker* (1992) 175 CLR 479, 490 [READ, as quoted in *Montgomery* [72]]:** a risk is material if a reasonable patient, "if warned of the risk, would be likely to attach significance to it."
- ***Canterbury v Spence*, 464 F.2d 772 (1972) [READ, Caselaw Access Project]:**
  - Part VI places disclosure that would "complicate or hinder the treatment" under the privilege, which must not "devour the disclosure rule itself."
  - It also holds there is "no responsibility for discussion of hazards the patient has already discovered."
- **Cave 2017, *Common Law World Rev* [PART].** After *Montgomery*, the therapeutic exception is "obfuscatory, unnecessary and unjustified". It has no nocebo discussion.

**Self-fulfilling information [MEM]**
- Merton 1948.
- Grunberg & Modigliani 1954 and Simon 1954, on fixed points of public predictions.
- Jeffrey 1983, on ratifiability.
- Perdomo et al. 2020, on "performative stability".

T4's compliance test *is* performative stability or ratifiability applied to disclosure. That is the "known device plus an application" shape the brief warns against.

## 3. Soundness

**(a) Coherent but idle, and self-validating.**
- The fixed-point test is coherent, but it has several solutions: full disclosure, silence, and every partial list. It therefore selects nothing. All the work is done by whoever chooses the equilibrium.
- That choice fixes which risk profile the patient's body will carry. It is the very decision consent law assigns to her.
- The test also certifies any non-disclosure that lowers the risk it conceals, scaled to the size of the expectation effect. That is self-validation in the precise sense.
- "Produced mainly by expectation" is not a clean class.
  - Finasteride's sexual side effects rose roughly threefold when patients were told about them (Mondaini 2007 [MEM]), yet part of the risk is attributable to the drug.
  - Howick shows that the placebo-arm estimates T4 relies on overstate nocebo.

**(b) Choosing silence does need the patient's authority, on the doctrine's own terms.**
- "Disclose, then prescribe" and "prescribe silently" are variants of one treatment with different side-effect rates. *Montgomery* [87] makes such variants disclosable.
- The material fact is the *dependence structure*: "telling you raises your risk from 2% to 20%". It is not the risk under whichever policy the doctor adopts.
- A reasonable patient plainly attaches significance to that. Disclosing it is exactly Colloca & Miller's authorised concealment or nocebo education.
- Waiver in *Montgomery* [85] is triggered by the patient.
- *Canterbury* and *Montgomery* [91] treat harm-based silence as an exception that must not "devour" or "subvert" the rule. T4 relocates the exception into the *definition* of materiality, which is the move both courts block.

**(c) The legal test is not met.**
- *Rogers*' counterfactual, which *Montgomery* [73] calls "undoubtedly right", evaluates significance in the *warned* world. There the nocebo risk is high, so silence fails the test.
- [89] rules out "low under silence, therefore immaterial".
- The empirical evidence on what reasonable patients care about (Sieg 2024) points toward disclosure.
- Silence is rarely the policy actually in force. Package leaflets must list adverse reactions under EU Directive 2001/83, art. 59 [MEM], and *Montgomery* [85] itself mentions the leaflet.

**(d) The predictions do not discriminate.**
- Prediction (ii) is the standard "particular patient" limb (*Rogers*; *Montgomery* [73]). Read literally, it collides with *Canterbury*'s "already discovered" rule.
- Prediction (iii) is held by Colloca & Miller and by Wells & Kaptchuk.

## 4. Generalist appeal

The *general* problem is attractive: duties to inform when the information is self-fulfilling. It connects to performative prediction, and to Simon and Grunberg & Modigliani on public predictions. But T4 is that general idea applied to one legal doctrine, and its conclusion is the one Wells & Kaptchuk drew in the most-cited paper in the area. A judge who checks Wells & Kaptchuk will score originality and accuracy low.

## 5. Best version, if it survives

It does not survive as stated. The nearby positions are all occupied:
- "Disclose the dependence, not the list": Colloca 2017; Nestoriuc 2021.
- "Nocebo effects are not risks of the treatment": Howick 2021; Colloca & Miller on attribution.
- "Relational autonomy dissolves it": Specker Sullivan & Niker.

The only unoccupied fragment is narrow and doctrinal: whether *Rogers*' "if warned" clause, dropped from *Montgomery* [87], fixes the world in which materiality is evaluated. That argues *against* T4 and has little generalist appeal. I recommend abandoning T4.
