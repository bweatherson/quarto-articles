## Candidate 1: "Who may judge?" Jury service is a duty of justice, not a privilege of the demos

**Question.** Resident non-citizens are tried, taxed, subpoenaed and (in the US, for men aged 18-25) required to register for the draft, yet are excluded from juries almost everywhere. Who should be eligible, and liable, to judge a defendant: "the people", or those who live under the same law? The boundary problem in democratic theory has been posed almost only for the vote.

**Candidate thesis.** Jury eligibility and liability should follow ordinary residence under the state's justice system rather than citizenship, because jury service is a compulsory duty of justice owed to defendants, not an exercise of popular self-rule.

**Core argument.**
1. Structure. The jury is lot-selected, ad hoc, secret, unaccountable, bound to apply the law as instructed, and unable to make law. It checks officials on behalf of the accused. It does not authorise lawmakers, which is what the vote does.
2. The two stated rationales for citizen-only rule are *Perkins v. Smith* (D. Md. 1974): familiarity with local institutions, and allegiance. Neither picks out citizens. Familiarity tracks length of residence. Allegiance is untested and unguaranteed: naturalised and dual citizens serve, and voir dire handles bias. The remaining rationale is the "political function" doctrine (*Foley*, *Ambach*), and it presupposes that the jury is an organ of sovereignty. Step 1 denies that.
3. Practice is already residence-based in places. UK Juries Act 1974 s.1 asks for electoral registration plus five years' ordinary residence and never mentions citizenship [READ]. The vicinage clause is territorial. The *de medietate linguae* mixed jury ran about 600 years. California AB 1401 (2013) passed the Assembly 45-26, with no recorded opposition arguments [PART].
4. Positive ground. The duty is to do one's share in just institutions that apply to us (Rawls's natural duty of justice, with Waldron's particularity). That explains why it binds residents but not tourists.
5. Minimal pair. Maria (permanent resident, 20 years, registered for the draft at 18) and Tom (citizen) commit the same offence in the same court. The state asks Maria for every burden of the justice system (defendant, witness, taxpayer, conscript) except the one that is, in legal form, a compulsory unpaid duty.
6. Payoff for generalists. Boundary answers are function-relative: co-liability for adjudication, membership for legislation. A reader can deny non-citizen national voting and still accept the thesis.

**Canonical treatments.**
- SEP "Democracy" §7, the boundary problem [PART]. It cites Whelan 1983, Goodin 2007, Abizadeh 2008, Miller 1995 and Song 2012, and says nothing about juries or judging. The gap in the standard reference is confirmed.
- Constable, *The Law of the Other* (1994) [MEM; existence and subtitle seen in search]. A history of the mixed jury and changing conceptions of citizenship. It is not a normative case for modern eligibility, as far as I recall.
- Amar, *The Bill of Rights* (1998), the "populist" jury [MEM]. This is the main rival: jury as the people, so citizens only. Must be answered.
- Duff, Farmer, Marshall and Tadros, *The Trial on Trial* vol. 3 (2007), and Duff, *Answering for Crime* (2007) [MEM]. The trial as the community calling the defendant to account. It is either support or rival, and I have not checked what it says about non-citizen defendants. Needs a check.
- Rawls, *TJ* §§19 and 51; Waldron, "Special Ties and Natural Duties" (1993); Walzer, *Obligations* (1970) [MEM]. Source of the duty ground. I do not know whether anyone has asked what grounds the duty to serve on a jury. A quick search found essentially no philosophical treatment, only an Uehiro prize essay on deadlock.
- Case law: *Perkins v. Smith*, aff'd 426 U.S. 913 (1976); *Carter v. Jury Commission* (1970, dictum); *Gordon-Nikkar* (5th Cir. 1978) [PART, via Blackman/Volokh 2013 and the California bill analysis]. *Powers v. Ohio* and *J.E.B. v. Alabama* treat jury service as a right of the citizen-juror [MEM].

**Recent closest work.** Blackman and Volokh blog exchange on the vicinage clause (2013) [PART]. California AB 1401 committee analysis [PART]. *Stanford L. Rev.* 64(6) "The American Jury" and the Oñati "Popular Sovereignty and the Jury Trial" appeared in search but I did not read them.

**Obvious answer.** A specialist would say jury service is a core civic function tied to citizenship (allegiance, political function). At most there is a stakes argument, as for non-citizen voting. My thesis does not run through stakes or participation rights. It runs through the ground of the duty and the structure of the institution, and it says the boundary depends on function.

**Biggest risk.**
- "No conscription without representation." If the argument is that residents *owe* service, imposing it on the voteless looks exploitative. Retreating to "eligible, with a right to decline" gives a "may" thesis, which fails the desk screen. I need a principled reply: the natural duty does not depend on voice, and exclusion does not protect voice anyway.
- Amar's and Duff's community conceptions could make the jury a membership institution.
- It may read as US-centric legal policy rather than philosophy.

**Confidence.** Low-medium.

## Candidate 2: Conscription or microcosm? Why advisory citizens' assemblies cannot be what they claim

**Question.** Assemblies are defended as lot-selected microcosms of the public. In practice an invitation lottery is followed by *voluntary* acceptance, often by a small minority. The one lottery institution that is representative, the jury, compels service. Can a volunteer assembly be representative?

**Candidate thesis.** A lot-selected body represents the public only to the extent that service is compelled. Compulsion is justified only where the body's decisions bind, as jury verdicts do. So advisory assemblies, voluntary of necessity, cannot be microcosms, and their authority must be argued on other grounds.

**Core argument.**
1. The representative and egalitarian claim of sortition rests on non-self-selection. Refusal gets no exit (Manin, Guerrero, Stone).
2. Jury duty is compelled with narrow excuses. The pool therefore approximates the population on unobserved dimensions such as political interest. Assemblies rely on honoraria and volunteering. Quotas repair observed demographics, not unobserved dispositions (interest, trust, spare time).
3. Compulsion needs a claimant. The defendant has a claim to lay peers, and the juror's duty is its correlative. Nobody has a claim to an advisory assembly's service, and nobody is bound by its output. Compelled lot-drawn service elsewhere (for example, Spain's polling-station boards [MEM]) protects the integrity of a binding procedure. It does not generate advice.
4. Trilemma. Binding, compulsory and representative describes the jury. Advisory, voluntary and unrepresentative of the unwilling describes the Irish and French assemblies. Making assemblies binding is what would make them representative, and it is also what Lafont fears.
5. Minimal pair: a jury of 12 and an assembly of 99 drawn from the same list differ in whether refusal is allowed.

**Canonical treatments.**
- Manin, *Principles of Representative Government* (1997) [MEM].
- Guerrero 2014, "Against Elections: The Lottocratic Alternative", *PPA* [PART: cited in SEP "Democracy" §4.2.6; text unread]. I have not checked whether he proposes compulsory service. If he does, the thesis is partly anticipated.
- Lafont, *Democracy without Shortcuts* (2019) [PART: via SEP]. Her objection is about excluding citizens from self-rule, not about volunteering.
- Landemore, *Open Democracy* (2020) [MEM]. She embraces self-selection ("open" minipublics), which is the main rival.
- Fishkin, *When the People Speak* (2009), and Goodin and Dryzek 2006 on uptake [MEM].
- Abizadeh 2021 (lot-selected second chamber) and Stone, *The Luck of the Draw* (2011) [MEM].

**Recent closest work.** Jacquet 2017 on non-participation in minipublics (empirical) [MEM]. The Flanigan-Procaccia selection algorithms (Nature 2021) [title only seen]. These treat volunteering as a technical selection problem.

**Obvious answer.** "Volunteer bias is a technical problem solved by stratified quotas, oversampling and pay." The thesis says the bias is structural, tied to the assembly's lack of authority.

**Biggest risk.**
- Landemore and Fishkin can reply that representativeness of the unwilling was never the point: informed, diverse deliberation is.
- Whether willingness correlates with relevant views is empirical.
- Guerrero may already require compulsion.
- It could be read as a "so what" or as a reframing the sortition camp can absorb.

**Confidence.** Low.

## Ranking
1. Candidate 1. The gap is checked (SEP, UK statute, bill analysis), the minimal pair is ordinary, and it is an intervention in the live boundary debate. The "no conscription without representation" objection must be answered first.
2. Candidate 2. More general appeal but thinner novelty, and its key premise (acceptance rates, Guerrero on compulsion) is unchecked.

## Considered and killed on canon or predictability (so nobody repeats them)
- **Caretaker and lame-duck restraint.** Canada's Guidelines list five tests: routine, non-controversial, urgent, reversible, agreed by the opposition [READ]. The official rationale is parliamentary confidence. "Do not foreclose the voters' choice" is the obvious answer, and mandate and foreclosure rationales are not separable.
- **Chenery and "actual reasons".** The discretion hinge is Chenery's own text (Stack, "Constitutional Foundations of Chenery", 2007). *Whren* and rational-basis review are counterexamples. Gardner on unknowing justification and Audi's "secular motivation" (a counterfactual-sufficiency test) already cover the public-reason version.
- **Unreasoned jury verdicts and public reason.** Schauer, "Giving Reasons" (1995), Ho's case for reasoned verdicts, *Taxquet v Belgium*, and a Legal Studies article on explained verdicts [titles only] are crowded.
- **Emergency ex post ratification.** Gross (2003), Ackerman (2004), Posner and Vermeule.
- **Citizenship-based taxation.** Kirsch and Mason in law; thin on philosophy but off-persona.
- **Collective responsibility, ministerial directions, opposition rights.** The Bundestag 2014 opposition-rights reform supports a scrutiny rationale, not an alternation rationale.
