# Pretest X3: predictability and canon test

Phase 1 was written after reading only pretest-questions.md. I opened no other scouting3 file and ran no web search. All literature is cited from memory [M]. Treat dates and titles as unverified.

## Phase 1: predicted theses and literature

**Q1. Lawful denials.**
(a) The predicted theses are these. (1) It is a lie the law permits or excuses: the denial is false-in-fact, and "deemed" is a legal fiction (35%). (2) It is true: the legal or institutional context fixes the question under discussion, so "convicted" means convicted-and-not-spent (30%). (3) It is not a lie because assertoric force or the sincerity presumption is suspended in that context (Shiffrin's "justified suspended contexts") (20%). (4) It is true-in-law but false-simpliciter, so the content is context-relative (15%).
(b) [M] Kogon and Loughery, "Sealing and Expungement of Criminal Records: The Big Lie" (JCLC, 1970); Jacobs, *The Eternal Criminal Record* (2015); Fuller, *Legal Fictions* (1967); Schauer on legal fictions; Shiffrin, *Speech Matters* (2014); Carson (2010); Saul (2012); Stokke (2018); Sorensen on bald-faced lies (2007); Roberts on the QUD (2012); Lewis, "Scorekeeping" (1979); Hoskins, *Beyond Punishment?* (2019); the UK ROA commentary (Henley, Larrauri).
(c) The lying literature is well-trodden, but the institution-fixes-the-question angle is fairly fresh. Overall: live.

**Q2. "People are saying."**
(a) (1) You are answerable for the accusation itself, because hearsay marking does not launder. This is the defamation repetition rule plus implicature or insinuation (35%). (2) You assert only the report, but you are answerable for foreseeable uptake and for the harms of spreading it (30%). (3) Relaying is a distinct speech act with its own norm: you need grounds that it is said *and* that it is worth passing on (20%). (4) "People are saying" is a deniability device (Camp-style insinuation), so the speaker is on the hook for what they insinuated (15%).
(b) [M] On the law: the repetition rule ("tale-bearers are as bad as tale-makers"), *Lewis v Daily Telegraph* [1964], and the US neutral-reportage doctrine. On linguistics: reportative evidentials (Faller 2002; Murray 2017), where reportatives do not commit the speaker to p. On philosophy: Camp, "Insinuation…" (2018); Saul on dogwhistles and racial figleaves (2017/18); Coady, "Rumour Has It" (2006) and *What to Believe Now* (2012); Rini, "Fake News and Partisan Epistemology" (2017), on retweets as "bent testimony"; Marsili on retweeting (2021); Arielli on sharing (2018); Goldberg, *Assertion* (2015); Lackey on selfless assertion; Alfano and Robinson on gossip (2017).
(c) Live and crowded. The sharing/retweet and evidentials literatures are very active.

**Q3. Juries and residence.**
(a) (1) Those subject to the law should be eligible and liable: the all-subjected principle applied to juries (40%). (2) Jury service is a political right of citizens, on a par with voting, so exclusion is justified (25%). (3) The peer/representativeness view: non-citizen defendants have a claim to non-citizen jurors, as in the jury *de medietate linguae* (20%). (4) An asymmetry thesis: liability tracks subjection, and eligibility tracks membership (15%).
(b) [M] Constable, *The Law of the Other* (1994), on the mixed jury; Ramirez on *de medietate linguae* (1994); V. Amar, "Jury Service as Political Participation Akin to Voting" (Cornell LR, 1995); Abramson, *We, the Jury* (1994). California AB 1401 (2013), which would have allowed non-citizen jurors, was vetoed by Brown. Duff, *Answering for Crime* (2007) and *The Realm of Criminal Law* (2018), on calling to account in the name of the polity, including "guests". Duff et al., *The Trial on Trial*. Abizadeh (2008); Goodin, "Enfranchising All Affected Interests" (2007); López-Guerra (2005, 2014); Carens, *The Ethics of Immigration* (2013), on social membership; Song (2018); Raskin on non-citizen voting (1993); Walzer, *Spheres* (1983).
(c) The boundary problem is well-trodden. Juries specifically are fresh in philosophy but trodden in law reviews.

**Q4. Nocebo warnings.**
(a) (1) Use "contextualised" consent or authorised concealment: ask the patient whether they want to hear about nocebo-prone side effects, and use meta-consent (40%). (2) The duty is met by framing, not silence. Positive framing satisfies materiality (30%). (3) Therapeutic privilege applies narrowly and covers this case (15%). (4) A self-fulfilling risk does not exist absent disclosure, so silence omits nothing material (15%).
(b) [M] Wells and Kaptchuk, "To Tell the Truth, the Whole Truth, May Do Patients Harm" (AJOB, 2012), with its open peer commentaries; Colloca and Miller (2011); Cohen, "The Nocebo Effect of Informed Consent" (Bioethics, 2014); Miller and Colloca on authorised concealment; Ploug and Holm on meta-consent; Blease on nocebo and consent; Alfano on placebo and consent (AJOB, 2015); Corsi and Colloca (2017); *Montgomery v Lanarkshire* [2015] on material risk and the therapeutic exception; O'Neill and Manson (2007); the SAMSON statin nocebo trial (2020).
(c) Well-trodden. An AJOB target article plus a decade of follow-up.

**Q5. Models of assisted dying.**
(a) (1) The Velleman thesis: having the option harms by making continued existence something you must justify, and harms more where the option is institutional and visible to others (35%). (2) The kept prescription has "insurance" or comfort value: about a third of Oregon patients never take it, so holding the option benefits (25%). (3) The clinician model is better for capacity and safety at the time of death. The modes differ only in safeguards (20%). (4) The real issue is final consent and advance requests (Canada's waiver) (20%).
(b) [M] Velleman, "Against the Right to Die" (J Med Phil, 1992) and "A Right of Self-Termination?" (Ethics, 1999); G. Dworkin, "Is More Choice Better Than Less?" (1982); Schelling on harmful options; Dworkin, Frey and Bok (1998); Sumner, *Assisted Death* (2011); Battin; Cholbi; Brock (1992); the Oregon DWDA annual reports.
(c) The core question ("can an option harm, does visibility matter") is canonical Velleman and Dworkin territory. Only the mode comparison is fresh-ish.

**Q6. Tainted eponyms.**
(a) (1) Continued display or use keeps honouring, as with statues, so renaming is permissible or required (35%). (2) Names only refer. The honour was in the baptism, so continued use is not honouring and stability wins (25%). (3) A middle view: honours can be withdrawn (degrees, knighthoods), but nomenclature has reference costs, so contextualise or annotate (25%). (4) The *caffra* asymmetry is principled because slurs carry content, whereas an eponym's honour lies in the act (15%).
(b) [M] The statue literature: Frowe, "The Duty to Remove Statues of Wrongdoers" (JPE, 2019); Lai, "Political Vandalism as Counter-Speech" (Ethics, 2020); Schulz, "Must Rhodes Fall?" (2019); Burch-Brown (2017); Lim, on tainted commemorations (2020); Timmerman (2020); Levinson, *Written in Stone* (1998). The Yale Calhoun renaming principles (Witt, 2016). Medical eponyms: Reiter's, Wegener's, Asperger's. Guedes et al., "Eponyms have no place in 21st-century biological nomenclature" (Nature E&E, 2023). Kripke on names as rigid designators; on honour, Brennan and Pettit, *The Economy of Esteem* (2004).
(c) The statues debate is well-trodden. Eponyms and the refer-vs-honour question are fresh-ish.

**Q7. Invented "true" stories.**
(a) (1) Genre contract: the wrong occurs when "this happened" is uttered in a genre whose conventions make it testimony (memoir, reportage, the monologue-as-journalism) rather than in stand-up (35%). (2) The use test: the wrong occurs when the anecdote is offered as evidence for a factual or political claim (Daisey on Foxconn, Minhaj on racism) (30%). (3) "Emotional truth" fails, because the audience's response was to a person and a real event, so the wrong is to their affective investment (20%). (4) Fiction-making: the performer does not assert, so there is no lie, only possibly misleading (15%).
(b) [M] Lejeune, "The Autobiographical Pact" (1975), is canonical. Also Friend, "Fiction as a Genre" (2012); Currie (1990); Lamarque and Olsen (1994); Walton (1990); García-Carpintero on fiction-making (2007, 2019); Stock (2017); Matravers (2014); Couser, *Memoir* (2012); D'Agata and Fingal, *The Lifespan of a Fact* (2012). The This American Life "Retraction" episode (2012). Stokke on assertion in fiction. Probably some recent work on the ethics of stand-up exists (unsure).
(c) Live. The genre-contract view is the default, and stand-up is fresh-ish.

**Q8. Asking permission.**
(a) (1) No: asking binds you. By asking, you represent, or implicitly undertake, that their answer will govern, so the veto comes from your own self-binding act, something like a promise (40%). (2) Yes: you may go ahead, since no right was transferred. Going ahead may still be disrespectful or misleading, but it violates no claim (30%). (3) Asking confers authority on them: a normative-power account in which the asker creates the respondent's power (20%). (4) It depends on uptake and convention, as with courtesy asks (10%).
(b) [M] Owens, *Shaping the Normative Landscape* (2012), covering requests, promises and consent; Enoch, "Giving Practical Reasons" (2011); Cupit, "How Requests (and Promises) Create Obligations" (PQ, 1994); Darwall, *The Second-Person Standpoint* (2006); Hurd, "The Moral Magic of Consent" (1996); Raz on authority; Chang on normative powers. I cannot recall a standard paper on exactly this question, which makes it suspicious and possibly fresh.
(c) Fresh-ish, but the normative-powers frameworks are close enough to deliver answer (1) cheaply.

**Q9. Revoking gifts.**
(a) (1) The conditional-gift view: gifts presuppose an ongoing relationship or good faith, and a grave wrong defeats the presupposition, so revocation is permissible (35%). (2) A gift transfers full title and gratitude is unenforceable, so there is no residual power (30%). (3) Ingratitude wrongs the benefactor, and revocation is a fitting blaming response, though not a property right (20%). (4) Revocation is permissible only to the extent the benefit is still held, on an unjust-enrichment model (15%).
(b) [M] Seneca, *De Beneficiis* (esp. III, on why ingratitude is not actionable) is canonical. Also Hobbes's fourth law of nature; Kant, *MdS*, on gratitude and the gift contract; Hyland, *Gifts: A Study in Comparative Law* (2009), which treats ingratitude revocation in civil law; Eisenberg, "The World of Contract and the World of Gift" (1997); Berger, "Gratitude" (1975); Card (1988); McConnell, *Gratitude* (1993); Manela on gratitude (SEP; 2016); Mauss.
(c) Comparative law treats this heavily. Philosophically it is fresh-ish, but Seneca owns the frame.

### Ranking (KEEP and MAYBE only)

1. **T4 (nocebo silence).** The only thesis neither predicted nor delivered by canon that I know of. Main risk: the "choosing the equilibrium is itself paternalistic" objection reinstates meta-consent. Read Manson and O'Neill (2007) and the full Wells and Kaptchuk (2012) first.
2. **T7 (intended emotion fixes the duty).** It is not the default genre answer and it has generalist reach. Main risk: Carroll (1997) plus Bok's publicity test absorb it, and amusement turns out to be belief-sensitive, which collapses the innocent side.
3. **T1 (institution fixes the question).** The most distinctive evidence base. Main risk: it was predicted, and the statute's own words plus the legal-versus-communicative content split make it look obvious. The notice requirement may not discriminate between views.

T8 is a near miss: it has the highest appeal, but its core was my top prediction and Raz's arbitrator likely absorbs it.

**DROPs: T2, T3, T5, T6.**
