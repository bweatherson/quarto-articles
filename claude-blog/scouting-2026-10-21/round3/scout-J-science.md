## Candidate 1: Honour is in the naming, offence is in the use (eponyms in science)

**Question.** A species, disease or law is named after someone later judged a wrongdoer. Does keeping the name keep honouring him? More generally: do names honour, or only refer? And can an honour be withdrawn?

**Candidate thesis.** An eponym honours its bearer in the act of naming, not in later uses of the name. So keeping a tainted name honours no one, and renaming cannot withdraw an honour. Only features located in each use can justify changing a name already in use: an offensive word, or a harmful reminder.

**Core argument and real practice.**
- *Minimal pair 1 (one congress).* The 2024 International Botanical Congress in Madrid voted 351–205 to change every epithet built on the slur "caffra" to "affra", retroactively. A separate proposal to remove names with derogatory connotations retroactively was amended to apply only to names published in future, screened by a naming ethics committee from 2026. The expressive view ("names honour, so dishonourable names must go") cannot explain why offence was treated retroactively and honour prospectively. The act/use view predicts exactly this split:
  - a slur offends in every token (prohibitionism about slurs);
  - an honour is conferred by the dubbing, so a code can control only future acts of honouring.
- *Minimal pair 2 (one bird).* In 2023 the American Ornithological Society decided to drop English names that honour people, so "Townsend's Warbler" goes. Under the zoological code (ICZN, priority principle) the scientific name *Setophaga townsendi* stays. Nobody thinks a taxonomist who writes "townsendi" is saluting Townsend.
- *Structural point.* Honouring is an act addressed to someone's merit. A referring use is not addressed to anyone's merit. Contrast the compelled salute in *Barnette* (1943), whose whole point is expressive. Statues differ from names because displaying a statue *is* the commemorative use (Frowe), whereas using a name is referring.
- *Payoffs.*
  - It dissolves the "stability vs justice" stand-off in taxonomy.
  - It separates statues (where use is commemoration) from names (where use is reference).
  - It explains why misattributed eponyms (Stigler's law) are never corrected: a use of the name is not a claim about credit.
  - It implies that the 2011 renaming of Wegener's granulomatosis needed a reason located in use, such as descriptive names or what patients hear, not "ceasing to honour".

**Canonical treatments.**
1. Merton 1957, "Priorities in scientific discovery", *ASR* 22 [MEM]. Eponymy is the most enduring form of recognition. He treats it as standing credit and says nothing about withdrawal. The thesis relocates that honour to the event of naming.
2. Kripke 1980, *Naming and Necessity* [MEM]. The baptism/use structure means descriptive content (and, by extension, honorific content) does not enter later uses. This gives the reference half of the thesis, not the honour half. NB: it is not the abandoned standard-metre topic; only the baptism/use distinction is used.
3. Frowe 2019, "The duty to remove statues of wrongdoers", *J. Practical Ethics* 7 [MEM]. Keeping a statue is ongoing endorsement. Her argument extends naturally to names (as in the Yale Calhoun case). The thesis denies that extension for names in referential use. **Must read: does she discuss names?**
4. Burch-Brown 2017, "Is it wrong to topple statues and rename schools?", *J. Political Theory & Philosophy* 1 [MEM]. She treats names and statues together as commemoration. This is the main target.
5. Anderson & Lepore 2013, "Slurring words", *Noûs* 47 [MEM]. Slurs offend because tokening them is prohibited, even in non-endorsing uses. This gives the use-located offence half of the thesis.
6. Stigler 1980, "Stigler's law of eponymy" [MEM]; ICZN Art. 23 and Ceríaco et al. 2023, *Zool. J. Linn. Soc.* [MEM]. The taxonomists' line is that names are labels and stability comes first. They assert this; they do not locate where the honour lies.

**Recent closest work.**
- Guedes et al. 2023, "Eponyms have no place in 21st-century biological nomenclature", *Nature Ecol. Evol.* [title only].
- Madrid congress report, botany.one, July 2024 [READ, secondary report].
- AOS 2023 decision [MEM].

No philosophy paper on scientific eponyms turned up in one search; this is weak evidence of absence.

**Obvious answer.** "It is a trade-off between nomenclatural stability and harm. Taxonomists say names are labels; critics say they are honours. Weigh the costs." The thesis is not a weighting. It says that "withdrawing an honour" is never available as a reason for changing a name in use. It derives the retroactive/prospective split from that, and it sorts statues from names.

**Biggest risks.**
- The memory/salience reply: a name in daily use keeps the person's prestige alive, and "Townsend's" is literally possessive. The reply is that salience is located in use and is not honour, but this needs a careful account of honour.
- The commemoration literature may already separate the naming event from its maintenance (Frowe, Lai, Schulz: unread).
- Possible overlap with scout G (names) or scout H (statues).
- The skeptic's reply: Madrid split the issues for reasons of cost (about 200 caffra names against thousands of eponyms).

**Confidence.** Medium, if Frowe and Burch-Brown do not already locate honour in the act of naming.

## Candidate 2: The dead coauthor and the AI (what authorship credits)

**Question.** A coauthor who died before the paper was drafted is listed on the byline. An AI system that drafted much of the paper may not be listed. What does that show about what authorship is?

**Candidate thesis.** Authorship credits *attributable* agency, meaning the work reflects the contributor's own judgement. *Answerability* for a paper belongs to the byline as a whole and passes among the living. So COPE's ground for excluding AI, that it "cannot take responsibility for the submitted work", is false of authors in general, and the real ground is attributability.

**Core argument and real practice.**
- COPE's position statement (last reviewed Feb 2023) gives three reasons AI cannot be an author: it cannot take responsibility for the work, cannot declare conflicts of interest, and cannot manage copyright [READ]. A deceased coauthor can do none of these three either. Yet posthumous authorship is standard. A JME paper (2019) argues the dead can "in principle" meet the authorship guidelines [ABSTRACT]. A BMJ proposal deals with a guarantor who dies by appointing a *proxy* guarantor [PART].
- The "they agreed while alive" rescue fails for contributors who died before drafting began. They are routinely listed, yet they never approved these claims, and an undertaking to answer that can no longer be performed is not answerability. What the rescue really tracks is that their contribution was an exercise of their own judgement: attributability (Watson; Shoemaker).
- Answerability already works at the level of the byline:
  - guarantors (Rennie 1997; BMJ);
  - proxy guarantors;
  - collaboration-level internal review in hyperauthored physics.
- This explains the credit–blame asymmetry of Porsdam Mann et al. 2023: humans answer in full for AI output (answerability belongs to the byline) but earn less credit for it (attributability).
- It also predicts that, for agentic "AI scientists", liability or guarantor arrangements will not settle authorship; attributability will.

**Canonical treatments.**
1. ICMJE criteria (accountability criterion added 2013) [MEM]; COPE 2023 [READ]. The official view: every author must be accountable for the whole paper.
2. Rennie, Yank & Emanuel 1997, "When authorship fails", *JAMA* [MEM]. The contributorship-plus-guarantor model **already separates credit from accountability**. That is half the thesis. It does not address non-human or deceased contributors.
3. Biagioli 2003, "Rights or rewards?", in Biagioli & Galison (eds), *Scientific Authorship* [MEM]. Scientific authorship is reward plus responsibility, not property.
4. Watson 1996, "Two faces of responsibility"; Shoemaker 2011, "Attributability, answerability, and accountability", *Ethics* [MEM]. The framework the thesis uses. Risk: "known framework plus an application".
5. Wray 2006, *SHPS*; Huebner, Kukla & Winsberg 2017 [MEM]. In radically collaborative research no one is accountable for the whole, and Kukla doubts there is an author at all. This supports answerability at the level of the byline, but they draw a different conclusion.

**Recent closest work.**
- *JME Practical Bioethics* 2026 symposium:
  - Earp, Porsdam Mann, Sawai & Wangmo, editorial, "Death, authorship and generative AI" [UNREAD; it may use the posthumous case];
  - Ray [PART];
  - Lavazza (authorship as "voluntary assumption of a public normative status") [PART];
  - Abernethy (functionalist) [PART].

  None of the three commentaries mentions deceased authors.
- Porsdam Mann, Earp et al. 2023, "Generative AI entails a credit–blame asymmetry", *Nature Machine Intelligence* [MEM]. Must check whether it already uses attributability and answerability.

**Obvious answer.** Either "AI cannot be held accountable" (COPE; Ray) or "AI is not an agent". The thesis rejects the first. It overlaps with the second: what is new is the posthumous argument and answerability located in the byline.

**Biggest risks.**
- The past-participation view (JME 2019; Lavazza read historically) absorbs the posthumous case, and the thesis reduces to "AI isn't an agent".
- Rennie 1997 anticipates the separation of credit from accountability.
- A crowded 2026 literature; likely overlap with scout I.

**Confidence.** Low. It would be medium if the Earp et al. editorial and Porsdam Mann et al. 2023 do not already make these moves.

## Ranking
1. **Eponyms.** Real codes, a vivid one-congress minimal pair, generalist reach (names, honour, slurs, statues), and no philosophy precedent found.
2. **Authorship and AI.** A live debate and a vivid pair, but it is half anticipated (Rennie) and at risk of collapsing into "AI isn't an agent".

## Ideas killed while scouting
- **Coauthors can pull a paper down but not leave it.** Absorbable as "the byline records facts plus an evidence-driven retraction"; Andersen & Wray, *Episteme* 2023, show that team retractions are mostly unanimous.
- **Image processing: an image stays data while its processing models the instrument, not the specimen.** Delivered by Kosso-style independence; adjacent to Magnus, *AI & Society* 2025.
- **Many-analyst studies and permissivism.** Menkveld et al.'s "nonstandard errors"; Auspurg & Brüderl on estimands.
- **Uniform significance thresholds.** Wilholt 2013.
- **Preregistration and predictivism.** Lipton; Mayo.
