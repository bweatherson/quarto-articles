# Predictability pretest X

## Phase 1: blind predictions

All author/literature attributions are **from memory** and unverified.

**Q1. Proof and probability (Mersenne case).**
(a) Theses:
1. Normic support (Smith): an LL "prime" verdict could be false only through malfunction, which needs special explanation. A PRP verdict can be false with nothing going wrong. Proof is what normically supports. 35%.
2. Performance vs competence (safety/virtue-theoretic): LL failures are performance errors in a method that is infallible when executed correctly, and double-checking removes them. PRP risk is built into the method. Knowledge and proof need competence-level infallibility, not low error rates. 25%.
3. Transferability/checkability (Easwaran-style): proof is a certificate that others can re-run and verify. The double-checking regime shows this, and probabilistic tests lack it. 20%.
4. Debunking/conventionalist: there is no deep epistemic difference; "final" is an institutional norm of the mathematical community about what counts as a theorem. Lottery and legal intuitions are not explained by it. 15%.
(b) Memory: Cohen 1977 (Gatecrasher); Thomson 1986; Nelkin 2000; Hawthorne 2004; Vogel 1990; Enoch, Spectre & Fisher 2012; Smith 2016 *Between Probability and Certainty* and 2018 (on conviction); Moss 2018 *Probabilistic Knowledge*; Buchak 2014; Blome-Tillmann 2015; Gardiner; Bolinger; Ross's survey; Fallis 1997/2002 and Easwaran 2009 on probabilistic proof; Paseau on mathematical knowledge without proof.
(c) The question is very well-trodden. The GIMPS case is fresh and is the only thing new here.

**Q2. Censored consensus.**
(a) Theses:
1. Higher-order/evidential: consensus is evidence only if dissent was free to surface. Suppression removes the public's grounds for treating agreement as the outcome of scrutiny, even when what is suppressed is junk. 35%.
2. Millian "dead dogma": without the collision with error, people lose understanding of the reasons and hold true beliefs as prejudice. 25%.
3. Trust/legitimacy: suppression signals distrust of the public and erodes warranted trust in experts. The damage is relational or political, not evidential. 20%.
4. Contrarian: it does not damage, or the damage is outweighed (epistemic paternalism, environment engineering). 15%.
(b) Memory: Mill; Goldman 1991 on epistemic paternalism; Ahlstrom-Vij 2013; Levy *Bad Beliefs* 2021; Nguyen on echo chambers; Fantl 2018 *Limitations of the Open Mind*; Anderson 2011; Miller 2013 "When is consensus knowledge based?"; Dellsén on expert autonomy and consensus; Kitcher and Zollman on diversity; Oreskes.
(c) The question is live and crowded. Thesis 1 is the obvious modern answer.

**Q3. Self-binding (GAMSTOP).**
(a) Theses:
1. The authority is not the earlier self's but third parties'. The registrant waives claims against operators or confers powers on them. You cannot bind yourself, but you can bind the world around you. 30%.
2. Diachronic or precedent autonomy (Bratman's planning agency, Dworkin's precedent autonomy): the reflective earlier self speaks for the temporally extended agent. 30%.
3. Paternalism and compromised agency: the "competent, insistent" later self is in an addiction-distorted state, so overriding it is soft paternalism. 20%.
4. Sceptical: a competent later self's wish should prevail, so only time-limited friction is justified. 15%.
(b) Memory: Elster 1979/2000; Schelling; Dworkin *Life's Dominion*; Dresser; Jaworska; Davis 2004 on precedent autonomy; Holton 2009; Bratman; Habib and Rosati on self-promises; Owens 2012 on normative powers; Ulysses contracts in psychiatry (Spellecy; Gremmen); Feinberg.
(c) The question is well-trodden and the case is fresh. The predictable move is "it's a waiver/normative power, not a self-promise".

**Q4. Quashed convictions.**
(a) Theses:
1. The presumption of innocence, extended past the verdict, forbids requiring proof of innocence. The state has no claim from possible guilt. 35%.
2. Compensation is owed for unjustified deprivation as such, a procedural or institutional wrong, regardless of factual guilt. 25%.
3. Thomson-style infringement: the state acted permissibly on the evidence then, but it still owes compensation, as in the cabin case. Factual guilt matters only to whether a right was infringed. 20%.
4. Probability-weighted or partial: possible guilt discounts the claim, or the UK rule is defensible (no injustice suffered by the guilty, scarce resources). 15%.
(b) Memory: Duff; Ashworth; Stumer 2010; Lippke 2016; Laudan 2006; Walen; Hoskins; Thomson 1986 on compensation; Allen v UK (ECtHR 2013); legal literature on compensation schemes (Bernhard; Hamer).
(c) The question is fresh in philosophy and well-covered in law.

**Q5. Stipulated standards (kilogram).**
(a) Theses:
1. Deflationary: stipulation never yielded knowledge of a contingent fact about the world, only metalinguistic or superficially contingent knowledge (Donnellan, Evans). The 2019 redefinition makes this vivid. 30%.
2. Metrological: even a definitional standard was only ever accessed under uncertainty (Tal's model-based account), so the a priori story idealises practice. The new SI shows that a definition does not fix empirical content. 25%.
3. Kripke vindicated or relativised: before 2019 "IPK = 1 kg" was contingent a priori, and after 2019 it is not. Contingent a priori status is relative to the definitional regime. 20%.
4. The case separates reference-fixing from knowledge: you can fix reference by a definition without thereby knowing the fact. 15%.
(b) Memory: Kripke; Wittgenstein PI §50; Donnellan 1977; Evans 1979; Salmon; Hawthorne 2002 "Deeply contingent a priori knowledge"; Chalmers; Tal (several papers on measurement and the SI); Chang; van Fraassen on coordination; Riordan 2015 on the kilogram; de Courtenay; Mari. I vaguely recall at least one paper explicitly on the new SI and the contingent a priori (uncertain).
(c) The question is well-trodden, and the case is half-fresh because philosophers of measurement have used it.

**Q6. Schisms.**
(a) Theses:
1. Fission/closest-continuer: there is no deep fact, or it is settled by best-candidate continuity of structure and membership, so the smaller faction usually loses. 30%.
2. Structural/constitutional: the group is whichever faction retains the authority structure or constitutive rules, so a minority can be the original (the Free Church "Wee Frees" case, 1904). 30%.
3. Doctrinal fidelity: identity tracks fidelity to defining commitments, which again favours a possible minority. 20%.
4. Conventionalist: recognition by outsiders, courts or the state settles it. 15%.
(b) Memory: Ritchie 2013/2020; Epstein 2015; Uzquiano 2004; Effingham 2010; Hawley 2017; Sheehy; Thomasson; Nozick's closest continuer; Parfit on fission; *Free Church of Scotland v Overtoun* (1904); *Jones v Wolf* (1979).
(c) The question is fairly fresh as a named question, and social ontology is live.

**Q7. Temptation.**
(a) Theses:
1. No. Temptation needs a desire with the power to shift or corrupt one's evaluative judgement or resolution, not merely a gap between strength and judged worth (Holton). 30%.
2. Attentional/evaluative appearance: temptation is a recalcitrant seeming of goodness, or the guise of the good. You can be tempted by what you judge best (Huck Finn) and untempted by stronger desires. 25%.
3. Menu-preference (Gul–Pesendorfer): x tempts you iff you would prefer a menu without x. 20%.
4. Affective/phenomenal: temptation is a felt pull, like an emotion. 15%.
(b) Memory: Holton 2009; Mele 1987; Watson 1977; Ainslie; Elster; Andreou; Tappolet; Kennett; Sripada; Gul & Pesendorfer 2001; Schapiro on "feeling like it"; Tenenbaum on the guise of the good.
(c) The question is live. It is surprisingly under-addressed head-on, though it is surrounded by the weakness-of-will literature.

**Q8. Over-persistence.**
(a) Theses:
1. Not weakness but excess of will: the dispositions that protect resolutions from reconsideration produce over-persistence. It is a good disposition misfiring (Holton, Bratman). 35%.
2. Epistemic failure: plan-continuation bias and motivated updating. It is a cognitive, not a volitional, failure. 25%.
3. Sunk-cost rationalised: persisting can be rational for the sake of the past or of narrative value (Kelly). 15%.
4. It is weakness of will after all: failure to follow a precommitted turnaround rule, an unreasonable revision of a resolution (Holton's own category). 15%.
(b) Memory: Holton 2009; Bratman 1987; Kelly 2004 on sunk costs; Morton & Paul 2019 "Grit"; Paul on persistence; Tenenbaum & Raffman 2012; Staw on escalation of commitment; aviation human-factors literature on "get-there-itis".
(c) The question is live and partly trodden through the grit and resolution literatures.

**Q9. Diminishing grief.**
(a) Theses:
1. Process view: grief is a process assessed as a whole, and diminishing is part of grieving well (Na'aman; Cholbi). 35%.
2. Reasons persist but are outweighed or rationally escaped; diminution is permissible but not fitting-explained (Marušić's puzzle). 25%.
3. The object shifts: grief's fitting object is the relationship's transformation, so as that is reconfigured, less grief fits (Cholbi). 20%.
4. Grief is fitting forever, and fading is a lapse we cannot help (Callard; Moller's worry about resilience). 15%.
(b) Memory: Marušić 2018; Na'aman 2021; Moller 2007; Cholbi 2021 book and papers; Preston-Roedder & Preston-Roedder 2017; Callard 2020; Solomon; Ratcliffe; Nussbaum.
(c) The question is well-trodden, with a large literature since 2018.

**Q10. Judging character.**
(a) Theses:
1. Doxastic wronging is real, and moral encroachment raises the bar for uncharitable beliefs. Give the benefit of the doubt (Basu, Schroeder, Bolinger). 35%.
2. Sceptic: beliefs do not wrong; the wrongs are in inquiry, attention, expression or treatment (Begby; Enoch & Spectre; Gardiner; Osborne). 25%.
3. Declining to believe can wrong too: withholding belief or trust can be a failure of recognition (Fricker; "believe victims"). 20%.
4. Relational partiality: friends owe charitable belief and strangers are owed neutrality (Stroud; Keller). 15%.
(b) Memory: Basu 2019; Basu & Schroeder 2019; Schroeder 2018; Marušić & White 2018; Moss 2018; Bolinger 2020; Gardiner 2018; Fritz; Begby 2021; Stroud 2006; Keller 2004; Preston-Roedder 2013; Fricker 2007.
(c) The question is very well-trodden.

**Q11. Risk for others.**
(a) Theses:
1. Buchak: when you do not know their attitude, defer to the most risk-averse reasonable attitude. Play safe. 35%.
2. Repetition changes things (Thoma's long-run argument): in repeated choices risk aversion largely washes out, so guardians and trustees should be closer to risk-neutral. 30%.
3. Aggregate or elicit: aggregate the plausible attitudes (Pettigrew), or use the person's own revealed attitude. 20%.
4. Anti-Buchak: playing safe is not neutral. It imposes an attitude, and risk neutrality is the proper default. 10%.
(b) Memory: Buchak 2013, 2017 ("Taking risks behind the veil of ignorance"); Thoma 2019 "Risk aversion and the long run"; Thoma on the moral proxy problem; Pettigrew 2019 *Choosing for Changing Selves*; Stefánsson & Bradley; Rowe & Voorhoeve; Hansson on risk imposition; Frick on ex ante contractualism.
(c) The question is live and recently crowded. "Repeated vs one-off" is exactly Thoma's territory.

### Top four (KEEP or MAYBE)

1. **T9b (grief on two clocks).** It is the only thesis I did not predict at all. Risk: Bradley or Atkins already drew the inference, or the eternalist "consolation, not fittingness" reply holds; it also explains only one component of grief, so it may read as changing the subject.
2. **T7 (temptation without desire).** Risk: the double dissociation is dismissed as verbal (alief, urge, "never decided"), and Bratman's "Temptation Revisited" may already individuate temptation by admissibility.
3. **T3 (self-exclusion as self-testimony).** Risk: the headline is the predictable answer, so the paper rests on its three predictions, and the epidural prediction smuggles in the paternalist evaluation the thesis disowns.
4. **T5 (demoted standards).** Risk: Tal and the 2019 SI volume already make the definition/realisation move, leaving only the anti-Donnellan rider, which the Kripkean "re-fixed by 10⁻⁸" reply targets directly.

T4 is fifth: the credit-on-reconviction lever is good, but the vigilante analogy is weak.
