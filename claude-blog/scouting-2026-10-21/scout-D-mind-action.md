## Candidate 1. Tempted without wanting

**Question.** What makes something a temptation? Is it just wanting something more than you think you should?

**Thesis.** To be tempted is for an option you have excluded to become live again against your standing decision; desire is one way of making it live, and is neither necessary nor sufficient.

**Core argument.**
- *Pull without desire (the balcony).* On a high balcony you think "I could jump", grip the rail, step back, feel relief. You do not want to die. Hames et al. (2012; 431 students) find this "high place phenomenon" common, present in people with no suicidal ideation and strongest in those with the least. They read it as a misread survival signal. That explains its cause, not its constitution. Whatever produces it, the agent undergoes a standing exclusion pressed on by a live "I could"; she resists by doing things, and asking "should I?" makes it worse, as with dessert. Augustine's pear theft is the moral version.
- *Desire without pull (the spouse, the vegetarian).* A faithful spouse is "attracted but not tempted". A committed vegetarian finds bacon smells good and says "it's not food to me". Hold stimulus and appeal fixed: the dieter is tempted and the vegetarian is not. What differs is whether the act is among the things she might do.
- So temptation is individuated by the option's status in deliberation (Bratman's filter of admissibility, Raz's exclusionary reasons, McDowell's silencing): it is the pressure of an excluded option to be re-admitted. Desire is one source of pressure; proximity, permission ("go on, one won't hurt") and bare salience are others.
- Payoffs. (i) It explains Holton's point that reconsidering is dangerous (to ask "should I?" is to make it live) and Schwenkler's slack (the boundary of what is excluded is vague). (ii) It predicts that liveness-removers (looking away, stepping back, "I don't" rather than "I can't") work on balcony-type pulls where desire-reduction does not. The "I don't/I can't" finding is Patrick & Hagtvedt 2012, psychology, background only. (iii) It recasts the continent/temperate contrast: the temperate person may feel appeal but is not tempted, so temperance is not absence of desire.

**Obvious answer.** "A strong desire for what you have resolved or judged against" (Mele, Holton, Bratman; Schwenkler's own definition requires a desire). The thesis denies the criterion, and the double dissociation is the argument.

**Closest prior work.**
- Schwenkler 2024, "How Temptation Works", JESP 27(3) [READ]. Temptation can violate a decision without revising it (slack). He keeps the desire condition and ends with practical wisdom and self-knowledge.
- Bermúdez, Berthelette, Fernández-Miranda, Anaya & Rodríguez 2024, "Temptation and Apathy", OSAR 8 [ABSTRACT]. Temptation is "a strong pull toward something undesirable", and is not central to self-control.
- McDowell 1979 [PART, from memory] and Sinhababu 2016, "Virtue, Desire, and Silencing Reasons" [ABSTRACT]. Sinhababu gives a desire-based account of silencing, the direct opponent. A 2024 J. Ethics paper, "Moral Transformation as Shifting (Im)Possibilities" [PART, excerpt], uses ruled-out options in virtue ethics but not for temptation.
- Schapiro 2021, *Feeling Like It* [PART, review only]. Inclination as a state distinct from judgment; the reviewer says she does not cleanly separate it from desire.
- Priority: I found no analytic paper individuating temptation by option-status.

**Generalist appeal.** High. Everyone knows the "call of the void" and "attracted but not tempted".

**Biggest risk.** The dissociation is called verbal: the balcony pull is an intrusive thought or fear, and the vegetarian lacks a desire to eat. It needs a non-question-begging mark of temptation (a pull against a standing decision, resisted by action, intensified by deliberation) and a hard case of desire-to-φ without pull. If the self-control predictions do not land, it is mere conceptual analysis.

**Confidence: medium.**

## Candidate 2. Over-persistence: the exit is not resisted, it is unseen

**Question.** Why do capable people go on past the point where they should stop (the climber past the turnaround time, the pilot pressing the approach)? Is that a failure of will?

**Thesis.** Over-persistence is characteristically a deliberative failure, not a motivational one: a plan filters what registers as bearing on it, so the exit never becomes a candidate. It is cured by triggers that do not depend on the agent noticing (scheduled review, outside voices, hard stops), not by willpower, and "reasonable habits of non-reconsideration" misdescribe the problem.

**Core argument.**
- Paul 2025 calls over-efforting (more investment after strong evidence of failure, at high price) weakness of will, "voluntarily impeding oneself" in pursuing one's own ends. She flags a tension: inertia-based accounts of resolve (Holton, Marušić, Buchak) block sensitivity to evidence for quitting.
- *Pair: Reader and Non-reader.* Two pilots get the same cloud-base report. R reads it, thinks "it'll clear", presses on (motivated misassessment). N, absorbed in the approach, never takes the report as bearing on whether to continue: "it didn't occur to me". Holton, Bratman and Paul group them as failures to reconsider, and Bratman counts N's non-reconsideration as rational if her habit is reasonable. But N did not refrain from reconsidering. Nothing triggered it, and the plan is why.
- Test of the distinction: the remedies that work are triggers (turnaround times, stabilized-approach criteria, "kill criteria"), not stronger resolve. And over-persistence should rise with absorption and investment, not with felt temptation.
- Consequence: a reasonable habit of reconsideration is a policy for responding to triggers; if the plan shapes the triggers, the policy cannot be assessed as the planning theory assumes. Positive norm: rational plans need reconsideration that is scheduled, not only triggered.
- It does not claim sunk-cost reasoning is rational, only that sunk costs do not explain N.

**Obvious answer.** Sunk cost, optimism, ego: they cannot bring themselves to quit (Paul: weakness of will). The thesis moves the locus from motivation to salience: the thought of quitting is not resisted, it does not arise.

**Closest prior work.**
- Paul 2025, "Over-efforting as weakness of will", Synthese 206:291 [READ]. Names the inertia tension and diagnoses distortion and self-deception.
- Schwenkler 2024 [READ]. Violation without revision; tempted people think themselves reasonable; the remedy is practical wisdom.
- Holton 2009 and "How Is Strength of Will Possible?" [PART]. Strength is reasonable refusal to reconsider; stubbornness is "unreasonable inflexibility".
- Lissia, "Pessimism, stubbornness and weakness of will", Paradigmi forthcoming [ABSTRACT]. Both traits are responses to pessimism.
- Paul 2022, "Plan B" [ABSTRACT]; Morton & Paul 2019, "Grit"; Paul & Morton, *Striving* (OUP, expected 2027) per Paul's CV [CV only, not read]. This is the main priority risk.

**Generalist appeal.** High ("why don't smart people stop?").

**Biggest risk.** The Paul/Morton book may pre-empt it. A reviewer may say failure to notice is negligence, not irrationality, and so outside action theory. The attention claim must be argued conceptually or the paper becomes a psychology import.

**Confidence: low to medium.**

## Candidate 3. Settled is not stuck

**Question.** When a law demands a "settled" or "enduring" wish, or we infer commitment from someone having stayed ten years, what does persistence show?

**Thesis.** Persistence of an attitude is no evidence that it is settled, because unreconsidered intentions persist by default (Bratman's stability); only persistence through reconsideration is evidence, so a waiting period with no occasion to reconsider tests transience, not settledness.

**Core argument.**
- Two sources of false positives. Transience: hot states pass, and delay filters them out, which is real. Inertia: unchallenged intentions persist because nothing reopens them.
- *Pair: Ana and Bea.* Both have stayed in their jobs ten years and say "I intend to stay". Ana has fielded offers, asked herself, and said yes. Bea is held by mortgage, lease and habit and never decided. A removal test (clear the mortgage) separates them; duration cannot. Ana is settled but free; Bea is stuck but not settled.
- Positive criterion: an attitude is settled to the extent it has been, or would be, held through reconsideration under live challenge. Institutional upshot: repeat-request designs (a second request after an interval) are crude reconsideration triggers; mere delay is not.
- This is the philosophical counterpart of "sliding versus deciding" in relationship science.

**Obvious answer.** "Longer persistence is good, if not conclusive, evidence of a real wish." The thesis says persistence is the null result.

**Closest prior work.**
- Bratman 1987, 1999, 2014 [PART, via Hinchman and Ferrero].
- Hinchman 2016, "What on Earth Was I Thinking?" [READ]. Quotes the no-regret condition; proposes projected trust-regret and unambivalence.
- Ferrero, "Diachronic constraints of practical rationality" (Phil Issues 2012; author per PhilArchive key) [ABSTRACT]. No genuinely diachronic constraint on stability.
- Stanley, Rhoades & Markman 2006 [TITLE ONLY]; Grüne-Yanoff 2026 on timing of intention-fixing [ABSTRACT].

**Generalist appeal.** Medium to high (law, medicine, relationships).

**Biggest risk.** It reads as obvious ("duration isn't proof") or as applied ethics. Bioethics literature on stable requests may pre-empt it; I could not search that well. "Survival of challenge" invites a reactance objection, and dispositional views may deny the inertia premise.

**Confidence: low.**

## Ranking
1. **Candidate 1.** It has a double-dissociation pair, a named opponent (the desire condition, including Schwenkler's definition), a thesis a fresh reader would not give, and wide appeal.
2. **Candidate 2.** It has a live target and a vivid pair, but sits in Paul and Morton's territory.
3. **Candidate 3.** It is the least distinctive. If only two are worth pursuing, they are 1 and 2.
