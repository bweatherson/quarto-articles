# Screening report (v1b)

**Paper:** The Record and the Account: What a Track Record Can Show About Knowing the Chances

**Word count:** c. 5,200 words of body text (plus a 290-word abstract); 44 bibliography entries.

**Thesis summary:** The expert-testing impossibility theorems in economics (Sandroni, Olszewski–Sandroni, Shmaya) show that any test of forecasts that is guaranteed to pass whoever knows the true chances can be passed by someone who knows nothing. The paper argues that the lesson is relativity: a record certifies knowledge of chance only relative to an "account", a tester-fixed class of possible chance hypotheses, and certifies only insofar as that class's members are mutually singular, so that accounts closed under mixture certify no one. This holds for sincere as well as strategic forecasters, because the record also teaches the chances, so what it measures is a head start over a baseline learner; and the metaphysics of chance supplies no certifying account on propensity views and supplies one on best-system views only if the standards of simplicity exclude mixtures.

**Verdict:** NOT READY

## Deal-breakers

**1. Priority (Criterion 1 and the antecedents requirement).** The paper states its aim as saying "what the relativity is relative to." That has been said, in the literature the paper imports, under the name *paradigm*. Olszewski and Sandroni's "Strategic Manipulation of Empirical Tests" (MOR 2009), which the paper cites for the convex-and-compact result, introduces the paradigm as "the class of permissible theories ... restricted ... from the outset," says that "our impossibility result does not necessarily hold when the paradigm is not convex," and in §4.1 gives a two-theory non-convex paradigm with a non-manipulable bounded-data test, which is the paper's §3.1 i.i.d. example in all but detail; it also notes that exchangeable processes form a convex compact paradigm, which is the paper's Pólya-urn point. Two further papers, neither cited, go beyond this. Al-Najjar, Sandroni, Smorodinsky and Weinstein, "Testing theories with learnable and predictive representations" (JET 2010), show that restricting to theories with a learnable parametric representation makes a non-manipulable test possible and that none is possible without the restriction: the paper's §3.2 link between learnability and certification. Pomatto, "Testable forecasts" (Theoretical Economics 2021), defines a paradigm as "a summary of preexisting knowledge about the problem," gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a paradigm to be testable, and shows that optimal tests are likelihood-ratio tests against a hypothetical Bayesian outside observer: the paper's "head start over a learner, and which learner sets the standard is part of the account." A paper that cites one of these and appears unaware of what it says, and omits the other two, has not located itself relative to its nearest predecessors. What is left as the paper's own is the philosophical framing and §4.

**2. The "single formal property" is the wrong property for the thesis, and the paper's own distinctions show it.** The paper says the work of an account "is captured by a single property," pairwise mutual singularity, citing Kavaler and Smorodinsky. Their Theorem 5 is a characterisation of *ideal comparative infinite-horizon* tests (the test must identify the knower with probability one, between two forecasters, in the limit). The paper applies it to single-forecaster certification on records, which is a different problem with a different characterisation: Olszewski–Sandroni need convexity *and compactness* with bounded data, and Pomatto's Theorem 1 is a total-variation condition that is neither. Pomatto's footnote 4 states that the learnable paradigm of Al-Najjar et al. 2010 "is convex, but testable," which falsifies the thesis's final sentence ("Accounts on which mixtures of chances are chances certify no one") as written. The paper's own §3.2 shows the same thing from inside: a diffuse mixture such as Laplace's rule is singular with respect to each component, so a class containing it and the fixed-bias coins is pairwise singular. The sentence "On finite records singularity becomes a matter of rates ... but the shape of the result is the same" is the only bridge offered, and it is not an argument.

**3. The metaphysical payoff advertised in the abstract does not go through (the "could the author have seen it" test).** §4 claims a "new epistemic consideration": knowledge of best-system chance is testable where knowledge of primitive chance is not, provided the standards of simplicity exclude "the relevant mixtures." The two examples given to show that actual standards may not exclude them do not bear on the criterion the paper has set. Loewer's statistical-mechanical chance is a mixture over deterministic histories, but the point-mass histories are not candidate systems (they are maximally complex), so the example shows that a mixture can be simple, not that the class of candidate systems fails pairwise singularity. The MDL example is the Laplace mixture, which by §3.2 is singular with respect to every fixed-bias coin; on the paper's own criterion that class *does* certify, slowly. So the paper has shown neither that any version of the best-system view fails to supply a certifying account nor that one does, and the "dilemma" rests on an antecedent the paper leaves unexamined. The author drew the finite-versus-diffuse distinction in §3.2 and did not apply it in §4.

## Key strengths

- The question is a generalist one (Meno, the novice/expert problem, what validation by record can mean for institutions and benchmarks), and the body of results imported is one philosophers of expertise have not used; as far as I could check, the "ignored by philosophers" claim holds (Herrmann 2022 and Belot 2013, the nearest philosophical neighbours, do not cite it).
- The Stewart example in §3.3 is the paper's best moment: two testers forecasting one-half every day, one certain of a fair coin (ε = 1, the test fails every expert) and one uniformly uncertain over deterministic sequences (ε = 0, a pass certifies a prophet). It makes vivid that what a pass shows depends on how the tester's uncertainty is carved, not on her predictive probabilities. It checks out against Stewart's definition of ε and his Proposition 1.
- The Charmides reading of the comparative results (a known knower can certify agreement and discredit by beating but cannot crown anyone as knowing more than he does) is an apt and accurate gloss of Al-Najjar–Weinstein Theorems 1 and 7.
- The economics is reported accurately throughout; I verified the Al-Najjar–Weinstein quotation and Theorem 7, Kavaler–Smorodinsky Thm 5/Prop 1, Olszewski–Sandroni MOR Prop 3, Belot, and the Levy quotation and his access diagnosis.

## Key weaknesses

- The reply to "isn't this just the old point that evidence is relative to alternatives?" is that chance is "special" because the record that tests it also teaches it. Nothing is said about why that is special to chance: a doctor's record of outcomes is also the textbook from which her diagnoses could be learned. Without that argument the paper has not separated its thesis from the statistician's commonplace that a test is relative to a model (Box 1980, which it cites), except by the results it borrows.
- §4 is c. 600 words doing the work of a paper: three metaphysical views, a New Principle analogue of type-I control stated in one sentence and never worked out, an unargued assertion that a state-recording mechanism "can realise any process whatever," and the computability route. The mismatch between the economists' "knows the process P" and the philosophers' time-indexed, history-relative chance, which on the best-system view is not fixed until the history is complete, is not addressed.
- Engagement with the literature on track records is thin for a paper framed by the novice/expert problem: Tetlock (2005; Tetlock and Gardner 2015) is absent although Brier-score certification of forecasters is the obvious real-world case, and Goldman's critics other than Levy are not discussed; "Brier scores work in practice" gets two sentences.
- The Objections section concedes the one that matters, that type-I control for every process is an idealisation no tester adopts, which leaves the thesis with little content beyond "a tester who relaxes it has a model."

The paper should be rebuilt around a precise statement of what it adds to Olszewski–Sandroni 2009 §4.1, Al-Najjar et al. 2010 and Pomatto 2021, with the formal criterion matched to the certification problem it is actually about, before it is screened again.
