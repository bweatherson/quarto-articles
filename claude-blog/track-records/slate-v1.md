# Candidate theses (slate v1) -- track-record paper

## S2. Trust Extended, Not Grounded
A forecaster's track record is the standard "objective" evidence of expertise. Results in economics show that no
test of a single forecaster's record, however much data it uses, can distinguish someone who knows the chances
from a strategic ignoramus, unless the tester brings something to the test. I argue that what the tester brings is
always a prior trust: in her own forecasts (the record then certifies only that the forecaster adds to what she
would have believed anyway), in a rival's honesty and independence (the record then certifies only that one
forecaster is better than another), or in a restriction on how the world could be. Track records therefore extend
trust; they cannot ground it. Two consequences: whether a whole field knows anything cannot be settled by its
members' records, even comparatively; and comparative rankings among forecasters who see one another's forecasts
reward recalibrating the crowd rather than knowing the domain. The novice/expert problem has no performance-based
solution, and Levy's pessimism has a theorem behind it.

## S3. Expertise Is Not Behavioural
Is expertise a matter of how one performs? In domains of chance, I argue, it is not. Theorems in economics show
that for any test of forecasts that a forecaster who knows the chances would pass, someone who knows nothing can,
by randomizing, pass it on every possible course of events. This is Block's Blockhead argument transposed to
forecasting, with one difference: for the tests used in practice the Blockhead is cheap. What separates the expert
from the charlatan is not any pattern in the forecasts actually made but the forecaster's commitments about
contingencies that never arise -- her model. Expertise in such domains is therefore a psychologistic property.
The routes by which testing can be partly rescued correspond to ways of reaching past behaviour: inspecting the
model (which helps only in the limit of unbounded data), comparing with another forecaster, importing the
evaluator's own prior, or bounding the charlatan's computation. Consequences for reliabilist accounts of expertise
and for evaluating AI forecasters, whose models can be queried as a person's cannot.

## S4. The Social Cost of Modesty
Bayesian orgulity -- being certain that one's inquiry will succeed on all but a negligible set of data -- has been
criticised as immodest (Belot) and defended or repaired (Elga, Huttegger, Weatherson). I argue that orgulity has a
social function its critics have overlooked: it is what makes an expert's claims testable. Results in economics
show that a forecaster who knows nothing can pass any test that a forecaster who knows the chances would pass, and
that this is avoided only when experts are required to be confident that well-supported claims are true -- the
confidence the critics of orgulity reject. A modest expert cannot be distinguished from a charlatan. So the debate
about orgulity is not only about an individual's attitude to her own inquiry; it is about whether others can hold
her to account. The right response is to separate first-person modesty from third-person commitment: experts may
be modest in credence but must be immodest in what they assert.
