### (b) My best response: two mechanisms, not one fact

The ✓ column runs together two phenomena, and neither needs FEM.

**(1) Graded items: likely, think, doubt, comparatives.** These are story-relative probability claims. "It's .5 likely that Bud's coin landed heads" is an epistemic-modal sentence, not a credence ascription. Epistemic modals take their information base from context, and a story can supply it, as in modal subordination. The measure belongs to the story: in-story chance where there is any (JAG, Flippy), and typicality or realism where there is none (Reilly, Bennet). B&H's own explanation in §8.2 needs exactly this measure. Their "rational indifference" makes credence about *which world is JAG's world* mirror the in-story measure, and they concede they have no argument for this. So FEM does no work: the measure explains the numbers. A standard theorist who relativises "likely", and Goodman–Holguín's comparative "think", to P-Fic gets the same predictions, including the fair/biased contrast in (12)–(14).

**(2) Inquisitive and bouletic items: wonder, curious, hope.** These are participatory. They report what readers do inside imaginative engagement (Walton's participation; Doggett & Egan's i-desire). Their felicity tracks immersion, not truth-aptness.

**(3) The ✗ column.** It follows on the standard view if "did it land heads?" is composed inside the story, with answers {□h, □¬h} (pointwise, as in inquisitive semantics). Then no answer is true, so "know whether", "sure" and counterfactual "wish" (which needs a settled ¬p) all fail.

**Discriminating predictions.** B&H posit one mechanism, rational uncertainty about a determinate fact. So they predict the pattern is invariant across contexts that hold the content fixed. The two-mechanism view predicts that the pattern comes apart:

- **(a) Thought experiments and pure suppositions** ("Suppose a fair coin is tossed unseen...") should license (1) but not (2). "Given the set-up, it's .5 likely heads" is fine. But "I wonder whether it landed heads" or "I hope it landed heads" invites the reply "it's your example; stipulate". B&H's PRP makes these suppositions counterfactual, so CEM gives them facts and B&H predict no difference. My ear hears one.
- **(b) Detached scholarly framing** ("as far as what Doyle's text establishes...") should suppress (2) but not (1).
- **(c) Oracle cases.** In §10 B&H allow that an oracle could give knowledge of the JAG outcome. "An oracle told me, so now I know the JAG coin landed tails" sounds defective, as the two-mechanism view predicts.
- **(d) Answering.** The natural answer to "Did the coin land heads?" is "The show never says", which is the standard view's answer. B&H's probes in (22) and (46)–(47) leave it out.

If these dissociations hold, the table shows that we hold attitudes *relative to a story's information state*. It does not show that a story has a world.

### (c) Weakest data points and steps

1. **The "Credence" row rests on (15)–(16), which use "It's .5 likely", an epistemic modal, not "I'm 50% confident".** (15a) also has a reading that reports a principal truth: the in-story chance is .5. B&H never test explicit credence ascriptions, or third-person, past-tense ascriptions.
2. **(17b) Deckard is not a case of known indeterminacy.** Whether Deckard is a replicant is disputed: Scott and Ford disagree, and the cuts differ. So "wonder" there tracks uncertainty about a principal truth.
3. **(21b) Sam Gamgee is not indeterminate either.** Appendix B of LOTR has Sam elected Mayor seven times, with a large family, before he sails West. "Lived in peace and prosperity" is close to a principal truth, so the hope is ignorance of the text.
4. **JAG itself is contaminated.** B&H's own footnote 27 reports a later NCIS: LA reveal. Speakers' "wonder" and "hope" may target future canon, and B&H must counterfactually stipulate the writers' intentions away.
5. **(19a) ✗ versus (20) ✓ treat "wish" inconsistently.** (19a) has an authorial reading that sounds fine to me ("I wish they'd had it land heads"), and that is the very reading (20) relies on.
6. **(22) is a rigged foil.** No standard theorist says "you should be certain that's not the case". The predicted retort is "they never showed it!", and that is felicitous.
7. **The inference to Scopelessness via (26)–(29) and footnote 35 is weak.** It rests on two contested claims: that "fail to" lexicalises negation, and that "whether" lexicalises negation, so that □f cannot apply to each answer. Pointwise question composition, or a homogeneity presupposition, makes (28) valid without FEM. Scopeless Inference is also stated for every attitude Φ but argued only for "know".
8. **The credence model in §8.2 is the crux, and it is unargued.** "C_S uniform over Dox_S" is ill-defined for infinite Dox, makes credence supervene on surety, and smuggles in the needed measure. Nothing explains why an *arbitrary* selection (§10) should be credally matched to in-story chance, still less to the typicality behind (16).
9. **§7.2 picks the wrong kind of gap.** (23)–(24) use an existence presupposition (the King of France). Future contingents, and Khoo-style indeterminate conditionals, are gap-type contents that do embed under wonder and credence. The abominable conjunctions in (25) assume disquotational truth, which gap theorists deny.
10. **(42) is a bad example.** The Breakfast Club has a Saturday detention and an essay, not a test (fairly confident). The Higginbotham equivalence in (40)–(43) is also predicted by homogeneity (von Fintel, Križ).
11. **§9.3, "we see no better way of explaining these similarities", is the paper's largest abductive leap.** A shared suppositional structure explains the parallel equally well. The PRP is also evaluated at w, so fictional truths depend counterfactually on irrelevant actual facts.
12. **§10's tests are skewed.** The vagueness table (45) is contested (Bacon 2018; Spencer 2022) and confounded, because there the speaker knows all the underlying facts. The "yes and no" test (46)–(47) is equally bizarre for future contingents and omits "it's not said". The oracle concession reads like a reductio.

### (d) Can a reply of at most 6,000 words be more than a reply?

Yes, if B&H serve as the occasion rather than the subject. Three things would make it so:

- **A general methodological thesis.** The felicity of graded, inquisitive or bouletic attitudes toward p does not diagnose bivalence for p, because attitudes can take a non-actual information state as their background. That bears directly on the CEM-from-credence arguments for counterfactuals (Moss, Schulz, Bacon, Mandelkern), on future contingents (Todd, Torre) and on vagueness (Bacon).
- **New, discriminating data that generalise.** Thought experiments, explicit "in the story" variants, third-person and past-tense ascriptions, oracle cases, and "the story doesn't say" answers.
- **A positive account of story-relative attitudes**, stated compactly enough to apply to suppositions and counterfactuals as well.

A paper that only marks up B&H's table remains a reply. A paper that offers a reusable test for when attitude embedding is evidence of factuality is not.

### 2. Soundness

**Does a story-relative account predict the whole table? Yes, but only if it is stated as support semantics.**
- Know, sure and "know whether" come out ✗; think, doubt and credence come out ✓ through the story measure; wonder and hope ✓; wish ✗ in (19), yet (20) ✓. Inference (28) is valid if negation inside "whether" and "fail to" is read as informational (excluding p in the story context).
- That reading means T1 is **not** "the standard analysis plus attitudes". The standard analysis makes ¬□h true; T1 needs bare "the coin didn't land heads" to be *unsupported*. T1 should be recast as Yalcin-style support semantics for internal discourse.
- Recast that way, it beats B&H's ad hoc charge in §7.2. The variation across attitudes falls out of each verb's own lexical condition applied to one shifted state, so no attitude-specific projection rule is needed.
- New datum for T1: B&H's King-of-France contrast (24) flips under explicit supposition. "Suppose France still had a king. I wonder whether he'd be happy" is ✓. What matters is whether a supposition is in force, not the type of gap.
- **Technical trap.** "Credence imaged on P-Fic" must be *general* (Gärdenfors) imaging, which spreads mass by in-story chance. Stalnakerian imaging gives Cr_f(p) = Cr(P-Fic > p) (Lewis 1976), which is exactly B&H's credence in their PRP counterfactual. On that version T1 is a notational variant of B&H.

**Is the argument from pure suppositions good? Good as an undercutter, not as a rebuttal.**
- In the die example, "it's 1/6 likely" and "I know whether ✗" are clean. "I wonder whether it landed six" is marginal, and "I hope it did" is odd unless stakes or immersion are added ("Suppose you've bet on six..."). That was my Phase 1 dissociation prediction, and it largely survives: T1 should add stakes or use narrative suppositions.

**Does the CEM reply neutralise it? Largely, yes.**
- B&H already identify fictional truth with counterfactual truth (§9.3), so the horn "fiction adds no independent evidence; it's the CEM debate" is one they will accept for the counterfactual cases. It does not refute them.
- What survives is real but modest. B&H present the attitude data as *abductive* evidence that is independent of CEM, and the dilemma shows that it cannot discriminate until CEM is settled. That imports the anti-CEM credence literature (Moss, Schulz, Khoo).

| P5 | A diagnosis, not a datum | Plausible: Khoo's footnote 29 uses "In the fiction F, p", so his credence denial targets the explicit-operator reading. It depends on P1. |

**Strongest objection: absorption.**
- B&H can say T1's story context just *is* their Dox_S pushed through the selection function. Every attitude judgment in the table then comes out the same, and the views differ only over whether the attitudes have factual objects.
- That difference shows up only in correctness and authority talk (P2, P3) and explicit operators (P1). Both sides already posit an internal/external ambiguity there (P5 included), so the decisive data are the most contestable ones.
- Second objection: T1's hope clause ("unsure in the story context") faces anomalous suspense (Gerrig 1989; Carroll 1996). Re-readers hope Romeo gets the letter.
- Third: Blumberg & Strohminger, "Imaginative hopes and other desires", *Analysis* 85(1), 2025 (verified on Crossref). One of B&H's own authors argues that imaginative analogues of hope fail the principles that "i-desire is to imagination as desire is to belief" would require. T1's hope clause is such a principle. The paper is paywalled and unread; it must be read.

### 3. Novelty

- **Oppy & Raivars** deny or deflate the data and gesture at extensions and pretence. But their "in any extension of JAG … credence 0.5" already *is* T1's credence clause, informally, so T1 must cite and differentiate it. T1's novelty is: granting all the data, giving a systematic semantics, and offering discriminators.
- **Khoo** denies informed credence. But §4's "related, but slightly different, theory … for incomplete fictions" is unread (Springer, not open access; checked) and **could pre-empt T1's positive account**. Read it before drafting.
- **i-attitudes and pretence** (Walton; Currie & Ravenscroft; Doggett & Egan) posit sui generis states. Langland-Hassan's "Operator Claim" (ordinary attitudes with "in the fiction" contents) is B&H's own frame and inherits their argument. T1 is a third option: ordinary attitudes with a shifted background. Neighbours to cite: Semeijn 2021 (the "workspace" common-ground account), Arcangeli 2019 (supposition as imagination), Yalcin.
- **Overall:** moderate. The theme is predictable; the semantics, discriminators and reconciliation are not.

### 4. Verdict: **PURSUE WITH CHANGES**

Changes:
1. Recast T1 as support semantics, and specify general imaging.
2. Make absorption the central dialectical problem, not a hidden risk.
3. Lead with P2 and its control, the "up to the reader" datum, and the supposed-King-of-France datum.
4. Present the CEM dilemma as parity, not refutation.
5. Drop the Edgington commitment and stay neutral on counterfactual semantics.
6. Read Khoo §4 and Blumberg & Strohminger first, and answer anomalous suspense.
7. Pitch the paper as a general test of when attitude embedding is evidence of factuality, which is what makes it more than a reply.

**Best version (one sentence):** B&H's attitude pattern is what any attitude must show when evaluated against an information state that leaves a question open, as stories and suppositions do; so it cannot discriminate fictional facts from story-relative support, and the evidence that does discriminate (correctness and authority talk, explicit operators, suppositions that flip the King-of-France contrast) favours support.
