# Fiction Is Not Counterfactual

*[Draft. Quotations from Blumberg & Holguín (B&H) are from the published version, with page references.]*

## 1. Introduction

When Holmes first meets Watson, does he have an odd or an even number of hairs on his head? Lewis thought it "absurd to suppose" that such questions about the world of Sherlock Holmes have answers (Lewis 1978: 42, quoted by Blumberg and Holguín 2025: 159). Blumberg and Holguín (B&H) argue that Lewis was wrong. "For any given work of fiction, there is a single world that determines what's true in it, and that world is as rich in detail as reality itself" (176). The parity of Holmes's hairs is "as factual as the question of whether he is a detective" (151). It is just unknowable.

Their case has two parts. The first is a profile of attitudes. We can wonder whether the coin at the end of a television series landed heads. We can think a biased coin more likely than not to have landed heads, and we can hope it did. But we cannot know, or be sure, how it landed. B&H argue that this profile is what we should expect if there is a fact we are ignorant of. The second part locates the fact. By their PRINCIPAL REALITY PRINCIPLE (PRP), what is true in a fiction is what would be true if the fiction's settled claims were true, on a Stalnakerian semantics for counterfactuals. Fictional truth is counterfactual truth. B&H support this by showing that indeterminate counterfactuals display the same attitude profile.

I argue that fiction and counterfactuals come apart, and in the place where the PRP needs them to agree. Strengthen the antecedent of a counterfactual with a detail that bears on a chancy outcome, and the outcome can change. Add the same detail to a story, without settling the outcome, and the outcome does not change; it stays open. Suppose a *JAG* reunion special had revealed that Bud tossed the coin unusually hard, and still not shown how it landed. Then compare:

- **(A)** It's about even odds that, if Bud had tossed the coin less hard, it would have landed the other way.
- **(B)** It's about even odds that, if the writers had left the special out, the coin would have landed the other way.

(A) is fine, and (B) is absurd. On the PRP, (A) and (B) stand or fall together.

The paper defends three claims.

1. **The strengthening test refutes the PRP** (§3). No account of the selection function that gets counterfactuals right gets fiction right.
2. **A story-state semantics passes it** (§4). On this semantics, bare fictional claims and attitudes toward them are evaluated against a body of information fixed by what the work settles. It predicts B&H's whole attitude profile with no single world. Given two bridge principles B&H need anyway, it agrees with them on every attitude toward a single fiction. And it predicts (A) and (B).
3. **What remains of the single world is idle** (§5). B&H can keep a single world by locating it some other way than by counterfactuals. But then nothing in the data favours it, and it buys bivalence with facts whose grounds B&H themselves concede are "almost entirely arbitrary" (188).

The alternative is not new in spirit. Williams and Woodward (2021) argued that the right attitude to what a fiction leaves open is an imaginative credence matching "the conditional probability of p given the fictive evidence", and that "there are, so to speak, no fictional facts of the matter" (MS 1–2). Lewis's (1978) analysis lets a story's truths grow as the story does, and Badura and Berto (2019) model truth in fiction as belief revision. Oppy and Raivars (2026) and Khoo (2026) reply to B&H by contesting their data. My contribution is the strengthening test, which separates the views where attitudes toward a single fiction cannot, and a semantics precise enough to show that the alternative passes it.

## 2. The case for fictional reality

B&H work within the modal approach to truth in fiction (Lewis 1978). A bare internal claim, such as "Holmes lives on Baker Street" on its natural reading, carries a silent operator □_f and is true iff its prejacent holds at every world in a set Fic_f. The *principal truths* of f are "the fictional claims that can be known to be true in f—at least in principle" (156): what it states or depicts, what its author intended, and what follows from these with background reality. P-Fic_f is the set of worlds compatible with them. The *standard analysis* sets Fic_f = P-Fic_f, so a claim the principal truths leave unsettled is false, and so is its negation.

**The data.** B&H's central case is the final episode of *JAG*. Bud tosses a fair coin to decide which of Mac and Harm will resign and follow the other, and the episode ends with the coin in the air. B&H stipulate, "perhaps counterfactually", that the writers had no intentions about the outcome "and then never thought about the events of JAG again" (161).[^jag] With complement "Bud's coin landed heads", they report: *know*, *be sure*, *know whether* ✗; *think* ✗ for a fair coin and ✓ for one biased 2:1; *doubt* likewise; "It's .5 likely that" ✓; *wonder whether* ✓; *wish* ✗; *hope* ✓ (161–167).

On the standard analysis, anyone who knows the show knows that "Bud's coin landed heads" is false. So they know whether it is true, are sure it is not, and should be able to wish it were. B&H call the bridge from known semantic value to attitude ATTITUDE EQUIVALENCE (167). Treating indeterminate claims as undefined does no better: a known gap makes "I wonder whether the King of France is happy" bad, but known fictional indeterminacy does not make "I wonder whether Bud's coin landed heads" bad (169–171). And any trivalent view, they argue, predicts that "I know whether p is true, but I don't know whether p" can be uttered truly (171).

**From the data to a single world.** B&H find this inference "unimpeachable" (172): "I don't know that Bud's coin landed heads. And I don't know that it failed to land heads. So I don't know whether Bud's coin landed heads." Since "fail to" lexicalises negation, the inference requires SCOPELESSNESS: ¬□_f p iff □_f¬p (173). Because □_f quantifies over Fic_f, Scopelessness forces Fic_f to have exactly one member (175 n. 39). Fictional excluded middle follows: □_f p ∨ □_f¬p (176 and n. 40).

**Locating the world.** The PRP says that p is true in f at w iff P-Fic_{f,w} > p is true at w, read on Stalnaker's selection semantics (182). The world of f is "the unique world that would accurately represent how things are, were all the principal claims of f really true" (183). Indeterminate counterfactuals ("If Flippy had been flipped, it would have landed heads") show the same attitude profile as fiction, which B&H take to give the PRP "strong abductive support" (182): "we see no better way of explaining these similarities" (183). Since similarity rarely favours one candidate world over another, the selection function picks "at random, so to speak" (184). That is why the facts are unknowable.

## 3. The strengthening test

**Counterfactuals.** Any adequate selection function must respect two judgements about chancy outcomes. Suppose Bud actually tossed a fair coin and it landed heads.
- *Relevant strengthening can change the outcome.* "Had Bud tossed the coin less hard, it would still have landed heads" is not true. The landing of a tossed coin depends sensitively on the force of the toss.
- *Irrelevant strengthening cannot.* "Had a moth been in the next room, the coin would still have landed heads" is fine. This is the moral of Morgenbesser-style cases (Schaffer 2004; Kment 2006).

A sophisticated Stalnakerian can respect both. Order worlds by similarity to actuality, and break near-ties by agreement with one fixed assignment of values to the exogenous variables of the apt causal model, the same assignment for every antecedent (cf. Briggs 2012). The force of the toss is a cause of the outcome, so strengthening by force redraws the coin. The moth is not, so it leaves the coin alone. This is the best the PRP can do. If a selection function like this fails, the PRP fails.

**The case.** Suppose that, years after *JAG* ended, its makers produced a reunion special set just before the finale. In it we learn that Bud tossed the coin unusually hard, a trick he had learned as a boy. We still never see how it lands. The special is consistent with everything in *JAG*, so its P-Fic, call it B, is a subset of *JAG*'s, call it A. Asked about the extended story, an informed viewer will judge:

| | Judgement | PRP | Story state |
|---|---|---|---|
| (A) | It's about even odds that, if Bud had tossed the coin less hard, it would have landed the other way. | ✓ | ✓ |
| (B) | It's about even odds that, if the writers had left the special out, the coin would have landed the other way. | ✓ | ✗ |
| (C) | If the special had shown the coin landing heads, it would have landed heads. | ✓ | ✓ |
| (D) | It's about even odds that, if the writers had left the special out, the coin would have landed heads. | ✓ | ✓ |

My verdicts are (A) ✓, (B) ✗, (C) ✓, (D) ✓. (B) and (D) have the same antecedent and the same frame, a credence report on a counterfactual about the telling with a bare fictional consequent. They differ only in the consequent. (D) shows that speakers give that consequent the reading B&H's credence data require, on which it can be .5 likely. A reading on which it concerns what the story settles would make (D) absurd, since without the special the story settles nothing about the coin. So the rejection of (B) is not a retreat to a "what the story says" reading. (C) shows that, under a counterfactual about the telling, the bare consequent follows the counterfactual story. (A) and (B) then differ only in whether the antecedent changes the toss or the telling.[^test]

**What the PRP predicts.** Under the PRP, the world of the extended story is s(B, @), the closest world to actuality in which everything in *JAG* and the special holds. Had the special been left out, the world of the story would have been s(A, @).
- If s(A, @) is already a world in which Bud tosses unusually hard, the two coincide. But nothing in *JAG* suggests an unusual toss, and a world closest to actuality will have an ordinary one. So almost certainly s(A, @) ∉ B, and s(B, @) is a different world, one that differs from s(A, @) by an intervention on a cause of the coin's landing.
- That is exactly the difference that, by the first counterfactual judgement, redraws the coin. Whatever tie-breaking makes (A) come out right, by letting the outcome depend on the force, makes s(A, @) and s(B, @) disagree about the outcome about half the time. So the PRP predicts (B) ✓, just as it predicts (D) ✓.
- Could the PRP keep (B) false by evaluating the bare consequent at the actual story's world whatever the counterfactual says about the telling? Then (C) fails: if the special had shown heads, the coin would have landed heads only if it did at s(B, @), which is even odds.

The general point is this. The PRP makes adding a detail to a story the same operation as strengthening the antecedent of a counterfactual. For details that bear on an open chancy question, counterfactual strengthening can change the answer, and adding the detail to the story cannot. No choice of selection function both gets counterfactuals right and gets fiction right.

**Why the test is counterfactual.** The same contrast can be posed over time. "Did the special change how the coin landed?" B&H's account gives it a ready answer. On their versions option, ordinary talk "is directed at the final version"; on their time-indexing option, by default "we talk about what's true in the most recent version" (193–194). Either way, claims about the coin made before and after the special are assessed at a single world. Developed as assessment relativism in the manner of McGonigal (2013), this makes every temporal judgement come out as it should. Counterfactuals about the telling are different, because they change which version is final.[^gloria]

## 4. Story states

**The fragment.** The rival view evaluates bare fictional claims against an information state shifted by the story.

> *Priors.* For each world w, π_w is a probability over worlds encoding w's regularities and the chances internal to whatever is supposed.
>
> *Shift.* P_f(w) is the set of worlds compatible with f's principal truths as they are at w. For an information state s with credence C: s⊕f = ⋃_{w∈s} supp π_w(· | P_f(w)), and C⊕f = Σ_w C(w) · π_w(· | P_f(w)).
>
> *Bare claims.* A bare declarative φ, with all its connectives, quantifiers and embedded counterfactuals, is interpreted inside the shift. s supports f:φ iff s⊕f ⊆ |φ|; s rejects f:φ iff s⊕f ∩ |φ| = ∅. Counterfactuals inside φ get their ordinary semantics at each world of s⊕f.
>
> *Stages.* A compound that mentions two stages or versions X, Y of a story, with Y's principal content including X's, is evaluated at the worlds of s⊕Y. At such a world v, the X-claim T_X φ has the value |φ|(v); it is undefined at worlds outside s⊕X.
>
> *Truth.* Internal "true" is transparent: f:(φ is true) ≡ f:φ. A bare claim about f is right at a world w, as assessed from a context c, iff {w}⊕f supports it, with f taken as it stands at w at the time of c.
>
> *Attitudes.* The story is a parameter of the context. An attitude verb applies its ordinary entry to the subject's own state shifted by f: D_S⊕f for her certainties and C_S⊕f for her credences.

Four consequences matter here.

1. **Scopelessness without a world.** Because ⊕ distributes over unions, support and rejection are settled world by world. f:φ therefore behaves like a homogeneous plural predication: supported where every candidate world is a φ-world, rejected where none is, and gappy otherwise (Križ 2015). "Bud's coin didn't land heads" and "Bud's coin failed to land heads" both express f:¬h, supported exactly when f:h is rejected. That is Scopelessness. In B&H's derivation of a single world (175 n. 39), "not every world in Fic_f is a p-world" is a fact *about* the state: the story does not settle p. It is not the content of any bare claim, so the slide from two worlds to a falsified Scopelessness never starts.
2. **FEM fails; internal excluded middle holds.** f:(h ∨ ¬h) is supported, and with transparent truth so is "One of (9a) and (9b) is true". But neither f:h nor f:¬h is supported.
3. **Refinement (for support).** If Y's principal content includes X's, then s⊕Y ⊆ s⊕X. So nothing X supports or rejects becomes unsettled in Y, and nothing settled becomes right and then wrong. (The credal half of this does not hold: C⊕Y is a general image, not C⊕X conditioned. Nothing below needs it.)
4. **Neutrality.** Say that an addition is *neutral* on φ if, at every world, the later image meets whichever of |φ| and |¬φ| the earlier image meets. A neutral addition leaves φ's status unchanged.

The assessment clause is the fictional analogue of MacFarlane's (2003) treatment of future contingents. Johnny, who said in 1977 that Luke is Darth Vader's son, said something unsettled as assessed from 1977 and right as assessed after *The Empire Strikes Back*, when he can "rightly claim credit for a lucky guess" (McGonigal 2013: 177). By Refinement, claims move only from unsettled to settled, never from right to wrong, unless the story is revised. B&H's view, with a selected world at each stage, is the fictional counterpart of the "thin red line" (Belnap and Green 1994): a privileged history the facts do not single out.

**The table.** For a viewer who knows *JAG*, D⊕f contains heads-worlds and tails-worlds, and C⊕f gives each .5 (2/3 and 1/3 for the biased coin).
- *Know*, *be sure* and *know whether* (support in D⊕f) are ✗.
- "It's .5 likely" is ✓.
- *Think*, which B&H gloss as "more likely than not" (175), is ✗ for the fair coin and ✓ for the biased one; *doubt* likewise.
- *Wonder* and *hope* (the question is open in D⊕f) are ✓.
- *Wish*, which requires ¬h to be supported, is ✗.

The "unimpeachable" inference is valid. No verb needs a special projection rule: each keeps its lexical entry and looks at the same shifted state.

**The test.**
- **(A)** For each world v of the shifted state, the embedded counterfactual is evaluated at v. On a Stalnakerian semantics with a chance–selection link, which I am granting for counterfactuals, it holds at about half of them. So (A) is ✓.
- **(B)** This compares the story without the special with the story with it. By *Stages*, it is evaluated at the worlds of s⊕B, where the coin lands one way, once. So "the coin would have landed the other way" is rejected at every such world, and (B) is ✗.
- **(C)** Its consequent is supported in the counterfactual story, so (C) is ✓.
- **(D)** In the story without the special, heads holds at about half the worlds of the shifted state, so (D) is ✓.

The asymmetry has a simple source. An in-story counterfactual moves from a candidate world to another world. Adding to the story discards candidates and moves none.

**Trivalence and vagueness.** Since the view has gaps, B&H's objection to trivalent views (171) applies to it. With a transparent internal truth predicate, "I know whether p is true, but I don't know whether p" is, on its bare reading, a contradiction, and so it is bad. It is acceptable only when "true" is heard as "true according to the story", and then it is acceptable. The gaps are also not those of vagueness, which B&H distinguish from fictional indeterminacy because vague claims are bad under attitudes and invite "Yes and no" (186–187). The candidate worlds in a story state are not precisifications of "odd" or "heads". They are factual alternatives, weighted by chances, and the story is what leaves them open. So *wonder* and *hope* are licensed, and "Yes and no" is not. The answer is "the story doesn't say". The indeterminacy is neither semantic nor metaphysical nor epistemic. It is the informational incompleteness of a work, which is Lewis's thought, made fit for attitudes.

**Absorption.** On B&H's view, a credence that p is true in f is a credence about where the selection lands. They need it to track in-story chances, and they suggest that a rational agent will be "doxastically indifferent to the various possibilities compatible with the principal truths" (175). Two bridge principles make this precise.
- **Chance–selection link.** Given any world w, S's credence that s(P_f(w), w) lies in X is π_w(X | P_f(w)). This is a link of the kind Schulz (2017) defends.
- **Openness.** The selections S's certainties leave open, given w, are exactly the support of π_w(· | P_f(w)). This follows from B&H's definition of principal truth as what can be known to be true in f (156). Anything that can be known about the world of f is principal, so S cannot rule out any selection that P-Fic allows.

Given both, for every attitude in B&H's table and any single fiction, S bears the attitude to □_f φ on B&H's view iff she bears it to f:φ on the story-state view.[^absorb] The data in B&H's §6 therefore cannot discriminate, and neither can the counterfactual parallel of their §9: any view on which these attitudes are evaluated against a shifted state predicts it. Absorption fixes credences one story at a time, and says nothing about how selections for different stories are related. That is why the strengthening test, which compares two tellings, can do what the attitude data cannot.

## 5. What remains of the single world

The strengthening test refutes the PRP. It does not refute every single-world view. B&H could keep a single world per fiction and locate it some other way.

**Defaults.** Rank the questions a world answers, fix a default answer to each, and let the world of f be the P-Fic_f-world that matches the defaults lexicographically. Rank the coin's outcome above the force of the toss, and the special leaves the outcome alone, so (B) is ✗. In-story counterfactuals still use the ordinary selection function, so (A) is ✓. But this ordering cannot be the counterfactual one: it holds outcomes fixed while their causes vary. Taking it means giving up the PRP. Two things go with it: B&H's answer to which world is the world of f, and their explanation of the counterfactual parallel, though by §4 that explanation was not needed.

**Supervaluation over selections.** B&H mention, and reject for themselves, the Stalnakerian option of supervaluating over admissible selection functions (185; Stalnaker 1980). Khoo (2026: §4) develops it for fiction. A defender could use it here, since it gives up the single world. But it passes the test only if every admissible selection keeps the outcome fixed when the force varies. That is the defaults view again, now as a penumbral constraint.

**Other temporal treatments.** A total narrative, on which every stage's world is that of the eventual whole (McGonigal's (2013: 168) "extreme realism"; Walters 2015), and path-dependent selection, s(B, s(A, @)), both still run through counterfactual selection. Neither passes the test: the counterfactual about the telling changes the whole, or the path.

**The ledger.** So the live single-world view is the defaults view. Compare it with the story state.
- *Shared posits.* Both need the priors π_w. On B&H's side they enter through the chance–selection link.
- *What the defaults view adds.* A ranking of all questions, and a default answer to each, which no one could know. In return it gets classical logic for the fiction operator and bivalence for every bare claim.
- *What the story-state view adds.* Gaps, governed by the logic of homogeneity, and two truth notions: transparent internal truth, and assessment-sensitive rightness.
- *Data.* By absorption and the test, the two views predict the same judgements, so the data tie.

B&H face this choice squarely. If fictional truth is to explain the attitudes, there must be facts "whose grounds will in many cases be almost entirely arbitrary. We would sooner accept the consequent of this conditional than deny the antecedent" (188). §4 denies the antecedent: the attitudes are explained without those facts. And with the PRP gone, the facts no longer earn their keep even as a unification with counterfactuals. What remains is bivalence for its own sake, bought with arbitrary and unknowable defaults that no judgement tracks. B&H's own standard was that we should posit what best explains our attitudes, and by it the single world has no support.

## 6. Objections

**Deliberate openness.** Some works leave questions open on purpose. Stockton's "The Lady, or the Tiger?" ends by asking the reader which came out of the door. On B&H's view an arbitrary fact answers it anyway. RESTRICTED FEM (192) exempts only fictions that make a truth-value gap a principal truth, and Stockton's story is open, not gappy. On the story-state view the question stays open, as its author meant.

**Re-readers.** Someone who knows how *Romeo and Juliet* ends can still hope, at Act V, that Romeo gets the letter (Gerrig 1989). If hope requires the question to be open in D_S⊕f, re-reader hope looks impossible. But the state that is shifted can be the one S occupies as a reader at a point in the story: what she takes as revealed so far. Imaginative theories of engagement say as much (Walton 1990; Currie 1990), and nothing in §4 depends on which state is shifted. B&H can make the same move, so this marks no difference between the views.

**Is (B) about authors, not coins?** Someone might hear (B) as a claim about what the writers would have done, and find it false for that reason. But (B) does not say the writers would have shown the coin landing the other way. Under the PRP its consequent concerns the world of the counterfactual story, which the writers do not choose. (D), with the same antecedent, is accepted at even odds, so speakers are not hearing the consequent as a claim about what the writers would have shown. (C) shows that they let the bare consequent follow the counterfactual story when the story settles the matter. The test asks whether a non-settling difference in the telling makes a difference to the coin, and speakers judge that it does not.

## 7. Conclusion

B&H showed that the standard analysis, combined with ATTITUDE EQUIVALENCE, mispredicts our attitudes toward fictional indeterminacy, and they were right about that. They then made two further moves. They took the attitudes as evidence of hidden facts, and they took counterfactuals to locate those facts. The first fails because attitudes toward one story are equally well explained by a story state. The second fails because stories and counterfactuals respond differently to added detail. A detail that bears on an open chancy question can change the answer to a counterfactual. In a story it cannot, because the story never gave an answer to change. What is left is a single world located by defaults that nothing tracks, and Lewis's answer to his question stands.

[^gloria]: The test needs a relevant addition. If selections for different antecedents were independent draws, even an irrelevant addition, such as "The Gloria Scott" (1893), which tells of Holmes's student days, would redraw the parity of the hairs on his head when he met Watson. With the causal tie-breaking above it does not, so I rest nothing on irrelevant additions.

[^absorb]: For credence, average the selection over the chance–selection link: C_S(□_f φ) = Σ_w C_S(w) π_w(|φ| | P_f(w)) = (C_S⊕f)(|φ|). For certainty, Openness gives: S is sure of □_f φ iff every open selection is a φ-world iff D_S⊕f ⊆ |φ|. The other attitudes in the table are defined from these.
