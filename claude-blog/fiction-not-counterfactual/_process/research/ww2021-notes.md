# Williams & Woodward 2021, "The Cognitive Role of Fictionality", PPR 102(2): 423-438, doi 10.1111/phpr.12660

- !1: incompleteness: "the heroine's eyes are neither fictionally blue nor fictionally some other colour"; "There are, so to speak,
  no fictional facts of the matter."
- !2: evidentialism: "one's degree of imaginative confidence in p should match the conditional probability of p given the fictive
  evidence"; "fictionality stands to imagining as evidence stands to credence" (vs Walton's truth analogy).
- §2: Fictional Incompleteness = not fictional that p & not fictional that not-p; prohibitionism vs permissivism; cases: Unspecified
  Details (Moriarty's eyes, Holmes's hairs, Hermione's skin: permissive), Deliberate Withholding (Blade Runner/Deckard; Twin Peaks
  if it had ended after season 1: "plausibly required to speculate and wonder" -- prohibitive), Genre Incompleteness (Turn of the
  Screw), Silly Questions (Walton 1990: 174).
- §3 Waltonian pluralism: w-fictional = g-fictional in every authorised game; permissive (multiple games) vs prohibitive
  (incomplete games).
- §4 KEY: the fictionality-truth analogy fails: "Truth value gaps prohibit uncertainty as much as they do belief" (!15); KoF example
  (!15); repugnant conjunction "p and it is not true that p" (!15). [cf. B&H §7.2 (23)-(25): same KoF + abominable-conjunction
  argument against gap views -- B&H do not credit W&W for it.] fn 12: vague borderline cases contentious.
- §5 Evidentialism: i-credences; "our metafictional beliefs are not hedged: we are fully confident that it is neither fictional that
  Deckard is human nor fictional that he is a replicant" (!17); explains weighting "at the level of the imaginative states
  themselves" rather than "credences about what is fictional" (!17). Rule: "Evidentialism. One's i-credence in p should be n iff
  the probability of p given the fictive evidence = n" (!18). Fictive evidence = what is fictional, or what is KNOWN to be fictional
  (Inception example; they prefer the latter, fn 17).
- §6: Fair and Bias films (dice; "our i-credence in the proposition that Hero lives should be differently weighted in the two
  stories", !20-21); the PERMISSION PROBLEM (evidentialism makes all incompleteness prohibitive); replies: enrichment (games add
  evidence) and relocation (fictive SUPPOSITION vs make-belief; affect: "horrifying make-beliefs tend to produce horror whereas
  horrifying suppositions do not (see Kind (2013), Arcangeli (2017))", !23).
- Conclusion: "(known) fictionality stands to the imagination as evidence stands to credence."
