**Say what speech act Ann and Carl perform: "right" may track parafictional content, letting B&H absorb all four judgements. Rest the growth argument on an attitude datum that forces the bare reading.**

*Where:* §5, "The data" and "B&H cannot read them as being about principal truth"; §3, "Explicit operators".

*The problem.* Judgements (1)–(4) concern whether Ann and Carl are "right". The paper never says what they are doing.

1. **Flat assertion is defective by B&H's lights.** Their ASSERTABILITY discussion (170) treats bare assertion of a known-indeterminate claim like (9a) as infelicitous. So Ann and Carl are either conjecturing, which B&H's *think* and *hope* data license, or asserting without warrant. "Right" tracks the hidden fact in the first case and warrant in the second.

2. **"Right" invites checking the work.** The ordinary reply to "Holmes had an odd number of hairs" is "Is that so? Check the stories". That is the parafictional use (Recanati's fictive/parafictional contrast, from memory; check; Kroon and Voltolini 2024, cited by B&H), whose content is that the stories make it so. By §3 the paper gives such explicit claims Lewis-style semantics that "may diverge" from bare ones. On that content Ann and Carl are both wrong, and nothing since 1890 changes it. That yields (1) and (3) ✓, (2) and (4) ✗, the paper's own predictions. B&H can accept all four judgements and say they concern parafictional content, for which they claim no hidden fact (153 n. 6 leaves this open). The paper's reply, that B&H hold bare claims "true or false beyond what the work settles", confuses a sentence's truth-conditions with what agreeing targets.

3. **The bare/explicit line is drawn by surface form** (an overt operator), following B&H's ostension (152–153). The relevant line is the mode of the speech act. And B&H's "by default … the most recent version" (194) lets them say the two tokens concern different versions, so (1), "same claim", is theory-laden.
