## 1. Verdicts

### I1. Bare internal attitude reports are story-relative attitudes, and fictional truth stays principal truth: PARTLY (strongly)

The core idea is in **Williams & Woodward 2021, "The Cognitive Role of Fictionality"** (PPR 102: 423–38). B&H cite this paper three times (fnn. 17, 22, 31), but only for examples. They never discuss its main proposal.

- They posit graded imaginative attitudes toward fiction's open questions, fixed by probability conditional on the fiction, while fictional truth stays incomplete. In their words: "one's degree of imaginative confidence in p should match the conditional probability of p given the fictive evidence" (p. 2). The rule is stated as "**Evidentialism.** One's i-credence in p should be n iff the probability of p given the fictive evidence = n" (p. 18). On fictional truth: "There are, so to speak, no fictional facts of the matter" (p. 1).
- They separate the two levels explicitly: "our metafictional beliefs are not hedged: we are fully confident that it is neither fictional that Deckard is human nor fictional that he is a replicant" (p. 17). They locate the hedging "at the level of the imaginative states themselves", rather than in "credences about what is fictional" (p. 17).
- **They use B&H's own example in advance.** "Consider two fictitious films, Fair and Bias. Both end with Villain rolling a dice… In Fair, the dice is fair. But in Bias, the dice is weighted"; "our i-credence in the proposition that Hero lives should be differently weighted in the two stories" (pp. 20–21).
- On inquisitive attitudes: "imaginative uncertainty, allowing for speculation and wonder" (p. 9).

Further precursors:
- **Badura & Berto 2019** model truth in fiction as the reader's belief state revised by the story: "The overall content of the pretence is what holds in the worlds where the explicit pretence premises are true, and which are the most plausible in terms of our background beliefs" (p. 7). This is T1's "story context" machinery, but they use it to define truth, not attitudes.
- **Lewis 1978**: worlds where the story is "told as known fact". From memory, but standard.

**What is missing.**
- Williams & Woodward posit sui generis i-credences (a "make-belief" attitude). They give no semantics for ordinary attitude verbs and say nothing about know, sure, think, doubt, wish or hope.
- They use conditionalization, not imaging.
- They contrast make-belief with supposition, whereas T1 calls the story context suppositional (see objection 4 in §4).
- No one I found proposes that bare internal complements shift the information-state parameter of ordinary attitude verbs.

### I2. T1 predicts B&H's whole table: PARTLY

- **Already in Williams & Woodward:** credence, sensitivity to in-story chance (Fair/Bias), and wonder or speculation.
- **The know-whether prediction** is partly anticipated in B&H fn. 35 (p. 19). A reviewer proposes that "□f takes wide-scope with respect to negation in 'know whether' clauses". B&H reply that "'whether'… 'lexicalizes negation'" and so "cannot be outscoped by operators such as □f". T1's parameter shift gives the same truth conditions as that wide-scope reading, so the paper must show why B&H's objection to it does not apply.
- **The counterfactual column under a suppositional reading** is anticipated by Bennett 2003, p. 251, quoted in Moss 2013, pp. 7–8: "the speaker means something of the form A>(P(C)=n)—if the antecedent were true, the consequent would have a certain probability". Moss objects that this reinterpretation strategy fails on "Would this coin have landed heads if it had been flipped? … #No" (p. 8).
- **Khoo 2022** gives a non-factualist account of "leaving open" counterfactuals: "conditionals are not in the business of stating facts at all… conditionals encode inferential dispositions" (p. 15).
- **Not found:** a derivation of the whole eight-row fiction table from a single story-relative information state.

### I3. The pattern recurs under pure supposition, and the case for fictional reality collapses into the case for CEM: PARTLY, plus NOT FOUND

- **The collapse into CEM is already B&H's own position.** They write that "fictional truth is a kind of counterfactual truth" (§9.3, p. 32). Their arguments for FEM "are primarily driven by judgments about embedding patterns, rather than a prior commitment to CEM" (fn. 57, p. 33). Woodward 2011a has CEM-based completeness for fictionalisms.
  - So the reduction is a premise B&H accept, not a reductio of their view.
  - What is new is only the further claim that fiction supplies no independent evidence.
- **The overgeneration argument from pure suppositions and thought experiments:** NOT FOUND.
  - I searched for replies to B&H, "imaginative credence", "conditional on the story" and "under the supposition", plus the replies by Oppy & Raivars and Khoo 2026. I did not find it.
  - The argument is weak, though (objection 2 in §4).

### I4. The dispute is between a bare (suppositional) reading and an explicit external ("true in f") reading: PARTLY

The distinction itself already exists in several places:
- **B&H** (p. 5): "we leave it as an open possibility that a certain fictional claim p could be true on its internal reading even when a sentence like ⌜According to the relevant work of fiction, p⌝ is false on all of its available readings."
- **Bowker 2021** (abstract only) argues that bare fictional sentences are not abbreviations of "In the Sherlock Holmes stories…".
- **Sainsbury 2014 and Semeijn 2023** distinguish "in" from "according to" (Semeijn not read).
- **Langland-Hassan 2020, ch. 10** (abstract only) holds that fiction-directed attitudes are beliefs and desires with "in the fiction" operators. This is the pure external reading.
- **Williams & Woodward** contrast "credences about what is fictional" with i-credences (p. 17). This is exactly I4's two readings.
- **Oppy & Raivars 2026** gesture at "extensions" and pretence readings (per our notes).

**What is new:** using the distinction to say what each side of the published dispute is tracking (B&H on one side; Khoo 2026 and Oppy & Raivars on the other). No one has done this.

### I5. The discriminating data: PARTLY

- **Explicit "true in f" (P1).**
  - Williams & Woodward's unhedged metafictional beliefs (p. 17) already give T1's verdict.
  - Khoo 2026 uses explicit "in the fiction of The Shining" forms (nn. 13, 23). He predicts that "someone who believes that p is not determined by the content of the fiction F will have no non-trivial credence that p" (n. 29). I have only the abstract and footnotes for this.
- **"Neither of you is right" (P2) and authorial openness (P3).** B&H §6.6 (p. 17) already consider and dismiss a reviewer's dialogue: "the writers said in an interview that they have intentionally left it open what happens to the coin? There isn't a fact of the matter whether the coin landed heads!" Their reply is that "'fact of the matter'… is a semi-technical notion" (p. 18).
  - Williams & Woodward have "Deliberate Withholding" cases (Blade Runner, Twin Peaks) and "Genre Incompleteness" (The Turn of the Screw), pp. 8–9.
  - B&H §11.2 (Restricted FEM) handles fictions on which "no fact" is itself a principal truth.
- **"The Lady, or the Tiger?" as a test case:** NOT FOUND in the philosophical literature I could search. Searches returned only literary summaries.
- **The non-technical "neither of you is right":** NOT FOUND.

## 2. Overall verdict: PARTLY ANTICIPATED

T1's central idea is that open fictional questions are objects of graded, chance-sensitive attitudes held relative to the story, while fictional truth stays incomplete. That is Williams & Woodward's evidentialism. A paper that presents it as new would be rejected by any referee who knows that paper, and B&H's own bibliography lists it.

**What the paper must cite:**
- **Williams & Woodward 2021:** the central precursor, including its make-belief versus supposition distinction and its "permission problem".
- **Badura & Berto 2019; Lewis 1978:** the story context as a revised belief state.
- **Bennett 2003, p. 251; Edgington; Moss 2013; Khoo 2022:** the counterfactual analogues, and Moss's objections to the reinterpretation strategy.
- **Yalcin 2007; Kolodny & MacFarlane 2010:** the information-state mechanism.
- **Walton 1990; Currie & Ravenscroft 2002; Doggett & Egan 2007/2012; Langland-Hassan 2020:** imaginative attitudes versus "in the fiction" attitudes.
- **Bowker 2021; Sainsbury 2014; Semeijn 2023:** bare versus explicit operators.
- **B&H fn. 35, §6.6 and fn. 57; Oppy & Raivars 2026; Khoo 2026.**

**What remains new (as far as I can find):**
1. **The opening in B&H.** B&H never consider a reply in terms of imaginative or story-relative attitudes, so that reply is unanswered in print. Their only alternatives to FEM are the standard analysis and undefinedness (§7).
2. **A semantics.** Ordinary attitude verbs taking a story-shifted information state, deriving the full table, including inference (28), wish versus hope, and the vagueness contrast, from one parameter instead of positing i-attitudes. This would also explain why Williams & Woodward's i-credences are reported with ordinary "likely", "wonder" and "hope".
3. **The diagnosis of the B&H–Khoo/Oppy & Raivars dispute** as a clash between two readings.
4. **The pure-supposition argument**, which is weak as stated.

**Suggested reframing:** "Williams & Woodward's evidentialism, made semantic, answers Blumberg & Holguín", not "a new suppositional account".

## 4. Possible decisive objections

1. **The two views agree on the credence data.**
   - By Lewis's imaging result (1976), credence in a Stalnaker conditional P-Fic > p equals credence imaged on P-Fic. So T1's "imaged credence" and B&H's credence in □f p coincide whenever B&H's uncertainty over selections is chance-guided. Schulz 2017 and Bacon 2015, both cited in B&H fn. 59, supply exactly that link.
   - P4 is therefore not a divergence, and all the weight falls on P1–P3, which B&H have already dismissed once (§6.6).
   - This is my own analysis; it needs checking.
2. **I3 has a modal-subordination escape.**
   - In "Imagine a die rolled… I wonder whether it landed six", the attitude verb is naturally read inside the supposition, so it reports a hypothetical attitude. With fiction, the attitudes reported are present and persisting (fan speculation; B&H fn. 27).
   - B&H can also treat thought experiments as fictions (Walton-style accounts) and apply the Principal Reality Principle.
   - The "collapse into CEM" is their stated view (§9.3, fn. 57).
3. **P2 and P3 are already handled.** B&H §6.6 already answers the dialogue about the writers' interview. "Neither of you is right" can be heard epistemically ("neither of you is entitled to say"). "The Lady, or the Tiger?" adds little beyond the JAG interview case unless it is shown to differ from B&H's Restricted-FEM cases.
4. **"Supposition" may be the wrong attitude.** Williams & Woodward, citing Kind 2013 and Arcangeli 2017, note that "horrifying make-beliefs tend to produce horror whereas horrifying suppositions do not" (p. 23). Hope and wish about fiction are affective. If the story context has to be imaginative rather than suppositional, T1 drifts back to Williams & Woodward's i-attitudes. That would also undercut I3's appeal to pure suppositions.
5. **Mixed complements and quantification.**
   - A parameter shift escapes B&H's "whether lexicalizes negation" point (fn. 35), but it must handle a single attitude verb taking both internal and external material ("I wonder whether Bud's coin landed heads and whether the writers ever decided").
   - It must also handle mixed quantification of the Breakfast Club kind ((42), p. 31), where one information state cannot serve both. Edgington-style views' known trouble with quantified conditionals (Higginbotham) probably carries over.
   - The fiction version of Moss's "#No" ("Did Bud's coin land heads?" "#No") needs an explicit answer from T1's assertion norm. The standard analysis T1 keeps makes the bare "It didn't land heads" (narrow-scope negation) false, which is fine, but the external "No" is true, which is not.
