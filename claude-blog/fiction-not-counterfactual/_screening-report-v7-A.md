# Screening report (v7, screener A)

- **Paper title:** Fiction Without Hidden Facts
- **Word count:** about 5,730 words of body text including footnotes and the table, plus a 210-word abstract (about 5,960 in the source file)
- **Thesis summary:** Blumberg and Holguín (B&H 2025) argue that each fiction has a single world that settles every question, and that the Principal Reality Principle (PRP) locates it by Stalnakerian counterfactuals. The paper rejects both steps. A story-state semantics (bare claims supported or rejected by a story-shifted, chance-weighted information state, with homogeneous negation) validates Scopelessness, the "unimpeachable" inference and the Higginbotham equivalences without a single world, and it matches B&H's view on every attitude toward a single fiction. A "strengthening test" (a sequel adds a causally relevant detail without settling a chancy outcome) separates fiction from counterfactuals, and the PRP fails it. So the single world is unsupported and Lewis's plural worlds are the better-supported view.
- **Verdict:** PASS, FORMAL

Judged under the project owner's modification: being a reply is not held against the paper. The question is whether it is a good reply.

## Key strengths

- **It goes after the load-bearing parts of the target, not its edges.** The first stage targets B&H's footnote 39, where Scopelessness yields Uniqueness, and through it FEM. That is the inference that turns their attitude data into a single world. The diagnosis is exact: the step assumes that the negation in ¬□_f p is Boolean negation over a universal quantifier. If negation is homogeneous, Scopelessness needs "a single point of evaluation, not a single world". The second stage targets the PRP, which is B&H's answer to what fixes the world and why it cannot be known.
- **The argument is well designed.** The absorption result shows that, given a chance–selection link and Openness, attitudes toward a single fiction cannot tell the two views apart. That neutralises both the attitude table and the fiction/counterfactual parallel that B&H offer as "strong abductive support" (182). The paper then names the only place the views can part, comparisons between tellings, and builds its test there. A reply that first shows the data are neutral and then finds discriminating data is doing more than finding a bug.
- **The strengthening test is a new, specific datum, and it is properly controlled.** Under the PRP with uniform causal tie-breaking, the in-story counterfactual (A) and the telling counterfactual (B) come out equivalent. Judgement separates them. Item (D) blocks the objection that the consequent of (B) is read as "what the story settles", and item (C) blocks evaluating that consequent at the actual series' world. §5 maps the single-world views that survive (anchoring, defaults, path-dependence, supervaluation). For each it says what extra rule it adds and why counterfactuals reject that rule. The general point, that adding detail to a story removes possibilities without redrawing open outcomes, bears on any counterfactual or Reality-Principle analysis of fiction, not only B&H's.

## Key weaknesses

- **The decisive datum is the author's own judgement on a hard construction.** Item (B) is a probability report embedding a counterfactual about the telling, with a comparative "differently" whose reference point is, on one of the two views, a non-fact. B&H's data are simpler attitude reports. The paper says it is offering its judgements "on the same footing", but the footing is weaker. The obvious next case is not discussed: a telling that *replaces* a cause instead of adding one (the special shows the toss in still air, or a gust is swapped for calm). There the maximal coupling says the landing is certainly the same across tellings that depict different causes. That is the mirror image of the "Defaults" ordering the paper rejects for the single-world view ("keeps outcomes fixed while their causes vary"). It is not inconsistent, because the story-state view does not claim to be counterfactual. But it is where the coupling's predictions are most exposed, and the paper tests it only on additions.
- **The coupling is stipulated, and it carries the story-state side of the test.** It is defined to maximise "agreement on the question at issue", so it is relative to a question. The paper does not say how it behaves when several questions are in play, how it composes, or how a telling counterfactual and an in-story counterfactual interact when they are embedded together. Elsewhere, the claim that "no selection function that gets the counterfactuals right gets the fiction right" (§4) is shown for one model: a single exogenous assignment shared across worlds of assessment. Extending it to world-relative arbitrary selection rests on the short anchoring and defaults arguments in §5. The anchoring argument depends on how "might" counterfactuals are read on a Stalnakerian semantics, and the paper does not address that.
- **The paper is not positioned against its nearest relatives in the conditionals and attitude-semantics literatures.** The answer to Scopelessness transfers von Fintel's homogeneity response to arguments for conditional excluded middle. The literature that debates exactly that choice for Higginbotham-style quantified conditionals goes unengaged, though B&H themselves cite much of it (Williams 2010; Cariani and Goldstein 2018; Mandelkern 2018; also Klinedinst 2011). As a result, the paper never says what it adds beyond the transfer to fiction. Treating the story as a context parameter, with attitudes evaluated on a shifted state, is the informational or domain semantics of Yalcin, Moss and Swanson. It inherits that semantics' known costs: what an attitude toward fiction has as its content, reports of disagreement, and the factivity of *know*. None of this is acknowledged. The causal-model tie-breaking draws on Pearl, Hiddleston and Briggs, none of whom is cited. The CLAUDE.md priority standard is not yet met.
- **There are minor accuracy and attribution slips.**
  - B&H's note 53 says their case is "primarily driven by judgments about embedding patterns" in contrast to "a prior commitment to CEM". There, "embedding patterns" means the attitude-embedding data as a whole. The paper narrows it to the logical patterns.
  - B&H's abductive claim for the theories that yield a single world is at 175, not "182–183".
  - Woodward explicitly allows an epistemicist reading of his indeterminacy (2012: 789), so "so there is no single world" overstates his view.
  - The paper says the serial-fiction literature "concerns change that revises". But McGonigal (2013: 168) discusses cliffhanger additions under "extreme realism", which is the nearest prior discussion of the paper's own sequel case.
  - The abstract states agreement "on every attitude toward a single fiction" without the two bridge principles. The text calls both principles the author's own strengthenings.
  - Blumberg and Strohminger 2025 and Oppy and Raivars 2026 could not be checked against the supplied texts.

## Critical issues for revision

1. Support judgement (B) with more than one ear. At minimum, add the replacement case (a telling that swaps one cause for another) and the irrelevant-detail control from the footnote, and give each view's prediction. If possible, run the elicitation the footnote describes.
2. Define the coupling properly. Say when it applies, how it handles several questions, and how it combines with in-story counterfactuals in one sentence. Then either prove the §4 claim for world-relative arbitrary selection functions, or restate it as a claim about the uniform-tie-breaking model and lean openly on §5. Tighten the anchoring argument so that it does not depend on an unexamined reading of "might".
3. Locate the homogeneity answer within the CEM-versus-homogeneity debate over quantified conditionals, and the attitude semantics within informational semantics. State what is new, and acknowledge the costs that come from attitudes toward fiction having no propositional content.
4. Fix the attribution and page slips listed above, and qualify the abstract's absorption claim.

Why the formal referral: the central argument is formal semantics with a probabilistic core. It uses a probabilistic shift on states and credences, the absorption derivation, coupling values, and a claim about the whole space of Stalnakerian selection functions. The verdict on the reply turns on whether these hold.

Accuracy check (not a reason to pass): every B&H quotation and page reference checked out verbatim, as did Lewis 1978: 42 and Lewis 1976: 311 (page images), Schaffer: 305, von Fintel: 33, MacFarlane: 325, Williams and Woodward: 423–424, and Khoo: 1191 and §4.
