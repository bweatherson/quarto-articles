# Screening report: paper-v6pre.qmd

- **Paper title:** Fiction Is Not Counterfactual
- **Word count:** about 5,800 in the body (about 6,050 with the abstract). The bibliography has 27 entries, 3 of them TV-news pieces.
- **Thesis summary:** Blumberg and Holguín's Principal Reality Principle (PRP) says that fictional truth is Stalnakerian counterfactual truth. The paper argues the PRP fails a "strengthening test": adding a detail that bears on a chancy outcome to a counterfactual's antecedent can change the outcome, but adding the same detail to a story leaves the outcome open, so the PRP mispredicts counterfactuals about the telling. In its place the paper offers a "story-state" semantics. It evaluates bare claims against an information state shifted by the story, treats them as homogeneous, and weights credence by a Reality Principle. This reproduces B&H's attitude and embedding data without a single world, which leaves the single world supported only by a preference for bivalence.
- **Verdict:** NOT READY

## Deal-breakers

**Criteria 1–3: the paper is a reply, and it replies to a secondary part of its target.** B&H are the paper's subject, not the occasion for it. §1 states all three claims as claims about B&H: the test "refutes the PRP as B&H state it", the semantics "predicts B&H's attitude profile", and the last claim concerns "what remains of the single world". §2 summarises B&H, §4 is built to reproduce their tables, and the conclusion scores their "moves". The paper concedes that the positive view is "not new in spirit" (it cites Williams and Woodward's evidentialism, Lewis, and Badura and Berto). So what the paper adds is the test, together with a fragment that passes it. The test targets the PRP. B&H offer the PRP without argument ("We lack the space to argue for THE PRINCIPAL REALITY PRINCIPLE in detail here", 182) and say it is not what drives their case (183 n. 53). A philosopher outside this exchange has no reason to care whether one selection function serves both fiction and counterfactuals, unless they already care about B&H's 2025 proposal.

**Criterion 4, and the "could the author have seen it" test: the decisive judgement is deferred.** The refutation rests on (B) ✗. Yet the paper says its judgements "are predictions, to be tested, not reported data". It moves to a footnote an experimental design that itself names confounds: viewers may assume the makers decided, and a mean of .5 could hide 0s and 1s. (D) ✓ is meant to show that rejecting (B) is not a retreat to a "what the story says" reading, but (D) is also only predicted. The paper also leaves an obvious rival explanation of (B)'s oddness unaddressed. Viewers may hear a special as revealing what had already happened in a fixed fictional past, so that "if the special had been left out" leaves the events untouched. That reading predicts (B) ✗ just as well, and it is what the single-world survivors in §5 (anchoring, path-dependence) encode. The paper calls the result a refutation while, on its own account, the judgement is untested and confounded. The author has seen this gap and deferred it.

**Criterion 6: the literature engagement is too thin for what the paper claims.** Only 24 entries are scholarly. The paper relies on several literatures it does not acknowledge:

- The Reality Principle debate, which is what the title addresses: Currie 1990, Phillips 1999, Friend 2017 and Franzén 2021, and above all Woodward 2011a. Woodward combines conditional excluded middle (CEM) with a Reality Principle to make fictions complete, which makes it the nearest antecedent both of the PRP and of the supervaluational alternative.
- Work on conditionals that weighs CEM against homogeneity or supervaluation for Higginbotham's data: Williams 2010, Klinedinst 2011, Cariani and Goldstein 2018, Mandelkern 2018. The paper's answer to B&H's embedding arguments is this move carried over to fiction.
- Credence in counterfactuals without CEM facts: Edgington 2008, Moss 2013, Schultheis 2023.
- Information-sensitive semantics for attitudes (Yalcin 2007), the evident template for clause 6.
- Causal-model semantics (Hiddleston 2005, Briggs 2012), the source of §3's tie-breaking.

Without these, a reader cannot tell which parts of §4 are new.

## Key strengths

- **The strengthening test is a real idea.** It moves the dispute from attitudes toward a single fiction to comparisons between two tellings. Given its premises, the derivation in §3 that the PRP predicts (B) ✓ is clean.
- **The absorption result shows exactly why B&H's data cannot settle the matter.** Given a link between chance and selection, and the openness principle, credence in a fictional claim is credence imaged on the story's content (as in Lewis 1976). So every attitude toward a single fiction comes out the same on both views. The parallel with counterfactuals in B&H's §9 follows on any view that shifts the information state.
- **§5 sorts the surviving single-world views precisely.** Its argument that anchoring cannot be a general rule for counterfactuals is good. That argument is the "tossed, … in a gust, it might have landed tails" sentence.

## Key weaknesses

- **The story state passes the test only through a bespoke clause.** Clause 4 resolves "differently", "still" and "the other way" by mapping each candidate world to its nearest counterpart in the other telling, and a note adds a maximal coupling for details that shift chances. Counterfactuals do not use a rule like this for comparing tellings. That is the charge §5 brings against the single-world survivors, yet §5's ledger leaves the clause out of the story state's costs. The clause's own advertised prediction also counts against it. The view supports "If the writers had left the special out, there would still have been a gust" but not "…there would have been a gust", which breaks the step from "would still have been p" to "would have been p".
- **The costs are counted differently for the two sides.** To get (A), the story state evaluates counterfactuals "on a Stalnakerian semantics … with chances guiding credence about selection". So it accepts arbitrary, unknowable selection facts for counterfactuals. §5 then rejects the single world as "bought with arbitrary and unknowable facts that no judgement tracks". The paper owes one of two things: a counterfactual semantics without CEM that still gives (A) its .5, or an admission that it pays the main cost it charges B&H.
- **The title and conclusion claim more than the paper shows.** §6 concedes that the test "tells against the Stalnakerian identification, not against Lewis's", so the general thesis in the title is not shown. §5 concedes that both sides "predict the attitudes, the embedding patterns and the strengthening judgements". That parity defeats B&H's claim that there "must be" facts, since a countermodel is enough. It does not support the conclusion's first-order claim that "Nothing makes it true that Holmes had an odd number of hairs."
- **Accuracy (Criterion 7).** The paper says Khoo 2026 replies to B&H "by contesting their data", and that "unlike … Khoo" it accepts their judgements. Khoo 2026 is a paper about reactive predicates. It cites B&H once, with approval, for a selection-function semantics for fiction operators (n. 28). Its credence judgements concern explicit "in the fiction of *The Shining*" sentences, which B&H expressly set aside (153). Khoo would not accept that attribution. A smaller point: B&H's challenge to trivalence (171) is aimed at trivalent views built to give the attitude patterns, not at "any trivalent view". I checked the quotations from B&H, Lewis, Williams and Woodward, Schaffer, MacFarlane and von Fintel against the source texts, and they are accurate. I could not check Oppy and Raivars or Blumberg and Strohminger.
