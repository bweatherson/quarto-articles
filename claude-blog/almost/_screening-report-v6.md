# Screening Report: "Almost"

**Paper title:** Almost
**Word count:** c. 8,400 (body) plus a 320-word abstract; 82 bibliography entries.

**Thesis summary.** For event predicates, "almost φ" is true iff φ is false, a process whose completion would constitute φ was under way, and some state on its actual course admits a perturbation within the process's moment-to-moment noise ("local variation") whose continuation (i) stays within local variation of the actual course for as long as that course lasts and (ii) ends in φ with substantial probability. "Could easily have φ'd" is (ii) applied at the process's start with (i) dropped; the two come apart at the lottery. Scalar and stative uses reduce to a directed scalar semantics whose direction a salient process can reverse at interior points but not endpoints.

**Decision:** DESK REJECT

## Deal-Breakers

**Criterion 4 (unanswered objection the author could have seen).** The account's central primitive, local variation, is applied inconsistently across the paper's own cases, and the inconsistency is not a matter of detail: the paper's headline verdicts pull in opposite directions on the same question. In §6.3, Crane's near-miss is weak because "a jam not forming" is "not the moment-to-moment variation of a journey"; §5.2 restates this as a commitment ("a few minutes' difference in how a jam clears is local variation for a journey and ... a jam not forming is not"). In §5.1, Everest (a)–(c) are good near-misses because one may "perturb the state at which the climb stopped by a nudge within local variation, the illness holding off a day, the weather holding." An illness not striking and weather not turning are exogenous shocks of exactly the jam's kind. Applied consistently, either Crane's regret is as fitting as Tees's or "I almost climbed Everest, but I fell ill 200m from the summit" is not a good near-miss; the paper needs both verdicts and cannot have them. The same slippage recurs in the definition itself: §4.1 says local variation is "the moment-to-moment noise of the process and not the accumulated variation between one run and the next ... the difference between the two is what the clause turns on," yet §4.2 sets a jump's local variation as the change in run-up speed "that one attempt shows against the next," and §5.3 sets "a shooter's variability" as the local variation of her shots. Run-to-run variability is forbidden where it would spoil a verdict (the die, Crane) and admitted where it is needed (jump, shooter). §5.2 correctly identifies local variation as "where a critic should press"; the paper then presses there itself and does not notice.

## Key Strengths

- The (i)+(ii) structure makes a genuine prediction the nearest competitor does not. Gerstenberg and Tenenbaum's noisy re-simulation model gives the loaded die "almost showed six" at roughly 0.8; Kahneman and Varey's subjects reject the sentence; condition (i) blocks the launch perturbation because "its continuation leaves the actual tumble at once" while the teetering die passes (§4.2). This is a real divergence from the psychological model the paper builds on, not a redescription of it.
- The separation of "almost" from "could easily have" is clean and has a philosophical payoff: Sainsbury's "small differences in relevant parameters" gloss on safety is diagnosed as (ii) without (i), which is why it handles the lottery and would be wrong for "almost" (§6.1). The two-relations point about luck (lottery wins vs. lucky escapes, §6.1) is the best thing in §6.
- The abandoned-course reading of (ii) yields a non-arbitrary gradient across Everest (a)–(c), and the paper states a test that would refute its "under way" requirement ("I almost climbed Everest" vs. "I almost went to Everest, but I couldn't raise the money," §5.1). Committing to a falsifier is more than most papers in this literature do.

## Key Weaknesses

- **The metric is advertised but never used.** The paper's claim to improve on Lewis and Kvart is that it supplies "a supremum norm in the process's own units" (§4.1). No case computes or estimates a distance. Every verdict is reached by two verbal judgments: does the continuation "run beside" the actual course or "leave at once" (smooth vs. chaotic dynamics), and is the required perturbation "a nudge" or "a different state" (§4.2 on the stumble; §4.2 on the runner "without acceleration"). Taken literally, (i) is doubtful even in the paradigm case: a velocity nudge at release accumulates into a position gap at the moment the actual ball stops, and that gap (the distance to the target) is not per-moment position noise "of the size a hand's tremor or a bounce introduces." The account passes its own cases only if the units are chosen after the fact.
- **The paper's concessions undercut the empirical case it opens with.** §2.3 introduces the Doan et al. scratch-card data as "the signature of two relations"; §4.2 then declares the card "not a case in which 'almost' has a determinate value" and says "the account does not predict those numbers." The promissory note is withdrawn rather than redeemed. Likewise the pot/die divergence in Kahneman and Varey is conceded as "a cost" with no explanation (§4.2). Together these mean the account fits the assembled data only after the data it cannot fit are reclassified.
- **The "under way"/constitutive criterion is decided by verdict.** Driving to the airport is part of catching a flight; driving around looking for a victim is not part of robbing him (§5.2). The asymmetry is asserted from what the predicates "name," and Everest (d) (base camp inaccessible) is put on the "no climb was under way" side without argument, although the trek in stands to the summit as the drive stands to the gate. The reply that a critic "should say what the predicate names instead" shifts the burden the account was meant to carry.
- **§6.2 misdescribes the law it claims to diagnose.** The paper says a proximity test "has, on its own terms, no purchase" on the pickpocket who reaches into an empty pocket, and that a jurisdiction convicting him "has chosen the second test." Proximity-era courts routinely convicted in exactly this case (factual impossibility was no defence: *People v. Moran*, *R v Ring*). A generalist reader with legal background will lose confidence in the section, and §6 is where the paper's generalist appeal lives: §6.1's safety point is conceded to be already standard, leaving luck and regret to carry it.
- **The scalar clause adds little.** The paper does not derive canonical direction (§4.1, conceded), and its distinctive claim, that endpoints cannot be overridden, is explained by "an endpoint has one side," which the scalar picture already says; the "glass being filled through ten per cent" example is one no theory calls "almost full."

## Note on the decision

The positive thesis is statable in one sentence and is not table-stakes; the die and wall verdicts do separate it from the resemblance, scalar, McKenzie–Newkirk and Gerstenberg–Tenenbaum pictures. But the paper's contribution is "a nearby-worlds account with a metric," and the metric is not delivered: local variation is set case by case, and the jam/illness contradiction and the moment-to-moment/run-to-run contradiction sit in the paper's own text. These are flaws a careful author would have found on re-reading §4.1 against §5.1, §5.3 and §6.3, and fixing them forces a choice (exogenous shocks in or out of local variation) that changes headline verdicts. That is work the author must do before review, not a question for referees. Length and citations are not at issue. The paper does not fall squarely enough in the editor's formal areas to warrant EVALUATE: the account is stated verbally and no formal apparatus is actually deployed.
