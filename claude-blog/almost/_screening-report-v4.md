# Screening Report: "Almost"

**Paper title:** Almost
**Word count:** c. 6,500 body words plus abstract; 80-entry bibliography (51 keys cited in the body).

**Thesis summary.** For event prejacents, "almost φ" is true iff φ is false and a process of the kind φ names was under way and reached a *last open state* (the last state from which φ was reachable under the process's own dynamics and normal variation) at which the propensity for φ was substantial. For scalar and stative prejacents, "almost φ" is true iff the value lies within a contextual unit of φ on the side from which the scale is canonically traversed, a direction a salient contrary process can override at interior points but not at endpoints. The paper then argues that "almost" and "could easily have" are distinct relations, and that safety uses the second while luck-talk, the law of attempts, and near-miss regret use the first.

**Decision: DESK REJECT**

## Deal-Breakers

**Criterion 4 (obvious unanswered objection), compounded by Criterion 1.** The load-bearing notion, the *last open state*, is not defined in a way that survives the paper's own cases, and the author could see this: §4.1 hands the definition to "the grain of the best model of processes of that kind," and then §4.3 chooses the grain case by case to get the verdict wanted. With a fine grain the last open state is the boundary of the reachable region, where the propensity for φ is by construction small; with a coarse grain (takeoff, release, last bounce, final stride) the propensity is just the ordinary conditional chance of φ at that decision point. Neither reading yields the paper's verdicts uniformly, and the paper does not confront the choice. The scalar clause's "derived" direction rule is a restatement of what it claims to derive. §6 is a set of one-paragraph gestures. Each of these is visible before submission.

## Key Strengths

- **Problem-driven, statable thesis.** The paper starts from a puzzle that exists independently of any recent exchange ("what is it for something to almost happen?") and states its answer in two clauses (§4.1). The framing of the relevance for relief, guilt, attempts, and safety (§1) is a genuine attempt at generalist appeal, and the thesis is a claim of the form "X is Y," not "X can be Y."
- **The "almost"/"could easily have" distinction (§6.1) is the paper's best idea.** Using Doan, Denison and Friedman's finding that "almost" tracks possibility while "easily could have" tracks probability, and reading the raffle as the case where they come apart, is a clean point, and the observation that Sainsbury's "small differences in relevant parameters" gloss on safety is a third relation, distinct from both resemblance and approach, is sharp.
- **The wall case (§2.4, §4.1) is the right test.** Equal resting distances with unequal near-miss judgments is exactly what a distance measure cannot see, and the paper is right that McKenzie and Newkirk's condition-counting cannot see it either (§3).
- **Empirical grounding.** The paper builds on Kahneman–Varey, Gerstenberg–Tenenbaum, Covey–Zhang and Doan et al. rather than on intuitions alone, and is candid (§1) that the core ideas come from there.

## Key Weaknesses

- **"Last open state" is fixed ad hoc, and the paper's verdicts are inconsistent with one another.** In the die case (§4.3) the grain is fine: the last open state is "its last bounce," and the loaded die's 0.8 is dismissed as "the propensity at the start of the roll, which is not the last open state." In the raffle (§4.3) the grain is coarse: "if a hand went into the hat and came out with one, no state of the drawing carried a propensity for your ticket specifically above one in ten." But by the paper's own definition the hand's closing on a ticket has late states at which the propensity for each ticket is concentrating toward 0 or 1, so the ticket the fingers brushed *was* almost drawn. The raffle reasoning commits exactly the start-of-process error the die paragraph diagnoses. The account cannot deliver "you did not almost win" for the generic raffle; at most it delivers "probably not, and you cannot tell." That matters because the raffle is "the case that will matter most" (§2.3) and the hinge of §6.1. The process/preparation boundary is applied with the same freedom: "I almost climbed Everest, but I couldn't raise the money" (§5.1) is rescued as loose talk for "I almost set out," felicitous "in proportion as the preparation came close and the summit is a natural continuation of setting out," while Mark, who missed registration by a day and was the strongest player entered (§2.3, §4.3), is treated as flatly not having almost won and his 97 per cent rejection as confirming data. As applied, the account predicts Mark should pattern with Everest (f). The paper does not notice.
- **The Tees/Crane verdict (§6.3) is justified by outcome distance, the very thing the account rejects.** Tees's last open state is said to carry a substantial propensity "since he missed it by five minutes." But both men sat in the same jam; the last open state for each is the last moment a normal-variation ride could still make it, and at that boundary the propensity is a threshold quantity for both. Nothing in the account's machinery favours Tees; the paper simply reads the margin off the outcome. The same slippage appears in the race (§4.3): "the last open state of a race is the final stride" is asserted, and a final stride's noise does not overturn a losing margin, so the closing runner's "high propensity for winning" is not computed from the stated clause.
- **The account's own grading claim is contradicted by the data it claims to predict.** §4.1: "the higher the propensity at the last open state, the better the near-miss." §4.3 on the scratch cards: ratings "rising sharply with possibility and little with probability" are "what the account predicts," because "its size mattered less than its being positive." Those are opposite claims. Likewise "John almost won the pot" (§4.3): the account says it is "no better and no worse" than the die sentence, Kahneman and Varey's subjects said it was better, and the paper writes "I take the difference to be pragmatic." When the account and the data diverge, the paper reclassifies the data.
- **The scalar direction rule is stipulated, not derived (§4.2).** The clause builds in "the side from which the scale is canonically traversed" (a per-scale orientation: counting up, ageing up, time forward) plus an override for interior points. Endpoint immunity is trivial: an endpoint has one side, so no process can put ten per cent within a unit of "full." The claim that the scalar picture must "stipulate which scales are oriented and let context choose for the rest" while the account "derives it from one rule" describes the same amount of stipulation twice. The relevant scale-structure literature (Kennedy and McNally 2005; Rotstein and Winter 2004), where "almost" is already a diagnostic for closed scales, is absent; Ziegeler's counterfactual account is in the bibliography but never engaged.
- **§6 does not earn its place.** Luck, attempts, and regret each get one paragraph. The attempts section maps Holmes's proximity test onto "almost" and the substantial-step test onto "could easily" and then stops; Yaffe and Duff are named, not engaged. The luck section asserts two relations from Teigen's questionnaire data. The regret section depends on the Tees verdict, which the account does not deliver. A generalist reader is promised consequences for "the parts of philosophy that trade in nearby possibility" (§1) and receives a table of correspondences. The paper concedes (§6.1) that the safety point "is not news."

The decision follows from the calibration notes: a clever idea with an objection the author could see, raised by the paper's own examples and handed to "the best model of processes of that kind," is not ready. A resubmission that fixed the grain of the last open state, ran the raffle, Tees and race cases through the stated clause rather than around it, and developed one of the §6 applications properly would be a different paper and worth seeing.
