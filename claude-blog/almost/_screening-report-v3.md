# Screening report: "Almost"

**Paper title:** Almost
**Word count:** c. 6,400 (excluding references); 48 reference entries
**Thesis:** "Almost φ" is true when a directed path toward φ stopped short of it by a small margin. For eventive prejacents the path is an actual token process that (i) carried a high propensity for φ late in its course and (ii) lacked, at the point it stopped, only a perturbation that is small relative to the process's own normal variation; for scalar and stative prejacents the path is the canonical traversal of a scale, reversible by a salient process only at interior points. The payoff is that "almost" (propensity-closeness) and "could easily have" (disposition-closeness) are distinct relations that the idiom of close possible worlds conflates, with consequences for safety, luck, criminal attempts and near-miss regret.

**Decision: DESK REJECT**

This is not a philosophy-of-language paper in the editor-in-chief's areas: the central argument is not formal (no probability axioms, no formal semantics with a probabilistic core; "propensity" is used informally throughout), so the EVALUATE trigger is not met. It would go to a language area editor if it went anywhere. It should not, for the reasons below, though it is closer to the bar than most submissions and a revised version could be a strong paper.

## Deal-Breakers

**Criterion 4 (unanswered objections the author could have seen).** The account's verdicts depend on how the token process is individuated, where it starts, and at what grain its "propensity" is evaluated. The paper says these are fixed by "the description the prejacent's verb selects" and "the best predictive model of that kind of process" (Statement, para 3), but its own cases show the settings being chosen to fit the verdict:

- *Mark's tournament vs Everest (f).* Mark, who missed registration by a day, "fails (i): no process ever had a state with a propensity for Mark's winning" (Eventive cases, "Static closeness"). But "I almost climbed Everest, but I couldn't raise the money" is granted a process, "the expedition, individuated by 'climb Everest,' with states that carried some propensity for the summit" (Objections, "At a distance"). Nothing in the account says why the funding stage belongs to the climb but the registration stage does not belong to the tournament. If the expedition includes its preparation, Mark almost won; if it does not, (a)–(f) are not near-misses of climbing. The two passages are inconsistent and the paper does not notice.
- *John's pot vs the die.* "John almost won the pot," where the pot turned on that roll, is said to be better than "the die almost showed six" because John's evening "was a process with states, and the state just before the last roll carried whatever propensity the roll gave it" (same paragraph). But the die's own roll also has a state just before it, carrying the same 0.8; and both processes end at the same instant with the same tumbling. The account assigns the same (i)-profile to both and the paper reads off different verdicts.
- *Ballistic processes and "late."* For the first jumper, "the first jump, until it landed, carried a high propensity for clearing six metres." At the paper's own grain for throws (a physics engine with the process's noise), a jump in flight has a settled landing point; the high propensity was at the board, before takeoff. The same applies to Gerstenberg and Tenenbaum's ball, whose "minimum additional impulse" is a perturbation to the throw, not to the state where the ball stopped, as (ii) requires. The account as stated (propensity "late," perturbation "at the point where the process stopped") does not fit the empirical model it claims as support.

**Criterion 4 again: the headline consequence rests on a false claim.** The safety paragraph's "news" is that Sainsbury's gloss on "could easily" ("small differences in relevant parameters" of the actual mechanism) is propensity-closeness and "does not handle the lottery, where no parameter of the mechanism was near the value that would have printed your number." That is false for any physical or pseudo-random lottery: a small change in the ball-mixing state or the PRNG seed produces any number, including yours. Sainsbury's gloss delivers the lottery verdict directly; it is margin-closeness (ii) without approach (i), not "propensity-closeness." Without this claim the safety application reduces to "safety uses 'could easily,'" which the paper itself calls "not news."

**Criterion 1/4: selective use of intuition on the central case.** The scratch card is the paper's test case for the almost/could-easily distinction. The paper concedes "the feeling is real and is exploited by design" (Evidence, "Static closeness") and then declares "you did not almost win, however close the strings." Ordinary speakers say "I almost won" of exactly this card. The paper treats speaker verdicts on Mark, the tethered climber and the altered die as data, and speaker verdicts on the scratch card as error, with no error theory. Doan et al. are cited for the possible/impossible boundary, but a one-digit-off card is on the possible side of that boundary; the paper does not report whether their subjects' "almost" ratings for such cards were low, which is what the account needs.

## Key Strengths

- The question is genuinely broad and the framing is problem-first: "What is it for something to almost happen?" would be worth asking with no recent literature.
- The empirical base is unusually good for a semantics paper: Kahneman–Varey, Covey–Zhang, Gerstenberg–Tenenbaum and Doan et al. are used as data, not decoration, and the five-part evidence section (direction, effort, static closeness, equal distances, impossibility) is a real contribution to organizing the phenomena.
- The scalar clause's prediction that direction is reversible exactly at interior points and never at endpoints is crisp and, as far as it goes, correct on the cases given.
- The stative/eventive pair ("the ball is almost in the hole" / "the ball almost went in") is a clean argument that two clauses are needed.

## Key Weaknesses

- The eventive clause is underspecified where it matters: "late," "high," "the process," "its own normal variation," and "a unit at the contextual granularity" are all left to be set case by case, and the cases above show them being set after the verdict.
- The paper's contrast between propensity and disposition does not survive its own treatment of the expectation effect: shooter variance (a dispositional property of the shooter, learned from prior performance) is let into the model of the shot, while the runner's known strong finish is kept out of the model of the race. See Notes §5.
- The "could easily have" side of the distinction is glossed three ways (probability; small change to the world; resemblance) and the lottery supports only the resemblance gloss, which is the one the paper accuses others of running together with "almost."
- Two of the three applications (luck, regret) are thin. The luck paragraph misapplies the modal account (see Notes §3); the regret section's foil ("one might conclude that near-miss regret is a bias") is not attributed to anyone.
- Two sections ("Two readings, three places"; "'Almost' and 'approximately'") are lexical-semantics housekeeping for a Linguistics & Philosophy readership and do no work for the thesis.

# Notes for the author

## 1. The objection a good referee will press hardest

**Your account has a free parameter, process individuation and grain, and you set it by hand for each case.** State the objection in full: (i) and (ii) are only evaluable once we know which token process the prejacent "selects," where that process begins, at what grain its states are described, and what counts as its "normal variation." You say these are fixed by the verb and by "the best predictive model of that kind of process." But (a) Mark's tournament and Everest-(f) are structurally identical (an agent intends X, a precondition of X fails before X is under way) and you give one a process and deny the other one; (b) John's pot and the die are the same physical process ending at the same instant, and you give them different propensity profiles; (c) at the physics-engine grain you endorse for throws, the first jumper's in-flight state has no high propensity for six metres, so either "late" means "at the last point where the process's noise could still act" (in which case say so, and then the plain loaded die's last bounce is such a point and you owe us a reason its propensity for six was low) or the verdict is asserted; (d) at a coarse grain (the die is a 0.8 device until it stops) the plain loaded die satisfies (i) better than the ball-by-ball lottery at 0.1, and you say the reverse. A referee will conclude that the account is a schema whose instances are chosen to match intuitions, which is the reference-class problem for propensities in a new coat.

**Do you have the materials to answer it?** Partly. The right reply is that the process type is fixed by the verb's aspectual structure together with a notion of *initiation*: the process begins when the agent or mechanism starts doing the thing the verb names (the run-up, the roll, the drawing, the climb), and preparation is not part of it. That would rule Mark out and Everest-(d)–(f) out with him, contradicting your acceptance of McKenzie and Newkirk's list; you would then need to say (d)–(f) are near-misses only derivatively (via deciding/planning), as you already say for (g). That is a coherent position, but it is not the one in the paper. For the die/pot pair, you need a principled reason the grain differs, and I do not think you have one; better to concede the two are equal on the account and treat Kahneman–Varey's difference as pragmatic. For the ballistic cases you need to restate (i) and (ii) so that both are evaluated at the last state from which the process's own noise could have reached φ, and then check that the wall still comes out (it does: the impulse is to the throw, which is that state).

## 2. Problem paper or literature paper?

Problem paper, and clearly so. The first paragraph poses the question without reference to anyone; paragraphs 2–3 use the two standard pictures as foils, not subjects; the evidence section is organized by phenomenon. The literature is the occasion. Two places pull the other way: "Two readings, three places" (a contribution to the Morgan–McCawley–von Stechow decomposition debate that the thesis does not need) and "'Almost' and 'approximately'" (a response to Morzycki and Lasersohn). The Objections section's first subsection is a reply to McKenzie and Newkirk, but that is fair, since they are the nearest rival and the wall is a genuine differentiator.

## 3. Generalist appeal: does the final section earn it?

The question earns it; the final section only partly does. Ranking:

1. **Attempts** is the best application. The mapping (proximity test = "almost" = propensity; substantial-step = commitment; penal lottery = disposition) is clean, the Rizzo and empty-pocket cases are handled correctly, and "the disagreement about the impossible attempt is about 'tried' and not about 'almost'" is a genuine clarification. It is also the most modest.
2. **Safety / "could easily"** is the most ambitious and the least sound. The "almost vs could easily" result *as stated* has three problems. (a) Your intro says "with a ticket one digit off you could easily have won." Many speakers deny this of a one-in-a-million ticket, and Doan et al., whom you cite in the same breath, found "easily could have" tracking probability linearly, which predicts a *low* "easily could have" rating for a million-to-one ticket. Your own evidence cuts against your example. (b) The lottery supports the resemblance gloss on "could easily" (a world in which your number was printed resembles the actual one as much as any lottery-alternative world), not the probability gloss; you use both. (c) The Sainsbury claim is false (see Deal-Breakers). What survives is: safety needs a relation that "almost" is not. That is true and worth a paragraph, not a section.
3. **Luck and regret** are weakest. The luck paragraph is wrong as stated: the modal account holds "the relevant initial conditions" fixed, and the tether is an initial condition, so the modal account says the caught climber was *not* lucky; "he may have been lucky in the first [sense], if the tether might easily not have been there" imports a resemblance ordering the modal account explicitly excludes. Also, identifying the lucky-escape sense with "almost" is too quick: the pedestrian who almost gets hit but dives clear through skill had a near-miss and was not lucky. Luck needs the stopping-short to be outside the agent's control; "almost" does not. The regret section's claim that Tees's regret has as object "one's course of action" slips: Tees's course of action was identical to Crane's; what differed was the flight. "Course of events" is the right phrase and you use both. And the last sentence ("the agent-regret Williams describes has ... the same object as the guilt of the driver who was not so lucky") is a substantive claim in moral psychology asserted in a subordinate clause; Williams's point is precisely that outcome enters the object.

## 4. Testing the account case by case

- **Two jumpers.** Verdict right; mechanism wrong at the stated grain (see §1(c)). Also, as athletics, the second jumper's "5.90" is not a jump mark (sliding after touchdown does not move the mark), so the example is confusing; use a jumper who fouls or who lands at 5.90 off a bad takeoff.
- **The wall.** Right, and this is your best case against both rivals. But the perturbation is to the throw, so restate (ii).
- **Teetering die vs plain roll.** Teetering: right. Plain roll: verdict secured by asserting that propensity "fell to zero as it tumbled," which is true of every die that lands on two, teetering or not. What you need is that no late state carried a high propensity, and at the physics grain with noise the last bounce of an ordinary roll carries *some* propensity for adjacent faces; you give no reason it is low. And at the coarse grain the loaded die is a better near-miss than the ball-by-ball lottery. Grain chosen after the fact.
- **Wire-to-wire leader caught on the line.** Right: high propensity at the last stride. But this is the case that shows "late" must mean "at the last open state," which you should say.
- **Scratch card vs ball-by-ball draw.** Internally consistent, and the "drawn, not revealed" prediction is a real, testable claim. But (a) it convicts ordinary usage of error without an error theory; (b) Doan's subjects were judging scratch cards and you do not report their absolute "almost" ratings, which on your view should be near floor for possible near-miss cards; (c) 0.1 after five balls is granted as satisfying (i) "modestly," while 0.8 before a die roll is not "late." You need a threshold story that handles both.
- **"Almost every plant is dry."** Right: count scale, endpoint "all," Penka's moist plants come out false. Fine.
- **"Almost exactly three."** Right, and neat: "exactly" turns the prejacent into the endpoint of a distance scale and the halo follows. Keep.
- **Glass being filled, "almost empty" at 10%.** Right (endpoint; near side is interior). But the paired example, "a room filling through ninety-seven 'almost empty,'" tests nothing: 97 is not within a unit of 0 on any granularity, so the sentence is false for scalar reasons, not because of endpoint non-reversal. The datum you want is "a room filling through three is still almost empty," which is true and supports you.
- **Room emptying through 103 ("almost 100 left").** Right, though arguably "left/remaining" already supplies a downward canonical scale, so this may not be an override at all. Use "almost 100 people are here" said of a room emptying through 103 for a genuine override case.
- **Everest (a)–(g).** The gradient is predicted, but (ii) "may hold throughout, since each impediment is one event of a kind that such projects ordinarily survive or do not" is hand-waving: "raise the money" is not a small perturbation relative to an expedition's normal variation. More importantly, granting (d)–(f) any process at all lets Mark in (§1). The "derivative near-miss" treatment you give (g) should probably extend to (d)–(f).
- **Crane and Tees.** Verdict right on the account and matches intuition. Note that the propensity you attribute to Tees ("substantial until five minutes before he arrived") presupposes taxi-ride noise of the order of five minutes over a thirty-minute-late journey; say so. The "course of action" slip is noted in §3.

## 5. The expectation effect

It does not hold up, for three reasons.

(a) You define propensity as "the probability that the process reaches φ from that state under those dynamics *with that noise*" and then say the noise scale "enters (ii) and not (i)." By your definition it enters both: a rimmed-out shot's late state has a higher propensity for going in under large noise than under small. The direction is the same, so the prediction survives, but the sentence is false.

(b) Shooter variance is a dispositional property of the shooter, learned from prior performance, and you let it into "the best predictive model" of the shot. The runner "known to have a strong finish" who shows nothing on the day is a dispositional property of the runner, learned from prior performance, and you keep it out of the model of the race. The best predictive model of a race run by a known strong finisher includes late acceleration; on your own criterion that runner's late state carries a high propensity even if he "showed no evidence of talent on that particular occasion," and "almost won" comes out apt, contra Kahneman–Varey and contra you. You need a principled line between dispositional facts that are "part of the dynamics" and ones that are "reputation," and the paper does not draw it.

(c) Gerstenberg and Tenenbaum manipulated *observed practice performance*, which is evidence about variance but also, directly, an expectation manipulation. Your account "predicts the effect, and semantically" only on the assumption that subjects were tracking actual variance rather than expectation. Their design cannot distinguish these; you should say the account is *compatible* with the effect and predicts it under that assumption, and propose the experiment that would separate them (hold practice fixed, vary the shooter's actual dispersion).

This bears on the consequences: the "propensity not disposition" contrast that drives the whole final section is only as clean as the line in (b), and that line is not drawn.

## 6. Claims that are false, overstated or unsupported

- "Neither standard picture predicts [that endpoints never reverse]" (Direction). Overstated. A scalar theorist with an entailment scale for "all/every" has no alternatives beyond the endpoint, so non-reversal is trivial; Nouwen's contextual orientation for time plus the absence of points beyond an endpoint gives your exact pattern.
- "no parameter of the mechanism was near the value that would have printed your number" (Consequences, safety). False for any chaotic or PRNG lottery.
- "with a ticket one digit off you could easily have won" (Intro). Contested, and in tension with Doan's linear-probability finding for "easily could have."
- "the noise scale ... enters (ii) and not (i)" (Objections, Expectation). Contradicts your definition of propensity.
- "The first jump, until it landed, carried a high propensity for clearing six metres" (Eventive cases). False at your own grain for ballistic processes.
- "the tethered climber ... may have been lucky in the first [modal] sense, if the tether might easily not have been there" (Consequences, luck). Misapplies the modal account, which fixes initial conditions.
- "Doan and colleagues' subjects, judging scratch cards, are judging a process of the first kind" (Scratch card). Implies their "almost" ratings should be near floor for possible near-miss cards; you do not report them.
- "a room filling through ninety-seven 'almost empty'" (Evidence, Directed). Not an endpoint test; false for scalar reasons.
- "All of these are features of the process type ... not of anyone's expectations" (Statement). "Best predictive model" is an epistemic notion; either say "the true dynamics" and accept the epistemology, or drop the contrast.
- "the agent-regret Williams describes has ... the same object as the guilt of the driver who was not so lucky" (Regret). Asserted; Williams denies it.
- Citation glitches: "Sadock observed ... P. Amaral and Del Prete (2010)" is mis-nested; the case-law citations render as quoted strings; Dowty "7:242" is a volume/page artifact.

## 7. What to cut or tighten

- Cut "Two readings, three places" or reduce to a footnote: it takes a stand on nothing, its "prediction to test" is not tested, and it is a detour into a decomposition debate the thesis does not touch.
- Cut "'Almost' and 'approximately'" to two sentences inside the Statement (the "almost exactly" point is the only thing in it that does work).
- Compress the Evidence section by a third: the five headings are right, but each is over-illustrated.
- Rewrite the Statement to fix "late," "high," process initiation, and grain, *before* the cases. The cases should then be run mechanically from the stated account; at present several are argued backward from the verdict.
- Drop the luck paragraph or fix it (see §3); it is one paragraph and it contains an error.
- In Consequences, keep Attempts in full, reduce Safety to the surviving point (safety needs a relation "almost" is not, and the defender who glosses it via the mechanism's parameters is using margin without approach), and cut Regret to the Crane/Tees paragraph.
- Address the scratch card head-on: either supply an error theory for "I almost won" (with Doan's absolute ratings as evidence) or weaken the claim to "the near-miss is not one the process supports," which is what the slot-machine paragraph in fact says.
