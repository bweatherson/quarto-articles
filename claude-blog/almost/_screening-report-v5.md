# Screening Report

**Paper title:** Almost
**Word count:** ~7,600 words (body) plus abstract. Bibliography: 82 entries, 52 cited in text; all keys resolve.
**Thesis:** For event predicates, "almost φ" is true iff a process of the kind φ names was under way and some course of that process ending in φ stays within the process's normal variation of the actual course *throughout* (a supremum-norm distance on courses), not merely at its end. This is claimed to beat the resemblance picture, the scalar picture, and the McKenzie–Newkirk hybrid on the wall, two-jumper and loaded-die data, and to separate "almost" (close throughout) from "could easily have" (close at the start), with consequences for safety, luck, attempts and regret.

**Decision:** DESK REJECT

## Deal-Breakers

**Criterion 4: the account's central device is not fixed well enough to deliver the paper's own verdicts, and the paper sees this and defers it.** The one thing the paper claims to add to Lewis, Kvart and Gerstenberg–Tenenbaum is the metric. §4.1 defines distance as "the largest deviation between [two courses] at any point, measured in units of normal variation," and then glosses normal variation as *input* noise: "run-up speed and takeoff angle for a jump, launch velocity and spin for a die, the duration of a traffic jam for a journey." A supremum norm, however, needs a distance on the *state of the process at every time*, and the paper never says what one unit of normal variation of a mid-tumble die orientation, or of a runner's position with 300m to go, is. The two die verdicts in §4.2 then require incompatible answers. The loaded die is denied a near-miss because a six-course "left the actual tumble at the launch and never returned," which needs mid-tumble orientation differences to count as large, even though across normal rolls a die at its third bounce occupies every orientation. The teetering die is granted one because the six-course "departs from the actual course only at the end, by a variation of the kind the process exhibits"; but on the paper's own gloss the die's variation is "in its launch," and by the paper's own chaos argument no launch perturbation yields a same-tumble-different-topple course. The teetering verdict needs perturbations applied throughout; the loaded-die verdict needs them confined to the launch. §5.2 circles this, says "the account is hostage to [normal variation], and that is where a critic should press," and stops. That is the paper raising its decisive problem and leaving it to the reader.

## Key Strengths

- The separation of "almost" (a φ-course close throughout) from "could easily have" (a φ-course close at the start) is a real, one-sentence distinction, and §6.1's observation that Sainsbury's "small differences in relevant parameters" gloss on easy possibility is a start-of-process relation rather than a course relation is a sharp point that safety theorists would want to see made.
- The loaded die (§4.2) is the one case where the account demonstrably outruns Gerstenberg and Tenenbaum's minimal-perturbation model, which the paper otherwise concedes it is formalizing: a minimal launch perturbation to a chaotic process is small on their measure and large on the paper's, and Kahneman and Varey's 77% rejection is on the paper's side.
- The scalar clause's override rule (§4.1), on which a salient process can reverse direction at interior points but not at endpoints, is a tidy generalization of the Sadock, Nouwen and Amaral–Del Prete data that the scalar picture had left to "contextual choice."
- §6.3 turns Tees/Crane into a fittingness claim with a truth-maker in the world, which is the kind of consequence a reader outside semantics can use.

## Key Weaknesses

- **The stated metric cannot produce the Everest gradient the paper claims for it.** §5.1 says the summit-course is further from (c) than from (a) because in (c) it "ends there only if a great deal else went right as well." That is accumulation of many individually normal deviations. A supremum norm takes the largest single deviation and does not accumulate. Either each of the "great deal else" is within a unit, in which case (c) is as good a near-miss as (a), or one is not, and the paper should say which. The paper explains its own gradient with a metric it has not stated.
- **The unit is set by the verdict.** Tees/Crane (§6.3) turns on a five-minute-shorter jam being "within a unit" and a jam-that-did-not-happen not being. Jams ordinarily vary by more than thirty minutes, so on the paper's definition ("the noise that processes of that kind ordinarily exhibit") Crane's φ-course is also within a unit and "Crane almost caught his flight" comes out true. The paper asserts the opposite without stating the unit. The same move recurs in the race, where §5.2 concedes the verdict rests on an unargued claim that beginning a kick is normal variation for one runner and not for the other.
- **The "under way" clause imports the indeterminacy it claims to explain.** §6.2 says Holmes's preparation/attempt line "is the account's line between preparing and doing," and that the proximity test's indeterminacy "is the indeterminacy of 'almost,' which is graded." But the account's gradedness lives in the distance; the under-way requirement is binary and has no criterion. Driving to the airport is "doing" catching the flight (§4.1); driving around looking for the clerk is not "doing" the robbery (§6.2); nothing sorts these except the verdicts wanted. §5.1 then rescues Everest (d)–(g) as loose uses, felicitous "as the summit is the natural continuation of setting out," which is an inertia clause of exactly the McKenzie–Newkirk kind the paper rejects, and a dispositional consideration the paper elsewhere excludes.
- **The headline empirical claim overreaches, and a cited datum contradicts the account.** The abstract says the two relations "come apart at the lottery, in the way experiments on the two expressions have found." The experiment (Doan et al.) used scratch cards, and §4.2 concedes the eventive clause "has nothing to evaluate" there: impossible cards get "false outright," possible cards get no verdict, and the mid-scale ratings are explained by a story about imagination. "I do not say the account predicts those numbers" is honest; the abstract is not. Separately, §2.4 quotes Gerstenberg and Tenenbaum's subjects denying that a non-colliding ball "almost caused" the outcome "even when a small perturbation to [its] path would have been sufficient." The account has a close φ-course right there and so predicts the opposite; the paper cites this as data it fits and never returns to it.
- **§6 is largely assignment rather than argument.** Beyond the Sainsbury and regret points, the consequences are mappings ("safety uses the second; proximity the first; the penal lottery the second; substantial step neither"). No safety theorist is cited who states the condition with "almost," so the "three relations" charge in §6.1 has no named target, and §6.2 closes by saying the account "says only" that Duff and Yaffe disagree about "tried," a modest yield for the section the introduction bills as the consequence the author cares most about.

The idea is worth a paper. This is not yet that paper: the metric that distinguishes it from the models it credits has to be stated for states as well as inputs, and the cases have to be re-run against the stated metric rather than against the verdicts the metric was chosen to produce.
