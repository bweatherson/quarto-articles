# Screening report: "Almost"

**Paper title:** Almost
**Word count:** c. 6,500 (excluding references)
**Thesis summary:** "Almost φ" is true iff a directed path toward φ stopped short of it by a small margin. For eventive prejacents the path is an actual process whose propensity for φ rose as it unfolded and whose completion at the interruption point would have needed only a perturbation small relative to the process's own normal variation; for scalar and stative prejacents the path is the canonical traversal of a scale and the margin is a contextual unit. The payoff is that "almost" (propensity-closeness) is a different notion from "could easily have" (disposition-closeness): safety epistemology needs the latter, the modal account of luck the former, and the law of attempts and the fittingness of near-miss regret answer to "almost."

**Decision: DESK REJECT**

## Deal-Breakers

**Criterion 4 (unanswered objections the author could have seen).** The paper's core notion, "propensity of φ given the state of the process," is defined as "a feature of the process, not of anyone's expectations" (Statement), but the crucial lottery verdict is then derived from "the propensity of winning given the *revealed* state" (Observations, Static closeness). Those are different quantities, and the headline consequence turns on which one is meant: on the objective reading, a sequential ball draw whose result is printed on a scratch card is a near-miss when the last ball is off, contradicting the Doan et al. data the paper relies on; on the epistemic reading, the paper's own principle that expectation-changing manipulations are pragmatic (Objections, Expectation) is violated by its own flagship semantic prediction. The paper contains both readings and never notices.

**Criterion 4 again, on the flagship application.** "Luck wants 'almost'" gets the paradigm case of the modal account of luck wrong: the lottery winner. On the paper's own reasoning about the loser (nothing in the drawing approached any particular number), nothing in the drawing approached the winner's number either, so on propensity-closeness the win occurs in every history-matching nearby world and is not lucky. Pritchard's account is explicitly built to call the winner lucky and handles the tethered climber by holding "relevant initial conditions" fixed, which the paper does not mention. The "two notions" result is stated in a form that is false, and the true weaker form (almost ≠ could-easily; safety needs could-easily) is already in Doan et al. and in the safety-and-chance literature the paper does not cite (Hawthorne and Lasonen-Aarnio 2009; Williamson 2009; Sosa 1999; Pritchard 2014).

**Criterion 1 (precision of the positive contribution).** The author says the account's two ideas "are not new" and that the contribution is stating them "precisely enough to be tested." The statement is not precise enough: "process," "state," "dynamics" and "normal variation" are left at the level of "in the sense in which a physics engine assigns it," and the verdicts on the stumbling jumper, the loaded die, the scratch card and the flatly refused Everest application are each secured by individuating the process or its normal variation after the fact (see Notes, item 4).

## Key Strengths

- The question is broad and the paper says why in its first paragraph: relief, guilt, the law of attempts, and safety all trade on what nearly happened. A generalist reader would want to know the answer.
- The empirical base (Kahneman and Varey; Gerstenberg and Tenenbaum; Covey and Zhang; Doan et al.) is well chosen, well summarized, and genuinely constrains the semantics. The two-jumpers and wall cases are a real problem for both standard pictures, and the paper is right that no distance-on-a-scale measure handles them.
- The attempts application is the best thing in the paper: the observation that Holmes's proximity test was a test of "almost," that the Model Penal Code's substantial-step test changed the subject from propensity to commitment, and that the impossible attempt is where the two come apart most sharply, is illuminating and, as far as I can see, correct.
- The Crane/Tees treatment gives near-miss regret an object in the history rather than in the imagination; that is a real payoff for moral psychology if the account holds.

## Key Weaknesses

- The account is not, as advertised, a rejection of "a space of alternatives." Propensity and normal variation are both modal-probabilistic notions; what the paper has is a Lewis-1979-style nearby-worlds account (match history up to the interruption, small departure, lawful evolution) with a metric relativized to the process. The paper concedes as much in the counterfactuals paragraph, which undercuts the framing of the introduction and of "Two pictures."
- Condition (i), "the propensity rose as the process unfolded," is refuted by the wire-to-wire leader caught on the line, who almost won though her propensity peaked at the start and fell throughout. The paper needs "was high near the end," but then the loaded die that simply shows two (propensity 0.8 at the start) becomes harder to exclude. This is Kahneman and Varey's propensity/disposition problem, which their epistemic construal was designed to solve and which the paper's objective construal reopens.
- The two-source story about direction is stated inconsistently: the Statement says the actual process fixes direction only when the scale has no canonical direction; the Observations say reversibility holds "exactly when the prejacent is a point rather than a scale endpoint." The paper's own "almost ten" example (a point, fixed) and "almost three o'clock" (a point on a scale that plainly has a canonical direction, reversible) contradict both formulations.
- The literature on the flagship application is thin: two Pritchard items and Sainsbury for safety and luck, with nothing on safety and objective chance, nothing on Lackey's or Pritchard's later refinements, and Sosa uncited. For the decomposition section, the strongest evidence for lexical decomposition is "again" (von Stechow 1996), not "almost," and it is not mentioned.

## Critical Issues for Revision

1. Fix the reading of "propensity" and rebuild the lottery discussion around draw order, not reveal order. Say explicitly what the account predicts for a sequential televised draw with the last ball off, and reconcile that with the Expectation section's semantic/pragmatic principle.
2. Restate the "two notions" result in its defensible form: almost and could-easily come apart; safety needs could-easily; there are two kinds of luck-talk (Teigen's close-call luck vs Pritchard's chancy-benefit luck) and only the first is "almost." Engage Pritchard's initial-conditions clause and the safety-and-chance literature.
3. Give a principled individuation of "process" and "normal variation" (e.g., the token course of events under the description the prejacent's verb selects, with the noise model fixed by that description), and show it does not need to be re-chosen for the stumbler, the die, and the Everest cases.
4. Replace "rose" in (i) with a condition that handles the fading leader, and check that the loaded die still fails.
5. State the direction rule once: default to canonical traversal; a salient actual process running the other way can override for interior points and never for endpoints. Drop the claim that time has no canonical direction.

# Notes for the author

## 1. The objection a good referee will press hardest

The account relocates near-misses "in the actual history," but its two conditions are stated in terms of propensity (a conditional probability) and normal variation (a distribution over perturbations). In a deterministic process, the propensity of φ given the full microstate is 0 or 1 at every moment, so "rose" and "high near its end" are meaningless unless the state is coarse-grained; and "normal variation" is a fact about a process *type* (what a jump, a roll, a taxi ride would do on repetition), not about the token history. So every verdict depends on a coarse-graining and a type under which the token is subsumed, and the paper fixes neither. Look at where the work is done: the stumbling jumper fails (ii) because his process is typed as "a stumble" rather than "a long-jump attempt" (whose normal variation includes not stumbling); the loaded die fails (i) because the roll is said to display "no propensity toward six," though by the paper's own definition its propensity at the start of the roll was 0.8; the scratch card fails (i) only if the drawing is typed as a single atomic event rather than a sequence of balls; Crane's propensity is stipulated to be zero "from the moment the jam began," though by the account's own lights it declined during the jam just as Tees's did. In each case the type is chosen to deliver the verdict. A referee will say that the account is therefore either (a) a nearby-worlds account with a history-matching metric, which is Lewis 1979 plus Kvart, and the anti-"space of alternatives" framing is advertising; or (b) an epistemic account in Kahneman and Varey's sense, in which case the objective/pragmatic line drawn in the Expectation section collapses.

Does the paper have the materials to answer? Partly. It can adopt (a) honestly: fix the coarse-graining by the noise model of the best predictive model of the process (Gerstenberg's physics engine, a race model), fix the type by the description the prejacent's verb selects ("qualify" selects a jump attempt, "show six" a roll, "win the lottery" the draw), and then present the account as a structured similarity metric rather than a rejection of similarity. That is a smaller thesis than the one announced, but it is a thesis, and it still yields the attempts and regret results. What it does not yield without more work is the scratch-card verdict, because the draw that produced a scratch card's number may have been sequential.

## 2. Problem paper or literature paper?

Problem paper in framing, literature paper in the body. The first paragraph ("What is it for something to almost happen?") and the consequences section are problem-driven and would exist if nobody had written on the word. But "Two pictures, and where they fail" is organized as a tour of Sadock/Morzycki vs Hitzeman/Penka/Amaral vs Nouwen vs McKenzie–Newkirk, and the whole Objections section is a reply to one 2020 paper ("At a distance"), with a second subsection whose only function is to explain away a Gerstenberg–Tenenbaum finding. The polar-component subsection is a reply to an objection nobody in the paper has raised. The passage "Its two ideas are not new ... What has not been done is to state this as the meaning of the word precisely enough to be tested" is the paper's honest statement of its contribution, and that contribution is one of precision and consequence, which is what the body fails to deliver. Reorganize around the five observations and the account, and treat the pictures as foils in one paragraph each.

## 3. Generalist appeal and the final section

The question earns generalist appeal; the final section earns it only in part. Ranking:

**First, attempts.** Proximity = "almost"; substantial step = commitment, a change of subject; penal-lottery arguments about outcomes track disposition, proximity tracks propensity; the impossible attempt is where they diverge. This is the section a philosopher of law and a generalist would both take something from. One claim needs support or removal: "a proximity test cannot convict [the pickpocket]." Courts under proximity regimes did convict on factual impossibility in empty-pocket cases; that is a legal-historical claim, not a consequence of your semantics, and as written it is asserted without a source.

**Second, regret and relief.** The fittingness point (Tees's regret has an object in the history that Crane's lacks) is good and the slot-machine contrast is well chosen. But the account as stated does not deliver Tees: neither man's propensity "rose" during the jam. This application survives only after condition (i) is repaired (see item 4).

**Third, the two notions of nearby possibility.** This is the most ambitious application and the one that is wrong as stated. Two problems.

(a) *The luck half.* Pritchard's modal account is explicitly probabilistic and similarity-based, and its paradigm is the lottery winner, who is lucky because in most nearby worlds with the same initial conditions she loses. On propensity-closeness the winner is not lucky: by your reasoning about the loser, the drawing approached no particular number, hers included, so every history-matching nearby world is one in which she wins. So "luck wants 'almost'" gives the wrong verdict on the case the account was built for. Your argument for the propensity reading is the tethered climber, but Pritchard's "relevant initial conditions" clause already holds the tether fixed; you do not mention the clause. The hedge "in this respect" does not save the result; it concedes that luck needs both notions, in which case the shared idiom is not hiding two relations but tracking one relation plus a context-sensitive initial-conditions parameter. What is true, and what you have the materials for (Teigen 1996, Pritchard and Smith 2004), is that "lucky escape" talk answers to "almost" and "lucky win" talk answers to "could easily." Say that instead.

(b) *The safety half.* The claim that safety needs disposition-closeness because the losing ticket did not almost win is correct but not new; it is why Hawthorne and Lasonen-Aarnio, Williamson (2009) and Pritchard already state safety in terms of chance or a wide class of worlds, and Sosa's original statement holds the basis fixed and varies the world. Your reading of Sainsbury as "propensity-closeness" that "does not handle the lottery" is fair, but "the distinction between the two notions is what a defender of safety needs in order to say both things at once" never says what the two things are. The paragraph on Lewis 1979 and Kvart says only that the similarity picture "is doing two jobs," which is name-dropping without a claim.

Verdict on the result as stated: incorrect. Almost ≠ could-easily is right and is in Doan et al.; safety-needs-could-easily is right and is old; luck-needs-almost is false.

## 4. Is the account stated precisely enough to evaluate? Test results

**Two jumpers (5.90 each).** Verdict correct, but secured by typing the second jumper's process as "a stumble." Type both as long-jump attempts and (ii) is satisfied for both. The account can still get the verdict from (i) (the stumbler's propensity collapsed at the board), so attribute it to (i), not (ii).

**The wall.** Correct, given Gerstenberg's impulse measure; both (i) and (ii) fail with the wall. This is the account's cleanest case.

**Teetering die vs plain two.** (ii) is trivially satisfied for both, because a die roll's "own normal variation" spans all six faces. Everything rests on (i), and (i) requires a coarse-graining at which "teetering on the six–two edge" is a state with propensity about 0.5 while "tumbling toward two" is not. The paper does not say what fixes that grain. The loaded die's 0.8 initial propensity also has to be excluded by hand ("the loading is a disposition"), which is the K&V distinction reintroduced without the epistemic construal that made it work for them.

**Scratch card vs sequential lottery.** Verdict depends on draw order, not reveal order, if propensity is objective; on reveal order if it is epistemic. The text uses both ("nothing in the drawing's state" vs "given the revealed state"). Under the objective reading, a sequential draw printed on a scratch card with the last ball off is a (weak) near-miss, contradicting Doan et al. Under the epistemic reading, the sequential-vs-scratch contrast is an expectation manipulation and by your own principle should be pragmatic, yet you present it as a semantic prediction. Also note that (i)'s "high near its end" is not met at 1/10, so even the sequential case is only "better," which is weaker than the intuition.

**"Almost every plant is dry."** Correct: count-of-dry-plants scale, endpoint "all," approached from below; moisture is not on the path. But the Statement's direction clause needs an endpoint rule you never state: "almost none," "almost empty," "almost all" are all approached from the interior, not by "canonical traversal" (there is no canonical traversal toward "none"). You use the endpoint rule in the Observations; put it in the Statement.

**"Almost exactly three."** The treatment (precision is a scale from coarse to fine; 2:58 and 3:02 sit at the same point) works only by making the scale |t − 3:00|, which is a symmetric static halo, i.e. an approximator. So "almost" applied to "exactly" behaves exactly as you say "almost" never does. That may be fine (the "exactly" does the work) but you should say it; as written it reads as ad hoc, and "coarse to fine" is not a direction 2:58 lies on.

**Glass being filled, "almost empty" at 10%.** Correct, and this is a good case for the endpoint rule. But then "almost three o'clock" reverses in the time machine although time has a canonical direction, and you say time "has no canonical direction," which is false; and "he is almost ten" of an eleven-year-old ageing backward would be sayable in the same way. The right rule is: default to canonical direction; a salient contrary process can override at interior points and not at endpoints. That is the reversibility prediction you want, and it is not the one in the Statement.

**Everest, close vote vs flat rejection.** The account says: same low (i), (ii) holds for the close vote and not the flat refusal, so a weak-but-genuine near-miss vs a poor one. Two problems. The intuition that the close-vote applicant "almost climbed Everest in a way the second did not" is weak; what is clearly true is that the first almost got funded. And "normal variation" of a funding process is undefined: a flat refusal this year and an acceptance next year are both within the variation of such processes. The verdict is secured by hand. Your argument that McKenzie and Newkirk cannot distinguish the two is fine; your claim that you can is not yet.

**Cases you did not run and should.** The wire-to-wire leader caught at the line (fails "rose"; obviously almost won). The patient whose vitals were stable until a sudden crash and resuscitation ("almost died," propensity rose late; passes). The person who "couldn't get into Nepal" because the border was legally closed (propensity zero; you say the impossible is never almost; McKenzie and Newkirk report felicity).

## 5. The agreement-data principle

It does not hold up as stated. (a) Manipulations rarely hold one thing fixed: the wall changes both the episode and expectations; the loading of the die is physically part of the episode (mass distribution affects the tumble) and by your principle should count semantically, yet you need it inert. (b) The shooter's reputation is, on your own definitions, part of the episode: skill is the dynamics of the shot and fixes its normal variation. Run (ii): a rim-out needs the same absolute perturbation for Jordan and for the bad shooter, but relative to Jordan's tight variation it is large and relative to the bad shooter's wide variation it is small. So your account predicts the bad shooter's is the better near-miss, which is what Gerstenberg and Tenenbaum found. You have a semantic explanation of the expectation effect and reach for a pragmatic one instead; but notice that if you take the semantic route, disposition-sensitivity has re-entered the account through "normal variation," which is in tension with the "modal of propensity, not disposition" claim in the consequences. (c) As a methodological principle for experimental semantics, it is stipulative: subjects see descriptions, so every manipulation is a manipulation of the description, and the principle needs an argument that "episode" descriptions and "expectation" descriptions are processed differently. The Jordan air-ball quote does not help; an air ball is not a near-miss on any account.

## 6. False, overstated or insufficiently supported

- Intro, para 2: Sadock 1981 is a pragmatic treatment (the polar component as implicature) in *Radical Pragmatics*; describing it as "adopting" a Lewisian nearby-worlds semantics is at best loose.
- Statement vs Observations: "propensity ... not of anyone's expectations" vs "propensity ... given the revealed state." Contradiction.
- Observations, Direction: "three o'clock and zero degrees name points on scales with no canonical direction." Time has one. And the reversibility claim ("exactly when the prejacent is a point rather than a scale endpoint") is contradicted by your own "almost ten."
- Observations, Static closeness: "the roll displayed no propensity toward six" for the loaded die contradicts your definition (0.8 at the start).
- "Two pictures": "Similarity is symmetric, so it has no direction." Direction on the scale is orthogonal to symmetry of the world-ordering, and Penka's own derivation of direction from the polar component under at-least numeral semantics is available to a similarity theorist too; the real problem for that picture is the non-entailment scales, which you say two sentences later.
- Three readings: "the account predicts that a predicate will have as many readings as its process has stages at which a propensity can peak and be cut off." Unfalsifiable as stated, and the decomposition case rests on "again" (von Stechow 1996), which you do not mention.
- Consequences: "The [safety] condition was built to deliver the verdict that you do not know your ticket will lose (Hawthorne 2004)." Safety was built (Sosa 1999, Williamson 2000) for Gettier and skepticism; the lottery is one application.
- Consequences: "worlds in which he is untethered are as similar as worlds in which lotteries go differently." Not on Pritchard's account, which holds initial conditions fixed; and the lottery-winner reductio in item 3.
- Attempts: "a proximity test cannot convict [the pickpocket]"; "most jurisdictions now do." Unsourced legal history; the first is doubtful.
- Regret: "Crane's process had a propensity of zero from the moment the jam began." False by your own definition; it declined during the jam.
- Regret: "Tees's ... propensity ... rose through the traffic jam as the delay held." It fell; the flight delay is a fact about the flight, not a rise in the propensity of his journey.

## 7. What to cut or tighten

- Cut the polar-component subsection; nobody in the paper raises the objection and the reply is two sentences.
- Cut or rebuild "Three readings of 'almost killed'"; as it stands it is a digression that ignores the "again" evidence for decomposition, and its "prediction" has no content.
- Fold "almost" and "approximately" into the Statement as one paragraph; the "almost exactly" datum should be presented as a case where "exactly" converts the scale into a halo, not as a canonical-direction scale.
- Cut the Lewis 1979/Elga/Bennett/Kvart paragraph or replace it with one sentence stating what the approach account says the similarity metric for near-miss counterfactuals is.
- Compress "Two pictures" to a paragraph per foil; the observations already do the argumentative work.
- Rewrite the safety/luck subsection around the defensible result (item 3) and add the missing literature: Sosa 1999, Pritchard 2014 ("The modal account of luck"), Lackey 2008, Hawthorne and Lasonen-Aarnio 2009, Williamson 2009, and on the linguistic side von Stechow 1996 and Ziegeler on "almost."
- Move the endpoint/override rule for direction into the Statement and delete the two inconsistent formulations.

The paper is close to being a good paper, and the attempts and regret material would survive the repairs. But the flagship result is false as stated, the account's central notion is equivocal in the paper's own text, and both are things a careful re-reading before submission would have caught. A revised version that addresses items 1 to 5 of the Critical Issues would be worth seeing.
