## 1. Verdict: (A) Do not write this paper for a generalist journal

The question is good, and the core thought is not false: the registration matters because of what it lets the operator know, and the operator's refusal discharges a duty of its own. But once the antecedents are subtracted, what remains is an application. The parts meant to make it more than that do not survive checking.

1. **The negative half is Husak's.** He also gives the structural point that consent is idle when the ground is non-paternalist (Craig/Jason, p. 118; p. 126). He even floats standing from the transaction itself (p. 125).
2. **The central positive move is Shiffrin's (*PPA* 2000).** The move is that refusing to supply, for the refuser's own reasons, is not paternalism and needs no authorisation. Shiffrin's own example is that it is "not paternalist to refuse to buy you cigarettes" [sec].
   - A May 2026 *ETMP* paper, Hanna, "Ulysses Contracts, Respect, and Motive-Based Anti-Paternalism", applies motive-based anti-paternalism to Ulysses contracts.
   - Its references include Franklin-Hall 2023, Shiffrin 2000, Cornell 2015, Time Out Ohio and the UK Gambling Commission's self-exclusion page. I could not read it.
3. **The positive structure and remedy are Amadio's.** Knowing exploitation of special disadvantage leads to restitution.
   - Its application to a self-excluded gambler was litigated and rejected in *Kakavas*.
   - The memo's way round *Kakavas* rests on a factual error (§3.1).
4. **The three distinguishing tests do not discriminate between the views (§3.3).**
5. **The exploitation premise faces a dilemma.**
   - On harm or vulnerability accounts, it imports the evaluative judgement it was brought in to avoid.
   - On Wertheimer's defective-consent route, it collapses into soft paternalism and the competence question. That repair would make the argument sound, but it removes what is new.
6. **The institution's defining rules fit binding better than notice.** These rules are: a term the registrant chooses; irrevocability within that term whatever the outside evidence; and release on her bare request plus 24 hours once the term ends.

The thesis is not wrong. But it is not a paper that can reach *Ethics* or *PPA* standard by 31 Oct. Retreat to topic selection.

## 3. Soundness

### 3.1 The memo's characterisations, checked against the texts

**Husak.** Accurate at pp. 117, 119, 121 and 124–6. Two corrections.
- **p. 122.** What Husak sets aside is the manager's *self-interest* as a point of view on the decision, alongside the law and the scheme's effectiveness. He does not set aside the moral relevance of the casino's gain. So "takes up both threads he set aside" overstates the case.
- **Husak already supplies the shape of the move.**
  - Consent is idle under a non-paternalist rationale (pp. 118, 126).
  - Standing can come from the encounter itself: "Perhaps Jones's mere appearance in a casino creates a special relationship" (p. 125).
  - His definition of paternalism is motive-based (p. 108). So a refusal *motivated* by the casino's own duty is simply not paternalism on his terms. He would not resist the "replacement".
  - What is new against Husak is only the *content* of the non-paternalist reason.

**Franklin-Hall.** Two of the memo's claims are wrong, and one omission matters.
- **p. 61.** A Ulysses contract authorises the deputy "to act in a way that interferes with or *limits* the principal's freedom … in some way that would otherwise be impermissible or inappropriate".
- **p. 62.** In the Russian Nobleman case, the wife "does not actively intervene … her refusal to revoke the deed" still enforces the contract.
- **So "his objection presupposes interference" is false.** The live question is whether the casino's refusal "would otherwise be … inappropriate". For a business open to the public that is arguable: *Uston v Resorts International* (N.J. 1982) limits a casino's common-law right to exclude [MEM].
- **p. 76.** The "arguably" concerns end-of-life refusals, not "bodily matters". The same page gives the deputy a significant-harm limit. That limit is principled, not ad hoc.
- **What the memo should use.** FH p. 62 explicitly sets aside the deputy's reasons "out of concern for others" (Bonnie and Clyde). That is the opening the thesis occupies, and the memo should present it that way.

**Walker, pp. 89–90.** Accurate, but Walker hedges: "it is not clear … It appears plausible that he was not".
- Real schemes contain exactly Walker's stipulation. GAMSTOP: "YOU WILL NOT BE ABLE TO DEACTIVATE". New Jersey's lifetime list: "your name cannot be removed".
- So Walker's consent-in-scenario model plausibly covers real self-exclusion, and on that model enforcement is non-paternalist. The thesis must beat Walker on real schemes, not merely deny "consent or paternalism".

**Long 2023, pp. 427 and 434–5, and Long 2025 fn 2.** Quoted accurately. But "refutes" Long overstates.
- 3.4.3(9)(e) lets an operator end *its own* relationship with a customer. Long's premise is about a mechanism for excluding non-registrants.
- Say "weakens". Then add that the premise was already false when published: 3.4.3 has been in force since Sept 2022, and the 888 penalty for customer-interaction failures dates from 2017.

**Kakavas.** The memo's reply ("readmitted after a 2005 psychologist's report") is wrong. The record [relay; verify pinpoints]:

On step 4, his assurances are testimony from *inside* the predicted state. So the thesis entails that Crown was on notice in 2005 and that *Kakavas* was wrongly decided. That is a bolder claim than the memo makes, and it runs against the Court's finding that he "had the capacity to self-exclude" [108] and could "refrain" [33], [135].

**Calvert.** Causation point verified (CA [8], [31]); decided on scope ([48]). CA [14] describes self-exclusion in Ulysses terms ("a moment of clarity … restraint").

### 3.2 §4.0, step by step

1. **"A seller is at liberty to decline."**
   - (a) This is relative to role and jurisdiction. It is doubtful for businesses that hold themselves out to the public (*Uston*). It is false for clinicians, which matters for the analgesia case.
   - (b) More importantly, it is irrelevant to whether the refusal is paternalist. On Shiffrin's, Quong's (2011, ch. 3) and Cornell's (2015) accounts, a refusal or omission is paternalist if it is done for paternalist reasons.
   - Step 1 blocks FH's demand for authorisation only if the refusal would not otherwise be "inappropriate".
2. **"Gain = loss."**
   - The expected money flow from buyer to seller is the same in every sale. Gambling is special for *her* only if the thing she buys (the gamble) has no value, or negative value, *to her*. That is a welfare judgement, and it is the first switch of standard (§3.4).
   - "Taking advantage of vulnerability" is the Wood/Goodin family of accounts. It is not Wertheimer's (§3.5).
3. **"Best placed" at t1.** This is contestable.
   - Registrations are often made in crisis, after heavy losses.
   - Husak p. 120: registration may be "rash or the product of external pressure"; Smith "need not have been an addict".
   - The report supports a belief; calling it "knowledge" overclaims. Its content is also time-indexed.
4. **The later request does not defeat the report.** This is the memo's best idea.
   - The request is predicted equally by "recovered" and "not recovered", so it is evidentially idle: its likelihood ratio is about 1.
   - But then the operator's warrant is the t1 report, *decayed* by base rates of natural recovery. The memo needs a decay schedule.
   - The step also allows *external* defeaters. GAMSTOP (within the minimum term) and New Jersey's lifetime list do not, so here the thesis is revisionary.
5. **"So the operator must refuse."** This is invalid as stated.
   - On fairness accounts, an anti-exploitation duty can be discharged by offering non-exploitative terms: loss limits, zero edge, monitored play.
   - Wertheimer's non-worseness discussion allows that mutually advantageous exploitation may be permissible.
   - So the thesis predicts a *disjunctive* duty (refuse, or serve on protective terms), not self-exclusion's categorical refusal.
   - *Kakavas* [161] [sec] also demands a "predatory state of mind", not mere knowledge.

### 3.3 The tests

**Test 1 (notice without registration).**
- This does not separate the thesis from paternalism: Husak's parity predicts the same duty.
- The regulation frames itself protectively. LCCP 3.4.1: "minimises the risk of customers experiencing harms". So the regulatory text is evidence for the protective reading, not for exploitation.
- It defeats Long. It does not support the exploitation ground.

**Test 2 (remedy).**
- **Validity.** "The wrong is a wrongful gain, so the remedy is gain-based" is a valid default (restitution for wrongs). But "*to the person exploited*" does not follow.
- **Moral hazard.** Where paying the victim would reward the conduct the rule deters, law sends disgorged gains elsewhere. A rule of refund-on-loss while keeping winnings gives the excluded gambler a free option.
- New Jersey's symmetric rule is the standard way to disgorge without creating that option. Its terms are: no winnings and no loss recovery; and the licensee's gains are forfeited to the State on wilful violation (5:12-71.3(c)–(d)).
- So "makes sense only if she broke her binding" is false.
- **The comparative claims also fail.**
  - On FH, the deputy's obligation is secured *to the principal* (p. 62), so the authority view predicts a remedy to her too.
  - Husak contemplates damages (p. 122).
  - Equity already rescinds.
- **What survives.** A gain-based remedy sidesteps Calvert's counterfactual-ruin problem (CA [8], [31]). That is real, but it is Bauer's point and equity's.

**Test 3a (analgesia).** The memo's explanation is incomplete and covertly evaluative.
- (i) "No duty to refuse" plus "no authority" yields only a *permission* to give the epidural. Practice imposes a *duty*: ACOG's "maternal request is a sufficient medical indication"; NICE 1.6.27, "support her decision". That duty comes from the clinician's role-duty to relieve pain, which is beneficence.
- "Payment for a benefit" is the same welfare judgement that separates the recreational from the compulsive gambler in objection 3. So yes, it is covertly evaluative.
- (ii) There is a simpler rival that is not evaluative: default claim-rights.
  - A capacitous patient's contemporaneous request is backed by a claim against her clinician. English law gives advance decisions effect only on incapacity, and only to refuse treatment (MCA 2005 ss. 24–25 [MEM]).
  - The gambler has no claim to be served.
  - That explains the divergence from the memo's own step 1, without any appeal to exploitation.
- (iii) There is also an epistemic rival. In labour she has acquaintance with the pain that her plan lacked (Paul 2014, *Transformative Experience*). So the pair does not isolate the mechanism.

**Test 3b (firearm do-not-sell lists).**
- The waiver framing is a legal artefact: the Second Amendment, and NICS denial needs a disqualifying legal status.
- Duties *not to supply* that are triggered by notice are well established without exploitation: negligent entrustment and dram-shop liability rest on facilitating harm. So notice-based duties outrun exploitation, which undercuts the choice of exploitation as *the* ground.

### 3.4 Where the memo switches standards

The memo announces the standard "what the operator may do given what it knows". It then uses "what is good for her" in four places:
1. step 2 ("loss");
2. §5.3 ("payment for entertainment");
3. §4.3 ("payment for a benefit");
4. §4.1 (a harm-minimisation duty cited as support).

The §5.1 reply slides from *loss of control* (agential) to *loss* (welfare). The only standard that stays epistemic-deontic throughout is: "do not transact on consent you have reason to think defective". That standard is the repair, and §4 explains why it costs the novelty.

### 3.5 Exploitation theory (brief item (a))

**The leading accounts.**
- **Wertheimer 1996.** The chapters are: 2 "Unconscionable Contracts" (pp. 37–76); 6 "Sexual Exploitation in Psychotherapy" (158–202); 7 "Unfair Transactions"; 8 "Consent" (247–77).
  - Unfairness is measured against a fair market price: "a hypothetical competitive market that excludes asymmetries of information and undue pressure" (SEP §2.2.2.1).
  - House terms identical for every patron are not unfair. *Kakavas* [38]: Crown had "no greater advantage over the appellant than … over any other gambler".
  - So Wertheimer delivers "standard terms + known impairment" only through **defective consent**. The transference analysis in ch. 6 is the closest model.
- **Wood 1995.** Exploitation is playing on another's weakness for one's own ends, and need not be unfair, so his account delivers the claim [MEM; verify his view on whether permitted exploitation is wrong].
- **Goodin 1987.** Exploitation is "playing for advantage in situations where it is inappropriate to do so", "regardless of the particular source of their vulnerability" (SEP). This delivers the claim, but its ground is the duty to *protect* the vulnerable (Goodin 1985). That is the protective rationale the thesis disowns.
- **Sample 2003.** Her account needs a baseline of needs or injustice, which is evaluative.
- **Mayer 2007.** Exploitation is wrongful gain from failing to benefit another as fairness requires. On this account, standard terms are not obviously exploitative.

**Applied work.** None found for self-exclusion or do-not-serve rules. Goodin's tobacco work (*Ethics* 99, 1989) [MEM] is a consent-defect argument for regulation, not knowing exploitation.

**Equity (item (b)).** Equity supplies nearly all of the positive account: knowledge, special disadvantage that need not involve unfair terms, and restitution. It lacks two things, and they are the contested part:
- an *ex ante* duty to refuse (*Kakavas* [26]: "no general duty upon a casino to protect gamblers from themselves");
- the claim that notice survives the gambler's assurances.

### 3.6 Fit with the institution

The LCCP and GAMSTOP share a structure:
- the registrant chooses the term;
- the exclusion is irrevocable within that term whatever the outside evidence;
- after the term she is released on "positive action" plus one day (GAMSTOP: 24 hours).

The default persistence differs by channel: 7 years for remote operators (3.5.4) but 6 months for premises-based ones (3.5.2). The memo omits the non-remote figure.

The expiry of a term she chose is a fact about *her will*, not about her condition. On step 4, a bare request after expiry should not lift the operator's knowledge. These are the signature features of a self-binding device. The notice view is therefore revisionary about the institution's core, and that defeats the title claim ("what makes self-exclusion work"). §4.4's split does not help, because irrevocability attaches to the refusal itself.

## 4. The strongest objection at *Ethics*

> "The author claims to explain mandatory refusal without authority and without paternalism. But the non-paternalist reason (dealing would be exploitation) contains the paternalist judgement. Calling the transaction exploitative rather than an ordinary sale to a willing adult requires one of two things. Either one judges that the gamble is bad *for her*, which is Long's value-partiality relocated from the refusal to the classification of the sale. Or one judges that her present consent is not really hers, which is the competence question the Ulysses literature is about. On judgement-based definitions of paternalism (Shiffrin 2000; Quong 2011; Cornell 2015), acting on that judgement is paternalist whether one interferes or merely refuses. So the 'refusal needs no authorisation' move does no work. What remains is Shiffrin's refusal-to-assist argument applied to casinos, plus Amadio. And the discriminating tests do not discriminate."

**Can it be answered?** Only partly. The best reply has two parts.
- **Shiffrin's distinction.** The operator's reason concerns its *own* participation, not her sphere.
- **The knowing-intoxication analogy.** One may not have sex with, or close a contract with, someone one knows to be too impaired to consent, even if they ask. Contracts of intoxicated persons are voidable where the other party knew [MEM; e.g. *Gore v Gibson* (1845)].

But that reply (i) is Shiffrin's; (ii) requires that her t2 consent *is* defective, so "competent and insistent" must go, and Feinberg's soft paternalism then also licenses refusal without authority; and (iii) may be exactly the debate Hanna 2026 is in. The objection can be answered only by conceding the novelty.

## 5. The best version, if BW proceeds anyway

I do not recommend this for 31 Oct. If written, target *Legal Theory* or *Law & Philosophy*, and only after reading Hanna 2026.

**Thesis (one sentence).** A self-exclusion registration matters because it puts the operator on notice that the registrant's later consent to gamble is defective, so that later dealing is the operator's own wrong. It follows that *Kakavas* was wrongly decided, that exclusion should end on outside evidence rather than on the gambler's say-so, and that the remedy is restitution. The fixed, irrevocable terms are a separate Ulysses layer that needs its own justification.

**Section plan (about 9,000 words).**
1. The puzzle, through *Kakavas*'s 2005 readmission.
2. Three accounts: authority (FH), consent in the scenario (Walker, applied to GAMSTOP's terms), and paternalism (Husak, Long).
3. What is already settled: Husak's parity, Shiffrin's refusal to assist.
4. The knowing-counterparty principle:
   - defective consent plus knowledge;
   - analogues: intoxicated contracting, sexual consent, *Amadio*;
   - why it is not paternalism, against Quong, Cornell and Hanna.
5. What a registration supports:
   - its content, and its indexing to time;
   - the later request is evidentially idle;
   - outside defeaters; decay.
6. Applications:
   - *Kakavas* (the main case);
   - *Calvert*: causation versus restitution;
   - LCCP 3.4.3;
   - New Jersey, with moral hazard faced honestly.
7. The remainder: the irrevocable terms as a Ulysses layer, assessed on FH's model.

**Lead cases:** *Kakavas* first, then Husak's Smith and Jones, then *Calvert*.

**Cut:**
- the analgesia minimal pair (at most a footnote with the claim-rights rival);
- the firearm lists;
- the "gain = loss" formula;
- the "New Jersey backwards" rhetoric;
- the generalist hook "you can't bind your future self".
