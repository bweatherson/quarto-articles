## 1. Verdicts

| Claim | Verdict | Source and exact claim |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | **PARTLY ANTICIPATED** (legal half) | The idea that the self-exclusion request gives the casino *knowledge* that grounds its own duty is in US student law-review work: Bauer 2006 says once a patron self-excludes, the casino is "aware that any gambling by the patron will bring harm upon him" (§II.A, c. pp. 79–82). But Bauer grounds the duty in the tort doctrines of *voluntary undertaking* and *special relationship*, not in anti-exploitation. He never asks whether the earlier self binds the later one. The English court in Gibson v TSE Malta [2024] also makes knowledge central (summary only, see §2). The philosophical half (no self-binding; an anti-exploitation duty does all the work; no t1 authority) was not found anywhere except in Long's stated-and-rejected paragraph (already known). |
| **P2** | **CLEAR** | No source applies Franklin-Hall's p. 70 objection, or any reply to it, to self-exclusion or refusal to supply. Two cautions follow (see §3). (i) Franklin-Hall's own text already says that involving someone "does not make it a Ulysses contract" unless they "aim to assist Ulysses in self-management" (p. 61, Frog and Toad). So the classification move is partly his. (ii) Real schemes are not pure refusal: they also involve removal and trespass. |
| **P3a** | **PARTLY ANTICIPATED** | Bauer 2006 argues casinos should be liable for the gambler's *losses* when the casino knows. He calls Indiana's jackpot forfeiture a one-sided "win-win" for casinos (§I.C, c. p. 70). This is the position without the disgorgement/exploitation rationale. The opposite view is also in print: recovery would be "unjustly enriching problem gamblers for their trespass" (Crowne-Mohammed & Harper 2010, p. 112). **Factual warning:** the scout's "Australian refund ruling" is not confirmed. Both NT Racing Commission decisions found *refused* refunds (see §2). |
| **P3b** | **CLEAR** | No source pairs gambling with labour analgesia, or explains any divergence by third-party gain. Burcher 2013 treats birth plans as Ulysses contracts and says clear choices in labour "should supersede decisions made before labour" (abstract). His ground is the woman's capacity and clarity, not the clinician's position. Roache 2008 notes that bookmakers' "profits suffer when their customers stop gambling", but uses this to argue against self-enforcement by operators, not to explain the divergence. |
| **P3c** | **PARTLY ANTICIPATED** | Selvaggio & Vars 2016 defend a removal hearing with the burden on the registrant. Their reason is epistemic: high-risk people "may have extended periods when they feel fine" and misjudge their risk (p. 674). That is the substance of the exit asymmetry, though framed as risk assessment, not as testimony defeating later testimony. Franklin-Hall p. 80 (waiting periods) is already known. Contrary positions in print: re-entry "can reasonably be seen as a rescission of the contract" (Crowne-Mohammed & Harper 2010, p. 116). And in Kakavas, later evidence of recovery defeated the earlier exclusion history (see §3). |

## 2. Closest prior works (new this round)

1. **Bauer, J.E., "Self-Exclusion and the Compulsive Gambler: The House Shouldn't Always Win", *N. Ill. U. L. Rev.* 27 (2006): 63ff [PART]. The closest new source.**
   - Argues a casino should "be held liable for the gambler's losses when the casino knows" (Introduction).
   - The duty comes from knowledge plus the casino's undertaking: "aware that any gambling by the patron will bring harm upon him" (§II.A).
   - Criticises forfeiture of winnings (§I.C).
   - No precommitment, Ulysses, paternalism or exploitation vocabulary.
   - Page numbers are approximate: the PDF is unpaginated, so these are section starts from the table of contents.
2. **Crowne-Mohammed, E.A. & Harper, M.A., "Rewarding Trespass & Other Enigmas: The Strange World of Self-Exclusion & Casino Liability", *UNLV Gaming L.J.* 1 (2010): 99–119 [PART].**
   - Self-exclusion "comes closer to bringing the problem gambler into the definition of neighbour" (p. 106).
   - Foreseeability may be met where the gambler is clinically identified (p. 110).
   - On their contract analysis, a self-excluder "must necessarily lack the capacity to form a binding agreement" (p. 115). Re-entry is rescission (p. 116). Recovery would be unjust enrichment (p. 112).
   - This is the main legal *opponent* of P1 and P3a.
3. **Brooks, G. & Sparrow, P., "Bookmakers and a Duty of Care: Customers' Views in England", *J. Gambling Studies* 32 (2016): 737–47 [PART; accepted MS read in part].**
   - "A bookmaker's liability then depends on specific knowledge of their customers" (MS p. 2).
   - In their survey, 94.4% strongly agreed that bookmakers owe "a duty of care not to exploit" problem gamblers.
   - Empirical, not normative. It gives the best bibliography of the legal duty-of-care literature (see §3).
4. **Gibson v TSE Malta LP (t/a Betfair) [2024] EWHC 2900 (Comm) [ABSTRACT: Cooley law-firm summary only].**
   - No general duty of care.
   - Knowledge was central: Betfair neither knew nor ought to have known, so it had not assumed responsibility.
   - Per the summary, the position might have differed had Gibson self-excluded.
   - Calvert was approved. **Read the judgment for the exact paragraph.**
5. **Selvaggio, A. & Vars, F.E., "'Bind Me More Tightly Still': Voluntary Restraint Against Gun Suicide", *Harv. J. on Legis.* 53 (2016): 671ff [PART].**
   - Answers search item 6: **Vars frames the list as self-binding and as a waiver of rights, not as information to sellers.**
   - "Ulysses understood that self-imposed restrictions may not be effective if too easily undone" (p. 672).
   - The sample form reads "I may be waiving some of my rights under the Second Amendment" (p. 684).
   - Three removal options: 7-day wait, a hearing, or permanent (pp. 673–4).
6. **Bigwood, R., "Kakavas v Crown Melbourne Ltd — Still Curbing Unconscionability", *MULR* 37 (2013): 463 [PART: first ~88% via summariser].**
   - Reports the High Court finding no "finding that he had lost the power to exclude himself" (fn 15). The gambler's *capacity to self-exclude* told against special disadvantage.
   - Reports the Court's contrast with an operator who would "prey[] upon a widowed pensioner" (Part II.A).
   - Bigwood is "quite comfortable with the result" (Part I).
7. **Burcher, P., "The Ulysses contract in obstetrics: a woman's choices before and during labour", *J. Med. Ethics* 39 (2013): 27–30 [ABSTRACT].** Discussed under P3b: the obstetric Ulysses paper, with no third-party analysis.
8. **Roache, R., "Reasonable steps to prevent gambling", *Practical Ethics* blog, 12 Mar 2008 [PART].** On Calvert. The bookmaker has an "obvious" conflict of interest. Not a citable rival, but the only philosophical comment on Calvert found.
9. **Slavina, I., "Don't Bet On It", *Chi.-Kent L. Rev.* 85 (2010): 369 [ABSTRACT].** Argues for no liability. "Casinos are not bargaining for their patrons to refrain from gambling", and exculpatory clauses should be enforced.
10. **NT Racing Commission decisions [PART].**
    - *Luxbet v S* (19 Feb 2016): the Commission "does not have the power" to order a refund (para 31). It found no evidence of the alleged 2011 self-exclusion (para 29).
    - *G v Sportsbetting* (27 Aug 2020): the self-exclusion form was delayed by Licensing NT; "no monies are payable" (para 21).
11. **Gaming Law Review exchange (Ontario) [ABSTRACT or title only]:**
    - Sasso & Kalajdzic 2006 (venues and province can be liable for problem gamblers' losses);
    - Cameron 2007, *GLRE* 11: 554–71 (reply);
    - Kalajdzic 2008, *GLR* 12: 55–9;
    - Mohammed/Crowne 2008, *GLRE* 12: 340–3;
    - Rhea 2005, *GLR* 9: 462–9;

## 3. What the paper must cite and answer

1. **The tort literature's rival mechanism: undertaking and assumption of responsibility.**
   - Calvert (duty arose on *acceptance*), Bauer (voluntary undertaking) and Gibson (assumption of responsibility) all ground the operator's duty in the operator's own *undertaking*, with knowledge as one input.
   - That is a third model, distinct from both Ulysses and testimony: a bilateral promise to the registrant.
   - P1 must explain why the anti-exploitation duty, not the undertaking, carries the weight. A good test: an operator that learns of a gambler's addiction from a third party, with no self-exclusion and no undertaking.
2. **The Kakavas point.** The High Court treated Kakavas's ability to self-exclude, together with later evidence of recovery (the 2005 psychologist's report [MEM: verify]), as defeating special disadvantage. This is the law reaching the opposite of P3c, and it is the hardest case for the exit asymmetry. Cite Bigwood fn 15.
3. **Removal and trespass.**
   - Slavina describes self-exclusion as a petition "to be physically removed".
   - Crowne-Mohammed & Harper treat re-entry as trespass, "criminally and civilly" (p. 111).
   - Removal and arrest are *interference*, which on Franklin-Hall's p. 61 needs authorisation. P2 holds only for the refusal-to-supply component. The paper must split the institution, or concede that the removal component is a Ulysses element.
4. **Vars's own framing.**
   - The firearm list's proposer explicitly calls it Ulysses self-binding and a rights *waiver*. Using it as a second case means arguing against its author.
   - A further problem: a gun dealer profits from the sale, not from the vulnerability. On P3b's own test ("does the third party gain from the vulnerability?"), the firearm case may fall on the epidural side. The paper must state the gain condition precisely enough to settle this.
5. **The remedy facts.**
   - Drop the "Australian refund" claim unless another decision is found.
   - Cite Bauer for refund-not-forfeiture, and Crowne-Mohammed & Harper for the opposite.
   - New Jersey forfeiture rulings exist (NJ DGE rulings, 2021–23 [ABSTRACT: search-result titles only]).
   - UK Gambling Commission divestment settlements [MEM] would be the best real-world support for disgorgement; verify them.
6. **Must cite:** Bauer 2006; Crowne-Mohammed & Harper 2010; Burcher 2013; Selvaggio & Vars 2016; Gibson v TSE Malta 2024; Bigwood 2013; Long 2023 (with "enlistees to enforce the Ulysses pact", p. 427); Franklin-Hall pp. 61, 70, 80.
