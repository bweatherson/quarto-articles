# Reading notes: Walker 2012; Davis 2004

## Walker, T. (2012). "Ulysses Contracts in Medicine." *Law and Philosophy* 31: 77–98. doi:10.1007/s10982-011-9116-z [READ: §§I–II in full, the casino passage, the safe-word passage]

PDF supplied by BW (texts/walker2012.pdf).

**Thesis.** A Ulysses contract in medicine contains two things:
1. consent to treatment in a specified scenario;
2. a stipulation that behaviour which would normally count as refusal in that scenario is not to be taken as refusal.

So a later competent refusal does *not* withdraw the consent, and treatment is not paternalistic (pp. 83, 88–91). The refusal is "embedded in the scenario" (p. 89). The analogy is consensual "forced" sex with an agreed safe word (pp. 88–89, 94–95). Walker argues that modified contracts with a safe-word-like exit should be legally enforceable (abstract; §III).

**The casino passage (pp. 89–90).** This is directly relevant.
- Walker takes up Husak's example: the gambler Smith "authorizes a lifetime self-exclusion program", later says "he has overcome the problems", and wants a modest wager (quoting Husak, p. 117).
- Walker's verdict: Smith "has withdrawn his consent to being excluded". So if the casino excludes him, "this will need to be on paternalistic grounds" (p. 90).

**Source to obtain.** Husak, D. (2009/2010). "Paternalism and Consent." In F. G. Miller & A. Wertheimer (eds.), *The Ethics of Consent* (OUP), pp. 107–130. The Smith case is at p. 117, and the point about Ulysses treatment and paternalism at p. 111.

**Relevance to T3.** Walker and Husak share an assumption: refusing Smith needs *either* his consent *or* a paternalist justification. T3 denies it. Declining to take someone's bet needs no justification from his consent. What needs explaining is why the operator *may not* take it, and that comes from the operator's duty once it knows. This is T3's central dialectical move, and **Husak's chapter is a must-read**.

## Davis, J. K. (2004). "Precedent Autonomy and Subsequent Consent." *Ethical Theory and Moral Practice* 7: 267–291 [PART: abstract, §§ on resolution preferences, skimmed]

PDF supplied by BW (texts/davis2004.pdf).

**Thesis.** Respecting autonomy can require respecting a *former* or *later* preference: the "resolution preference" about which of two conflicting preferences to satisfy (abstract; pp. ~277–279). Davis says it does not matter when the resolution preference exists. Applications: living wills (precedent autonomy) and subsequent consent. The acknowledgements say the paper was extended to Ulysses contracts at a reviewer's suggestion.

**Relevance.** Long 2023 targets the resolution-preference account, arguing that operators cannot tell which preference is authentic. Franklin-Hall targets the *2008* Davis paper, "How to Justify Enforcing a Ulysses Contract When Ulysses Is Competent to Refuse" (*KIEJ* 18: 87–106), and calls it "purely evidentiary" there (p. 70). For T3, Davis is a foil, not a precursor: he locates the authority in a preference *of the agent's*, and T3 denies that any preference of the agent's carries authority.

## Spellecy, R. (2003). "Reviving Ulysses Contracts." *Kennedy Institute of Ethics Journal* 13(4): 373–392 (Franklin-Hall cites the pages as 372–92). doi:10.1353/ken.2004.0010 [PART: abstract, the planning-theory section, the criterion, the five cases skimmed]
PDF supplied by BW (texts/spellecy2003.pdf). The extraction lacks page numbers, so cite Franklin-Hall's page (384) for the interview passage after checking it against the PDF.

**Thesis.** Enforcing some Ulysses contracts is justified as *weak* paternalism, not strong (abstract). The argument runs on Bratman's planning theory: a plan has inertia that desires lack.

**Criterion.** "If a patient wishes to break a Ulysses contract, her health care provider should interview her and ascertain whether she rationally has reconsidered her Ulysses contract qua plan." The test is "end-neutral". If she has rationally reconsidered and can "provide evidence of a sufficiently consistent change in desires and/or true beliefs, the contract should not be enforced."

**Relevance to T3.**
- Spellecy already makes the release question an *evidential* test, and one aimed at the later self's process.
- T3 differs in three ways:
  - T3 asks what the *third party* knows, not whether the agent reconsidered rationally;
  - T3 grounds the refusal in the third party's duty, not in weak paternalism;
  - T3 applies only to refusal-to-supply arrangements, not to interventions such as forced medication.
- Franklin-Hall (pp. 77–78) argues that rational reconsideration is neither necessary nor sufficient for a change of mind.
