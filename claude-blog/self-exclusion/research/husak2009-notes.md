# Reading note: Husak, "Paternalism and Consent" (2009) [READ in full, pp. 107–127 plus notes]

**Citation.** Husak, D. (2009). "Paternalism and Consent." In F. G. Miller & A. Wertheimer (eds.), *The Ethics of Consent: Theory and Practice*, ch. 5, pp. 107–130. Oxford University Press. doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195335149.003.0005.

Walker cites it as 2009 and OUP Online dates it 30 Oct 2009; check the print year with Crossref. PDF supplied by BW (texts/husak2010.pdf).

## Thesis (tentative)
- Consent, including explicit *prior* consent, is "irrelevant to the justification of paternalism" (pp. 113, 118, 123).
- Paternalism is justified when it is *reasonable*, judged by five criteria (pp. 108–111):
  1. minimal intrusion;
  2. a valuable objective;
  3. effective means;
  4. a subject in an unfavourable position to decide;
  5. an interferer standing in an appropriate relationship.

## Self-exclusion: the main case (pp. 117–124)
- **New Jersey's programme (p. 117).**
  - The minimum period is 1 year, 5 years or life, and "the exclusion is irrevocable throughout whatever period is elected".
  - "If participants in the program somehow gamble and win, their winnings are to be confiscated. If they lose, their losses are not to be returned."
  - **This is the remedy fact T3's prediction targets. Verify the current NJ rule.**
- **Smith.** He enrolled for life and years later says he has "overcome the problems". He wants a modest wager (pp. 117–118).
- **The crucial test is Smith versus Jones (p. 119).** Jones is identical to Smith but never enrolled. On Husak's thesis the manager should treat them identically.
- **Craig versus Jason (p. 118).** This is the harm-to-others analogue (a would-be molester at a playground). Craig's prior request "is not effective in authorizing what would be impermissible in its absence."
- **Not a contract (p. 121).** There is no consideration, since "it is unclear how the casino gains from the agreement". Nor can the contract model explain why the manager cannot release Smith.
- **Not deferring to either time (pp. 121–122).** He rejects Feinberg's privileging of the earlier self (p. 119), citing evidence that people mispredict their later preferences (p. 120). The manager also need not defer to the later demand (p. 122).
- **He brackets the casino's own interest (p. 122).** "I am not concerned with the self-interest of the casino manager". He notes "casinos make money by admitting patrons, not by excluding them". He also brackets the law and effectiveness.
- **The evidential role (p. 124), explicitly noted and then set aside.**
  - Consent "serves as evidence that some of my criteria are satisfied ... a reason to believe that Smith has a gambling problem".
  - But "I propose to put such epistemological considerations to one side. Suppose ... the casino manager happens to know just as much about Jones as he knows about Smith."
- **His misgiving (pp. 125–126).** Prior consent may create the "special relationship" required by criterion 5 between Smith and the casino. On this point he is agnostic.

## What this means for T3
**Anticipated by Husak:**
- The earlier self's consent has no authority. Smith and Jones are treated alike.
- The self-exclusion request plays an evidential role.
- Self-exclusion is not a contract (no consideration).

So T3's *negative* half is in print, in the very chapter Walker cites. **The paper must present itself as building on Husak, not as discovering the negative point.**

**Not in Husak, and where T3 is new:**
1. **The ground.** Husak treats exclusion as *paternalism* (interference "for his own good", justified by reasonableness). T3 says the operator's refusal is *not paternalism*. It is the discharge of the operator's own duty not to profit from a vulnerability it knows of, which is a non-paternalist, anti-exploitation rationale. Husak explicitly brackets the casino's gain (p. 122). T3 makes that gain the crux.
2. **Refusal versus interference.** Husak's clearest cases of paternalism "involve coercion, or an interference with liberty" (p. 112). A seller declining a bet is arguably not an interference. T3 can use Husak's own definition.
3. **Husak's parity of Smith and Jones becomes a prediction.** Once the operator knows, it should refuse Jones too. The UK Gambling Commission's customer-interaction duties (verify) require exactly that, so the duty exists without self-exclusion. This answers Long's objection about forced exclusion (2023, pp. 434–5).
4. **The remedy reversal.** New Jersey (as Husak reports it) confiscates winnings and keeps losses. On T3 this is backwards: the operator's wrong is its gain, so losses should be returned.
5. **The divergence.** In labour analgesia, the supplier's gain is not the patient's loss, so no anti-exploitation duty arises and the later request governs. On Husak's framework this would have to be settled case by case through reasonableness.
6. **Husak's criterion 5 (special relationship).** T3 dissolves it: the operator's standing comes from its own transaction with the gambler, not from a special relationship created by consent.

**Risk.** A referee may say: "Husak plus the unconscionability doctrine (Kakavas), applied." T3 must show that the anti-exploitation ground does work the paternalism framing cannot do. The remedy and the divergence are the candidates.
