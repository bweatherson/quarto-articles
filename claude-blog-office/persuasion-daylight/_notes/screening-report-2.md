# Screening report: Persuasion in Daylight

- **Title:** Persuasion in Daylight
- **Word count:** c. 10,000 main text + c. 600 appendix (excluding references); 78 references
- **Thesis:** The philosophy-of-science models of persuasion-by-true-results (Weatherall–O'Connor–Bruner, Holman–Bruner, etc.) assume "cursed" audiences that update on content but not on the fact of having been shown it. Uncursing the audience splits the strategy in two: *selection* (choosing which existing results to share) is neutralised by unravelling, while *design* (choosing which experiments exist) survives via Bayesian persuasion, shifting a fully rational receiver's decisions while leaving her beliefs calibrated. The wrong of design is not deception or manipulation but Wertheimerian exploitation; hence transparency reforms relocate rather than solve the problem, and the remedies are receiver authority over design or advance commitment to a demanding standard of proof.
- **Decision:** DESK REJECT

This is the closest kind of call. If it were overturned it would be EVALUATE, not SCREEN: the core is decision rules and information design, squarely in the editor-in-chief's areas.

## Deal-Breakers

**Criterion 1 (positive contribution).** By the paper's own repeated admission, the formal content is borrowed: "The economics is not new" (§1); "This is the 'Bayesian persuasion' result of @KamenicaGentzkow2011, with the numbers of their prosecutor example" (§5); "The formal point is not mine" (§8.2, on threshold commitment, credited to Henry–Ottaviani); and the "first contribution", the diagnosis that the philosophical models have cursed audiences, is something "their authors also note" (§1) and flag as future work. The selection/design split is the disclosure/information-design distinction economists already draw; the paper cites Fréchette et al. 2022 as stating "the general contrast". What remains as the paper's own is the normative reclassification in §6–7 (c. 3,000 words), and that is under-argued exactly where it bears weight (see Weaknesses 1–3).

**Criterion 4 (unanswered objection).** The paper's running example and its key empirical citation (CAST, @Echt1991) are cases where designed evidence killed patients. The model has no third parties and gives the regulator symmetric payoffs, so the only "loss" is the regulator's expected accuracy, which at the Bayesian threshold is exactly zero. "Mutually advantageous exploitation" is an artefact of that modelling choice, and the paper never asks whether the wrong in its motivating cases is a wrong to the receiver at all.

**Criterion 5 (length).** Roughly 4,000 words of §§3–5 and the appendix rehearse textbook results (Grossman–Milgrom unravelling, Kamenica–Gentzkow, Grünwald–Halpern) for a reader who is then told they are not the contribution.

## Key Strengths

- The structural explanation of *why* sophistication cures selection and not design is genuinely clarifying: at selection the sender's choice is a function of private information, at design it is a function of interests and a shared prior, "so there is no private information for the choice to leak and hence nothing for her to be cursed about" (§5). This is the paper's best paragraph.
- The pairing of Hart–Kremer–Perry 2017 (commitment adds nothing against disclosure) with Henry–Ottaviani 2019 (commitment recovers everything against design) yields a clean receiver-side taxonomy, scepticism for selection versus commitment for design, and the reframing of standards of proof as commitment devices against evidence designers is a nice idea.
- The arithmetic is correct throughout (I recomputed the table and the threshold curve) and the toy model is well chosen.
- Literature coverage across economics, philosophy of science and ethics is broad and, with the exceptions noted below, accurately used.

## Key Weaknesses

1. **The fairness baseline is asserted, then half-conceded.** The exploitation claim needs a standard of fair terms. §7's answer: "She is not, I think, entitled to it, any more than the sweatshop worker is entitled to a job at a fair wage. The claim is rather that *if* the sender is going to produce evidence ... the fair way to choose the experiment is by reference to the value of the decision ... and not by reference to the sender's share of it." That is the whole argument. The nonworseness reply two paragraphs earlier ("a sweatshop that pays a starvation wage does not become fair because the alternative was no job") presupposes the very baseline it is meant to defend. A careful author could see that the load-bearing premise of the original contribution is a one-sentence stipulation.

2. **The case against attitude accounts conflates wrong with harm.** §6.2 argues that Gorin/Klenk-style accounts "do not track the harm", since two sponsors with the same attitude leave regulators differently informed. But those are accounts of what manipulation *is*, not measures of how much it costs the target; a manipulation can be harmless. §7 then adopts Valdman's account precisely because its wrong "is not reducible to harm". The paper uses harm-tracking to dismiss one category and harm-independence to embrace another. The abstract's claim that those accounts "do so on grounds that do not track what the designer takes" is true and not an objection.

3. **The receiver is the wrong victim, and the paper knows it.** Vrousalis's account requires domination in a systematic relationship; a drug regulator holds coercive power over the sponsor, not the reverse. Valdman's requires that the target "cannot reasonably refuse"; at t = 0.5 refusing costs her nothing (accuracy 0.7 either way). The paper concedes that regulators already have both remedies and that the serious cases are "the public, of most patients, of most jurors" (§7), then analyses none of them. The category that fits the motivating cases is harm to third parties, which the paper's two-player model cannot represent.

4. **"Fully rational" is doing narrow work.** The headline is that "a fully informed, fully rational audience ... is still moved". But §8.2 shows that a receiver who reasons at the level of rules is barely moved, and admits the "discomfort" that the remedy asks her to be "less than perfectly evidence-responsive". The honest statement is that act-by-act Bayesian rationality does not protect the receiver and resolute rationality (@McClennen1990, which the paper cites) does. That is a much less striking thesis and it undercuts "reason is engaged rather than bypassed" as a contrast with manipulation.

5. **The overgeneration reply does not close.** Scientists choose "the experiment most likely to yield a publishable result"; readers of the literature have neither design authority nor a commitment device, so on the account they are *seriously* exploited, not mildly. The reference-letter case is dispatched with "discounting conventions", which is exactly the sophistication the paper has just argued is no cure for design.

## Critical Issues for Revision

1. Argue for, rather than stipulate, the single-decision-maker benchmark as the standard of fair terms, and do so without the sweatshop analogy, which assumes it.
2. Either drop the claim that attitude accounts of manipulation misclassify design, or replace the harm-tracking argument with one that engages the accounts on their own terms.
3. Put third parties in the model (or in the regulator's payoffs) and re-examine whether "mutually advantageous" survives; re-examine Vrousalis and Valdman against a receiver who has coercive power over the sender.
4. State the headline in terms of act-by-act versus rule-based rationality, and confront the fact that the second is available to any receiver who can commit.
5. Cut §§3–5 to what a philosophical reader needs (see (d) below).

## Checks a referee would run

### (a) Claims that look false or unsupported

- §6.1: "A cheap screening test ... adopted by a health service *for budgetary reasons* ... is not deceptive on Skyrms's definition, because no one profits." The health service profits; that is what "budgetary reasons" means. On Skyrms's definition the case is deceptive, which collapses the paper's contrast.
- §7: "Design is exploitation on all three accounts." Vrousalis requires a relationship of domination; Valdman requires inability to reasonably refuse. Neither holds for the regulator in the paper's own set-up (see Weakness 3).
- §5/§8.1: the reforms "are the commitment device that design requires". §5 itself shows (Felgenhauer–Loerke 2017, Dai–Fudenberg–Pei) that the sender already prefers public, committed experimentation, so he had a commitment device before the reforms. The reforms kill selection; they do not create design. "Relocates the problem" overstates.
- §5, first sentence of the table discussion: "Sophistication is a complete cure for selection." True only when the count is known, as §4 itself concedes via Dye, Jung–Kwon, Henry 2009 and Arieli–Stewart. The paper is internally consistent but the slogan is not.
- §5: "Design dominates selection for the sponsor, and the regulator is worse off under design than under selection-with-sophistication." The appendix admits these are different experiments and "the table is not a comparison of their informativeness". The sentence in the body reads as exactly that comparison.
- §8.2: "The commitment never binds on the path of play." Only for a sender with an unrestricted experiment set. With a bounded set (which §8.2 later invokes), off-threshold posteriors between 0.5 and t can arise and the commitment does bind.
- §8.1: "the ordinary tools for detecting distortion ... find nothing". An approval rate of 0.6 against a base rate of 0.3 is detectable in aggregate, and the paper says so in the next sentence. The claim should be that it is detectable only at the population level.
- §6.2: "@Cohen2018 argues that deception is a species of manipulation; if so, then design, which is not deception in his sense, is not thereby manipulation either." Non-deception does not imply non-manipulation if deception is a species; the sentence establishes nothing.
- §8.2: "That is a distinction the philosophical literature on scientific reform has not drawn." Unverified; a referee will check Bright, Heesen, Romero and Zollman on registered reports, and any philosophy-of-science uptake of Bayesian persuasion.

### (b) Citations whose use looks wrong or needs checking

- @DaiFudenbergPei2025 and @ArieliStewart2025: verify existence and that they say what is attributed (secret follow-up experiments never help; uncertainty about capacity lets disclosure mimic design).
- @PintoPinto2023: verify the lottery-funding proposal is theirs and is offered as a remedy for industrial selection specifically.
- @Kates2023: verify this is the nonworseness-claim reply intended.
- @Frechette2022: the key suggests one author; the Econometrica paper is Fréchette, Lizzeri and Perego. Check the bib entry and that "commitment reduces what is transmitted with verifiable information" is their stated finding rather than a gloss.
- @HartKremerPerry2017: the commitment-irrelevance result holds under specific conditions (sender prefers higher actions, truth-leaning equilibria). "A broad class of evidence games" should be checked against their actual scope.
- @Wood2014: the paper says this chapter "rules design out of manipulation on the bypassing account". Wood's definition is in terms of influencing through non-rational means; check that "bypassing" is his term and that his exploitation gloss is as quoted.
- @Fallis2015: "likely to create false beliefs ... that has the function of doing so". Fallis's definition is in terms of misleading information with a function; check the paraphrase.
- @HolmanBruner2017: the paper says they "introduce industrial selection" with the CAST case. Verify that the CAST example is in the 2017 paper rather than Holman & Bruner 2015.
- @Susser2019: three authors (Susser, Roessler, Nissenbaum); check the key and the quoted definition.
- @Lundh2017: "not explained by the standard risk-of-bias measures". The Cochrane finding is that industry studies had comparable or lower risk of bias yet more favourable conclusions; the paraphrase is fair but should be sourced to the specific comparison.

### (c) Objections a well-read reader would raise that the paper does not address

- **Ethicist:** exploitation requires that the exploiter's gain come *from* the exploitee. Approvals of non-working drugs are gains from patients and the public; the regulator loses nothing. "Appropriates the value of information" is a metaphor, since the receiver's surplus is zero before and after. The paper needs either a harm-to-third-parties account or an argument that appropriation of a benefit one was never owed is a wrong.
- **Ethicist:** a simpler rival account. The sponsor stands in a relationship (regulatory, or epistemic in Hardwig's sense) that generates a *duty* to produce informative evidence; the wrong is breach of that duty. Exploitation is a more elaborate category than the case needs, and the paper never considers the simpler one.
- **Formal epistemologist:** the "fully rational receiver" is myopically rational. A receiver whose payoffs weight false approval more heavily than false rejection, which is the realistic regulator, has a Bayesian-optimal threshold well above 0.5 with no time-inconsistency at all. The paper's "discomfort" about the remedy dissolves once payoffs are realistic, but so does the headline that rationality does not protect the receiver.
- **Formal epistemologist / economist:** competition in persuasion (Gentzkow & Kamenica 2017) shows that multiple senders with opposed interests tend to restore information transmission on the design side, as Milgrom–Roberts does on the selection side. The drug market has rival sponsors and independent researchers. The "single designer" framing that drives §8 is not argued for.
- **Philosopher of science:** the sender bears the whole cost of experimentation and the receiver contributes nothing. Wertheimer's mutually advantageous exploitation concerns a *cooperative* surplus. What is the receiver's contribution to the surplus here, such that she has a claim on a share of it?
- **Philosopher of science:** the paper conflates two things under "design": choosing among experiments of differing informativeness (the KG lever) and choosing the *question* (surrogate versus mortality endpoint). The second is not a choice of a garbling of the same state; it changes what the state is. The CAST case is the second kind and the KG model does not obviously cover it.
